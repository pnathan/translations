### Translation under review (19:16)

English: He lingered; then the men took hold of him by his hand, by his wife's hand, and by the hand of his two daughters, in the LORD's compassion for him; they brought him out and set him down outside the city.

Phrases:
- 19:16.1 | He lingered | 'and-lingered': wayyiqtol of the hitpalpel; the first act of the verse, the subject Lot.
- 19:16.2 19:16.3 | then the men took hold | 'and-took-hold the-men': the sequential verb after the lingering; the vav is rendered 'then'.
- 19:16.4 | by his hand | 'in-his-hand': the first of three hands.
- 19:16.5 19:16.6 | by his wife's hand | 'and-in-hand-of his-wife': the second hand.
- 19:16.7 19:16.8 19:16.9 | and by the hand of his two daughters | 'and-in-hand-of two-of his-daughters': the third hand, held by the two daughters.
- 19:16.10 19:16.11 19:16.12 | in the LORD's compassion for him | 'in-compassion-of the-LORD upon-him': the LORD's mercy is the ground of the rescue; the English states it as a reason.
- 19:16.13 19:16.14 | they brought him out and set him down | 'and-brought-him-out and-set-him-down': the two verbs of the second half.
- 19:16.15 19:16.16 | outside the city | 'from-outside to-the-city': the place where they set him down.

Grammar notes:
- The verbs are wayyiqtols, a chain of sequential past actions: he lingered (hitpalpel), they took hold (hifil), they brought him out (hifil), they set him down (hifil). The subject of the first verb is Lot (3ms); the later ones are the men (3mp).
- The Hebrew uses the singular yad three times for three hands: his, his wife's, and his two daughters'. Each is introduced by bet, so the English repeats 'by the hand' for each.
- The shalshelet on vayyitmahmah (19:16.1) is a rare accent of hesitation, which suits the lingering. The etnahta on alav (19:16.12) divides the verse between the holding and the leading out.
- The LORD's compassion is in a bet phrase: 'in the compassion of the LORD upon him'. The English 'in the LORD's compassion for him' keeps the sense that the rescue is by mercy, not by Lot's merit.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:19:16:1` follows: Ibn Ezra rejects the view that vayyitmahmah comes from mah, 'what': both heh's are sounded, the root is triliteral, and the word means 'linger, delay'. The English 'lingered' follows him.
- `ibn-ezra:19:16:2` follows: Ibn Ezra says the verb shows that Lot was afraid and had no strength to flee, so the men took hold of him. The English 'took hold of him' follows him.
- `ramban:19:16:1` records: Ramban quotes Ibn Ezra's reading of fear, but prefers that the men were pulling and urging Lot with force to hurry him out, like 'Egypt pressed (vatteḥezaq) the people' (Exodus 12:33). The English keeps Ibn Ezra's reading; Ramban's is recorded here.
- `ramban:19:16:2` follows: Ramban: Lot was saved not for his own merit but by the LORD's compassion, or the men hurried him out while the compassion was still on him, before anger could come out. The English's 'in the LORD's compassion' follows the first reading.
- `radak:19:16:1` records: Radak: Lot still lingered as though he begrudged his property; the men waited until dawn and let him take nothing, though they had said 'whatever is yours' (19:12). The English gives the delay without a motive, and Radak's reason is recorded.
- `rashi:19:16:1` records: Rashi gives the purpose of the delay as saving his property (kedei lehatsil et mamono). The English does not state a motive; Rashi's reason is recorded.
- `radak:19:16:3` records: Radak: the men hurried to bring Lot out for Abraham's sake, though the time of the overturning had come. This names Abraham as the cause; the English names the LORD's compassion.
- `sforno:19:16:1` follows: Sforno: the compassion was on Lot although he was delivered for Abraham's merit; his lingering was not rebellion or sin but sloth and a frightened spirit. The English follows the compassion; the reason for the delay goes in the commentary.
- apparatus: 19:16.2: the WLC spells the verb without the yod (vayyaḥazqu); the base text has vayyaḥaziqu. The same verb; no difference of sense.

### Translation under review (19:17)

English: As they brought them outside, he said, "Flee for your life; do not look back, and do not stop anywhere in the plain. Flee to the mountain, lest you be swept away."

Literal: and-it-came-about as-bringing-out them the-outside and-said he: escape for-your-life not look-back-at-you and-not stand in-all the-plain the-mountain escape lest be-swept-away

Phrases:
- 19:17.1 19:17.2 19:17.3 19:17.4 | As they brought them outside | 'and-it-came-about as-bringing-out them the-outside': a temporal clause with the ke-infinitive.
- 19:17.5 | he said | 'and-said': the singular verb; the English keeps the Hebrew subject.
- 19:17.6 19:17.7 19:17.8 | Flee for your life | 'escape for-your-life': the idiom, the speaker's first command.

Grammar notes:
- The clause 'as they brought them outside' is a temporal clause: wayehi with the ke-infinitive. The main verb is the next wayyiqtol, vayyomer, 'said'.
- The speech has four commands: two imperatives (himmalet, both nifal) and two prohibitions (al with the jussives tabbit and ta'amod). Each is followed by its reason or its object.
- Address is singular throughout: 'escape for your life', 'do not look back', 'your' (2ms). Ibn Ezra explains that the singular covers Lot with all that is his (commentary).
- The etnahta on hakikkar (19:17.15) divides the speech: the prohibitions in the plain come before it, the command to the mountain after it.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:19:17:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: kehotsi'am is an infinitive, and the ke does not mean 'like'. The angels are the doers, and 'them' are those brought out. The English 'As they brought them outside' follows him.
- `ibn-ezra:19:17:2` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'do not look back' is singular and speaks to Lot and all that is his, as the singular 'do not eat of it' covers a group. The English keeps the singular 'you' and reads the warning as covering the household.
- `ramban:19:17:1` records: Ramban gives the order as: do not stop anywhere in the plain, and do not look back once you are saved, since the disaster does not fall until Lot is in the mountain. The English keeps the order of the Hebrew clauses; Ramban's order is recorded.
- `radak:19:17:1` follows: Radak: while they were bringing Lot out, they were telling him to hurry and escape. The English 'As they brought them outside' keeps the clause simultaneous with the speech.
- `radak:19:17:2` follows: Radak: the speaker is the angel who came to save Lot, though the two were as one. The English 'he' keeps the singular subject; Radak's identification is recorded here, not in the English.
- `radak:19:17:3` follows: Radak: 'do not look back' was said so that Lot would not delay by turning to see whether the city had been overturned. The English follows this plain purpose.
- `radak:19:17:4` follows: Radak: all the cities of the plain were overturned, so the kikkar is the whole district. The English 'anywhere in the plain' follows him.
- `radak:19:17:5` follows: Radak: 'lest you be swept away' means that if Lot stays with the cities, he cannot be saved once the overturning begins, for it comes in an instant. The English follows this sense.
- `rashi:19:17:1` not-applicable: Rashi: it is enough that you save lives; do not spare your property. This is a homily on the verb, not the plain sense.
- `rashi:19:17:2` not-applicable: Rashi: Lot was condemned along with the others and saved for Abraham's merit, and he is not fit to look at their punishment. A homily on the prohibition; not the plain sense.
- `rashi:19:17:3` follows: Rashi identifies the kikkar as the plain of the Jordan (kikkar ha-Yarden). The English keeps 'the plain' and the context note records the place.

### Translation under review (19:18)

English: Then Lot said to them, "No, please, my lords."

Literal: and-said Lot to-them: no please my-lords

| token | gloss | lex | sense | note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 19:18.1 | and said |  | Qal 1 | Wayyiqtol 3ms of amar: the new speech of Lot. |
| 19:18.2 | Lot |  |  | Proper name (decisions: the English form). |
| 19:18.3 | to them |  | 1 | El with the 3mp suffix. The plural 'them' shows Lot addresses both men. The etnahta on this word divides the verse. |
| 19:18.4 | no |  |  | Al as a negation, 'no, not so' (Ibn Ezra, commentary), not the verb hoel 'be willing'. |
| 19:18.5 | please |  |  | Particle of entreaty (na): it softens the refusal. |
| 19:18.6 | my lords |  |  | Adonai with the 1cs suffix. The qamats is the pausal form at the end of the verse; the word is profane here (Ibn Ezra, Radak), so 'my lords' for the two men, not the divine name. Rashi reads it as sacred (commentary). |

Phrases:
- 19:18.1 19:18.2 19:18.3 | Then Lot said to them | 'and-said Lot to-them': the speech begins.
- 19:18.4 19:18.5 19:18.6 | No, please, my lords | 'no please my-lords': the refusal and the entreaty, addressed to the two men.

Grammar notes:
- The etnahta on the word 'to them' (19:18.3) divides the verse: Lot's speech follows it.
- The reply begins with al, the negation, and na, the entreaty: 'no, please'. It answers the command of 19:17 to flee; the content is understood from there.
- The final adonai has the qamats of a pausal form, which is why its reading is disputed: profane 'my lords' for the men (Ibn Ezra, Radak), or the sacred name (Rashi, commentary).

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:19:18:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: adonai here is profane (hol), not the divine name; its qamats is due to the pause at the verse's end. He takes al na as 'no' (lo), not as the entreaty of hoel that Samuel the Nagid proposed. When told 'flee to the mountain', Lot answers 'Not so, my lords'. The English follows Ibn Ezra.
- `rashi:19:18:1` records: Rashi, following the Talmud (Shevuot 35), takes adonai here as the sacred name, the one with power to kill and to give life, and renders its Targum as 'please, Lord'. The English 'my lords' follows Ibn Ezra; Rashi's sacred reading is recorded.
- `rashi:19:18:2` follows: Rashi: 'al na means 'do not say to me to flee to the mountain'. He reads the al as a refusal, as Ibn Ezra does.
- `rashi:19:18:3` follows: Rashi: na is a word of request. The English 'please' follows.
- `radak:19:18:1` follows: Radak: 'please, do not make us bring you up the mountain, for I cannot hasten my steps so much'. This agrees with the refusal and the entreaty of the English.
- `radak:19:18:2` follows: Radak: adonai is profane and pointed with qamats because of the pause. He finds the Sages' view that Lot's adonai here is sacred puzzling. The English 'my lords' follows Radak.
- `radak:19:18:3` follows: Radak: 'to them' is plural; an angel, sent by God, also has power to kill and to give life. This explains the plural address 'my lords'.
- `ibn-ezra:19:19:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: Lot says 'your servant' (singular) to one of them because he regards that one as great, since angels too have princes. The English keeps the singular. He also explains vamatti: the tav of mut is absorbed into the tav of the first-person ending, as in otho tashhit (Deuteronomy 20:20).

Grammar notes:
- The speech moves from entreaty (hinneh na, 'look, please') to a qatal, 'has found', and then to a wayyiqtol, 'and you made great'. The wayyiqtol after the qatal continues the same past.
- The address is singular throughout: 'your servant', 'your eyes', 'you did', 'you made great'. Lot speaks to one of the two angels, the greater (Ibn Ezra, Radak; commentary).
- The etnahta on nafshi (19:19.14) divides the speech: the thanks come before it, the plea after it. The vav with the pronoun in 'but I' marks the turn.
- After pen the imperfect (tidbaqeni) gives the feared event and the qatal with vav (wamatti) states its result: 'lest ... and I die'.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:19:19:1` not-applicable: Rashi: 'lest disaster overtake me': while Lot was among the people of Sodom, God saw him as a righteous man; once he came to Abraham, he was judged as wicked. A homily; not the plain sense.
- `ramban:19:17:2` not-applicable: Ramban, on Lot's words 'I cannot escape to the mountain' (19:19, filed here; the pack's text breaks off), takes the prohibition of looking as a natural warning: looking at the plague and at contagion harms, as a bitten animal's sight does. This is not the plain sense.
- apparatus: Ramban's comment on Lot's words 'I cannot escape to the mountain' (19:19) is filed at 19:17 and breaks off in the pack; only the part the pack prints is summarized here.

Grammar notes:
- The speech has two particles of entreaty, na in the opening hinneh na and na in 'let me escape, please': the first is the plea, the second the request.
- The question 'is it not small?' (ha-lo, with mitz'ar at 19:20.14) expects the answer yes. The repeated mitz'ar is the argument: a small city should be spared.
- The etnahta on mitz'ar (19:20.9) divides the speech: the city's nearness and smallness come first, the request after.
- The closing wish is a yiqtol 3fs of hayah, agreeing with the feminine nefesh, 'my life'. It is a wish, not a plain statement.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:19:20:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: mitz'ar means 'small', an adjective from tsa'ir ('little'), and it comes without the feminine ending, as shegal does. The English 'it is small' follows him.
- `rashi:19:20:1` not-applicable: Rashi: 'near' is a chronology of the settlement, which was founded shortly after the dispersion. A midrashic chronology; not the plain sense.
- `rashi:19:20:2` records: Rashi: 'is it not small?' means 'are not its sins few? you can spare it'. He reads smallness as few sins; the English reads it as size, following Ibn Ezra and Radak. Rashi's reading is recorded.
- `rashi:19:20:3` records: Rashi's plain sense of the clause: 'and my life will live in it'. The city is small with few people, so Lot need not mind leaving it, and his life will live there. The English 'my life will be spared' does not place him in the city.
- `radak:19:20:1` follows: Radak: 'near to flee there' means near enough to flee to. The English follows him.
- `radak:19:20:2` follows: Radak: the city is small with few people in it, though they are wicked; for Lot's sake God saved them from the overturning. The English follows the 'small' reading.
- `radak:19:20:3` records: Radak takes 'and my life will live' to mean 'with them', that is, with his people or household. The English keeps 'my life' alone; Radak's reading is recorded.
- `radak:19:20:5` follows: Radak: Lot repeats 'is it not small?' as one who pleads, recalling the city's littleness. The English keeps the question.
- apparatus: 19:20.8: the WLC reads vehi (וְהִ֣יא); the base text has vehu (וְהִ֣וא). No difference of sense.

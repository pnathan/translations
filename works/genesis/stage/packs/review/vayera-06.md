Grammar notes:
- הִנֵּה and נָא open Lot's speech to the men of Sodom: an interjection and an entreaty, 'look' and 'please'. The whole verse is direct speech, so it is set in double quotation marks.
- יָדְעוּ is a qatal, 3rd plural, with the daughters as subject, and the negative לֹא belongs to it. The verb has the lexicon sense 'know a person', the biblical euphemism for sexual relations.
- אוֹצִיאָה is a cohortative (hifil, 1st singular, 'let me bring out'); the particle נָא comes after it, so the request is softened twice.
- כִּי עַל כֵּן ('for this reason') joins the offer to its cause: the men came in under the roof, and so they are not to be harmed. The qatal בָּאוּ gives that cause.
- The pashta (disjunctive) on יָדְעוּ marks the end of the first clause, and the silluq on קֹרָתִי ends the verse.

Translator's note: Lot's offer to the crowd is the plain sense of the verse: his daughters in place of his guests. The English keeps the offer as Lot makes it and does not comment on it; the commentators (Ramban, Sforno) discuss his motive.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:19:8:1` follows: Ibn Ezra says יָדְעוּ is the plural of the past tense used of masculine and feminine alike, with no added vav and the same vowel in both. The English takes the verb with the two daughters as its subject.
- `ibn-ezra:19:8:2` follows: Ibn Ezra reads הָאֵל as הָאֵלֶּה, 'these'. He calls the explanation he attributes to the Gaon (that the form lacks its third letter) remote, since both forms are found in Aramaic.
- `rashi:19:8:1` follows: Rashi, like Ibn Ezra, reads הָאֵל as כְּמוֹ הָאֵלֶּה, 'these'.
- `rashi:19:8:2` records: Rashi paraphrases the clause as 'you will do this good for my honour, because they came under the shelter of my roof'. The English keeps the causal link and does not add the honour.
- apparatus: הָאֵל (19:8.20) is read as 'these' by Ibn Ezra and Rashi; the form is unusual, and Ibn Ezra records the view that a letter is missing from it.

- The list runs son-in-law, sons, daughters, then all that is his in the city. The order names the household first and the goods last.
- הוֹצֵא is a hifil imperative, 2nd masculine singular, addressed to Lot; the object is supplied from the list.

Translator's note: The English reads the condition (Ibn Ezra) and the sons-in-law (Ibn Ezra). Rashi and Radak read 'your sons' as the sons of the married daughters, since Lot had no sons; that reading is recorded.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:19:12:1` follows: Ibn Ezra paraphrases the phrase as 'if you have a son-in-law', so he takes the question as a condition. The English 'If you have anyone else here' keeps that reading.
- `ibn-ezra:19:12:2` follows: Ibn Ezra says ובניך means sons-in-law who are like sons. He argues against the view that one angel alone went out to save Lot: both speak in verse 13, and the one who speaks afterwards says 'I cannot do anything' (verse 22).
- `rashi:19:12:3` records: Rashi takes ובניך as the sons of the married daughters, and Radak says the same since Lot had no sons. The English keeps 'your sons-in-law' from Ibn Ezra and records this reading.
- apparatus: ובניך (19:12.10) can mean 'your sons' or 'your sons-in-law'; the English follows Ibn Ezra and the literal line keeps 'sons'.

Grammar notes:
- וּכְמוֹ הַשַּׁחַר עָלָה is a clause of time: 'and as the dawn rose'. Ibn Ezra defends the reading against R. Jonah's objection.
- The wayyiqtol וַיָּאִיצוּ, with the subject the angels, introduces the speech with the infinitive לֵאמֹר, 'saying'.
- קוּם and קַח are two imperatives; the three objects (wife and daughters) are the people to be taken out.
- The participle הַנִּמְצָאֹת is nifal, feminine plural, agreeing with 'your daughters': 'who are here'.
- The verse ends with the silluq on הָעִיר; the lest-clause gives the danger.

Translator's note: The angels are called 'the men' in verses 10-12 and 'the angels' here; the verse marks the change. The English follows the Hebrew noun and keeps 'urged' for the verb.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:19:15:1` follows: Ibn Ezra quotes R. Jonah the grammarian, who held that 'and as the dawn rose' cannot be said, and says that Jonah forgot 'they multiplied as they multiplied' (Zechariah 10:8), where the same construction occurs. The English reads the clause as 'as the dawn rose'.
- `ibn-ezra:19:15:2` follows: Ibn Ezra glosses וַיָּאִיצוּ as ויציקו, 'they pressed', comparing the word in Exodus 5:13, where the taskmasters press the people. The English 'urged' keeps that pressure.
- `radak:19:15:3` follows: Radak takes הַנִּמְצָאֹת as 'those found with you in the house'. The English 'who are here' gives the same sense.
- `rashi:19:15:2` records: Rashi reads הַנִּמְצָאֹת as 'those prepared for you in the house, to save them'. The English keeps 'who are here' and records his reading.

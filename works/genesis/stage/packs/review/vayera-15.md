### Translation under review (21:22)

English: At that time Abimelech and Phichol, the commander of his army, said to Abraham, "God is with you in all that you do."

Grammar notes:
- The verse opens a scene. The wayyiqtol וַיְהִי (21:22.1) is a narrative formula, and the wayyiqtol וַיֹּאמֶר (21:22.4) carries the story on; its subject follows the verb, as usual in this construction.
- אֱלֹהִים עִמְּךָ בְּכֹל אֲשֶׁר־אַתָּה עֹשֶׂה is a verbless clause. 'Is' in the English is the copula the Hebrew does not express.
- לֵאמֹר (21:22.11) is the infinitive construct that introduces direct speech. Its etnaḥta divides the verse before the speech.
- The participle עֹשֶׂה (21:22.17) in the relative clause gives a continuing practice: 'in all that you do'.

Translator's note: The copula 'is' is the only word added to the verbless clause. The divine name takes the sense 'God' (4); the note on 21:22.12 gives the reason.

Commentary rulings:
- `radak:21:22:1` records: Radak dates the time to when Isaac was born or weaned, while Abraham was still in Gerar in the land of the Philistines. The English keeps 'that time' and does not fix a date.
- `rashi:21:22:1` not-applicable: Rashi gives the reason for the speech: the Philistines saw that Abraham had come out of Sodom's neighbourhood in peace, had fought the kings and won, and had a son in his old age. That is a motive, not a reading of the words.
- `radak:21:22:2` not-applicable: Radak explains why the verse says 'said' and not 'went to': Abimelech wanted Abraham as an ally after he saw Abraham's success. A reason for the speech, not a reading of its words.
- `radak:21:22:3` not-applicable: Radak says Phichol's counsel lay behind Abimelech's words. A motive, not a reading of the words.
- `onkelos:21:22:1` declines: Onkelos renders the clause as 'the Memra of the LORD is at your side', a targumic rendering of the divine name. The English keeps 'God' for אֱלֹהִים, as the decisions set.
- `sforno:21:22:1` not-applicable: Sforno gives Abimelech's motive: he fears Abraham's standing with God, not his might, and so asks for an oath. A reason for the speech, not a reading of its words.

Grammar notes:
- The oath opens with the Niphal imperative הִשָּׁבְעָה and the conditional אִם, and the conditional has no apodosis. The curse that would complete it is left unstated, as Ramban explains; the English keeps the clause open.
- The verb תִּשְׁקֹר (21:23.7) is a yiqtol in a conditional clause. Ibn Ezra notes that it is in the light binyan, Qal, and the English reads it as Qal.
- The verb takes three objects joined by ו: 'me', 'my offspring', 'my grandson'. The preposition ל is repeated before each, so the English repeats 'with'.
- כַּחֶסֶד אֲשֶׁר־עָשִׂיתִי עִמְּךָ (21:23.11–14) is a comparison, 'as the kindness I did with you'. The main verb that follows, תַּעֲשֶׂה עִמָּדִי (21:23.15–16), is a yiqtol: 'you will deal with me'.
- The etnaḥta on וּלְנֶכְדִּי (21:23.10) closes the list of persons. The comparison runs on to the geresh on כַּחֶסֶד (21:23.11).
- The last clause (21:23.17–21) adds the land as a second object of the same request. The feminine suffix in בָּהּ (21:23.21) refers to אֶרֶץ.

Translator's note: The oath is a conditional with no apodosis: 'if you deal falsely with me' stands by itself, and the English does not complete it. A reading that completes it as 'that you will not deal falsely' is common, but the Hebrew has no negative, so the English does not add one.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:21:23:1` follows: Ibn Ezra, on תִּשְׁקֹר: «מֵהַבִּנְיָן הַקַּל וְאֵין לוֹ אָח», 'from the light binyan, and it has no brother'. He places the form in Qal, which has no sibling binyan. The English reads Qal, 'deal falsely'.
- `ibn-ezra:21:23:2` follows: Ibn Ezra, on נכדי: «בֶּן בְּנִי», 'son of my son'. The English gives 'grandson'.
- `rashi:21:23:1` not-applicable: Rashi remarks that the father's compassion for the son extends only as far as the grandson. A remark on the list of persons, not a reading of the words.
- `rashi:21:23:2` declines: Rashi, citing Genesis Rabbah, reads the kindness as Abraham's earlier words: 'here is my land before you'. A midrashic reading; the plain sense of כַּחֶסֶד is kindness, and the English keeps it.
- `ramban:21:23:1` follows: Ramban explains that אִם in an oath is a conditional whose curse is left unstated, with parallels in 1 Samuel 3:14 and Psalm 89:36. The English keeps the clause without its apodosis, as the Hebrew has it.
- `radak:21:23:1` records: Radak reads אִם as 'perhaps' (אוּלַי), not as a condition: 'perhaps you will deal falsely with me'. The English keeps 'if'.
- `radak:21:23:2` not-applicable: Radak gives the purpose of the oath: that Abraham's sons will keep it. A reason, not a reading of the words.
- `radak:21:23:3` records: Radak takes נִין as 'son' and נֶכֶד as 'son's son', citing Isaiah 14:22. The English keeps 'offspring' for נִין, which covers descendants more widely.
- `radak:21:23:4` records: Radak says the kindness was Abraham's gift of his land to Abimelech to dwell in as he wished. The Hebrew names no particular kindness, so the English does not state one.
- `radak:21:23:5` records: Radak reads עִם הָאָרֶץ as 'with its inhabitants' and says the oath binds them too. The English keeps 'the land'.
- `sforno:21:23:1` not-applicable: Sforno gives the purpose of the kindness: that Abimelech should be sworn to on behalf of his sons. A reason, not a reading of the words.
- apparatus: Radak reads אִם as 'perhaps' rather than 'if'. The English keeps 'if'; the commentary records his reading.

Grammar notes:
- The reply has three parts: a wayyiqtol, the name, and a pronoun with a yiqtol. The pronoun אָנֹכִי adds emphasis; the English shows it only through the choice of 'I' at the start of the reply.

- `onkelos:21:24:1` records: Onkelos renders the verb as 'I will confirm' (אֲנָא אֲקַיֵּם), taking it as making a word good. The English keeps 'swear', the sense of the Niphal in the lexicon.
- `sforno:21:24:1` not-applicable: Sforno reads the reply as a contrast: Abraham will do the kindness and swear, but Abimelech did not do kindness to him. A reading of the reply's logic that the words do not state.

### Translation under review (21:29)

English: Abimelech said to Abraham, "What are these seven ewe-lambs that you set by themselves?"

Grammar notes:
- מָה הֵנָּה (21:29.5–6) is a verbless question: 'what (are) these?'. The English supplies the copula 'are'.
- הָאֵלֶּה (21:29.9) follows the noun without an article on the noun, so it is attributive: 'these seven ewe-lambs'.
- אֲשֶׁר הִצַּבְתָּ (21:29.10–11) is a relative clause with a Hifil qatal, 2nd masculine singular. The addressee is Abraham.
- The feminine forms הֵנָּה and לְבַדָּנָה agree with כְּבָשׂוֹת. The English 'these' and 'by themselves' are plural, which agrees with the noun.
- The question ends with the silluq on לְבַדָּנָה (21:29.12). The zaqef qaton on הָאֵלֶּה (21:29.9) divides the question from the relative clause.

Translator's note: Ibn Ezra's reading of לְבַדָּנָה is in the commentary and the apparatus; the English keeps the plural.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:21:29:1` follows: Ibn Ezra, on מָה הֵנָּה: «לְשׁוֹן נְקֵבוֹת», 'a feminine form'. The English 'these' agrees with the feminine ewe-lambs, and the feminine gender is noted here since English cannot show it.
- `ibn-ezra:21:29:2` follows: Ibn Ezra, on לְבַדָּנָה: «עִם ה״א וְהִיא לְבַדָּהּ», 'with a heh; it is the same as lebadah'. He takes the word as the feminine form of 'alone, by herself'. The English keeps the sense 'by themselves' and the plural that the -nah suffix marks, since he does not discuss the number and the word agrees with the plural ewe-lambs.
- apparatus: Ibn Ezra's לְבַדָּהּ (alone, feminine singular) would give 'by herself'. The English keeps the plural that the suffix -nah marks, agreeing with the ewe-lambs.

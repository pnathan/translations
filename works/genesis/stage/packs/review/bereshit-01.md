### Translation under review (1:1)

English: When God began to create the heavens and the earth,

Literal: in-beginning-of created God [object-marker] the-heavens and-[object-marker] the-earth

| token | gloss | lex | sense | note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1.1 | in the beginning of |  | 1.a | בְּרֵאשִׁית is pointed as a noun that can be read in the construct state, 'in the beginning of (God's) creating'. The pack parses it as absolute; the data is kept and the construct reading is argued from Rashi and Ibn Ezra. |
| 1:1.2 | created |  | Qal | Qal perfect, 3rd masculine singular of בָּרָא; the verb has God alone as subject in the Qal. |
| 1:1.3 | God |  | 3 | plural in form (אֱלֹהִים) but takes a singular verb (בָּרָא). |
| 1:1.4 | (object marker) |  |  | אֵת marks the definite direct object. |
| 1:1.5 | the heavens |  | 1.a | שָׁמַיִם has a dual-looking plural ending and is always plural in Hebrew. |
| 1:1.6 | and (object marker) |  |  | וְאֵת: the conjunction repeats the object marker before the second object. |
| 1:1.7 | the earth |  | 1.a |  |

Phrases:
- 1:1.1 1:1.2 1:1.3 | When God began to create | 'in-beginning-of created God'; Rashi and Ibn Ezra both read בְּרֵאשִׁית as bound to the following verb, so the verse is a time clause
- 1:1.4 1:1.5 | the heavens | '[object-marker] the-heavens'; the marker has no English counterpart
- 1:1.6 1:1.7 | and the earth | 'and-[object-marker] the-earth'

Grammar notes:
- בְּרֵאשִׁית is read as a construct: 'in the beginning of God's creating'. Rashi cites בְּרֵאשִׁית מַמְלֶכֶת יְהוֹיָקִים (Jer 26:1) and תְּחִלַּת דִּבֶּר ה' בְּהוֹשֵׁעַ (Hos 1:2) for a noun of beginning bound to a finite verb. Ibn Ezra adds the same two parallels.
- On this reading the verse is a subordinate clause, and the main clause comes in verse 3 (וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים); verse 2 is a circumstantial description of the earth at that time.
- The etnaḥta on אֱלֹהִים divides the verse in two: subject and verb, then the two objects.
- אֵת ... וְאֵת: the object marker is repeated before each of the two objects; הַשָּׁמַיִם וְהָאָרֶץ is the standard pair for 'everything'.
- אֱלֹהִים is plural in form with a singular verb; Ibn Ezra takes the plural as a plural of honour, and notes that the verb is בָּרָא, not בָּרְאוּ.

Translator's note: The English follows the construct reading of both normative commentators. The other reading, an independent sentence 'In the beginning God created', is the form of the pointing בְּרֵאשִׁ֖ית with a tipḥa, which is also a disjunctive accent; the accent does not settle the question. Ibn Ezra rejects creation out of nothing as the meaning of בָּרָא; 'create' is kept as the plain English verb without that claim.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:1:1:1` not-applicable: Rashi, citing Rabbi Isaac: the Torah ought to have begun at 'This month is for you' (Exod 12:2); it begins with creation so that Israel can answer the nations who call them robbers of Canaan. This is homily on why the Torah opens here, not on the words.
- `rashi:1:1:2` follows: Rashi: if you would explain it by its plain sense, 'In the beginning of the creation of heaven and earth, the earth was formless and void ... and God said, Let there be light'. רֵאשִׁית is always bound to the word after it (as in Jer 26:1, Gen 10:10, Deut 18:4), so בְּרֵאשִׁית בָּרָא is like בְּרֵאשִׁית בְּרֹא. The verse does not teach the order of creation.
- `rashi:1:1:3` not-applicable: Rashi: the verse says אֱלֹהִים and not the LORD (יהוה) because God first intended to create by the attribute of justice, then joined mercy to it. This is a homily on the divine names, not on the sense of the words.
- `ibn-ezra:1:1:1` follows: Ibn Ezra rejects the view that the ב is superfluous or that בְּרֵאשִׁית is always construct only with time words. His own view: 'it is in construct, like בראשית ממלכת יהויקים', and one should not be surprised at a construct before a past verb, as in תחלת דבר ד' בהושע. The sense is explained at the next verse.
- `ibn-ezra:1:1:2` records: Ibn Ezra: most commentators say בְּרִיאָה means producing something out of nothing, but he cites ויברא אלהים את התנינים, את האדם and ובורא חשך (darkness is the opposite of existing light) against that. He takes בָּרָא as 'to cut, decide, set a limit' (cf. ובראת לך, וברא אותהן), and tells the wise to understand. The English keeps 'create' without the claim of creation from nothing.
- `ibn-ezra:1:1:3` follows: Ibn Ezra: since the singular אֱלוֹהַּ exists, אֱלֹהִים is a plural of honour, like אֲדֹנִים and בְּעָלִים, and therefore the verb is singular (בָּרָא, not בָּרְאוּ). He also says the name is a title (a descriptive term), and one may call God's agents by it. The English renders it 'God' as a singular.
- `ibn-ezra:1:1:4` follows: Ibn Ezra: אֵת means 'the very thing' and is the sign of the object, though it is sometimes omitted (Deut 4:32) and once or twice stands for 'with' or 'from'. Here it is the object marker, with no English word.
- `ibn-ezra:1:1:5` follows: Ibn Ezra: הַשָּׁמַיִם takes the article of knowledge to show that the visible heavens are meant, and the word is always plural. He thinks that 'the heavens and the earth' here are the firmament and the dry land, since the first verse creates only one thing at a time. The English has 'the heavens'.
- apparatus: Radak (non-normative) reads בְּרֵאשִׁית as absolute, 'at first', as in מַגִּיד מֵרֵאשִׁית אַחֲרִית (Isa 46:10); this is the alternative reading 'In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth'.
- apparatus: Radak and Sforno take הַשָּׁמַיִם as the spheres; Ibn Ezra as the firmament.

Grammar notes:
- The clause begins with ו + noun + verb (וְהָאָרֶץ הָיְתָה), not with a wayyiqtol. This is a circumstantial clause that describes the earth at the time of verse 1; Ibn Ezra explains the ו as a 'ו rafa' (a weak connective, 'fa' in Arabic), and so does not break the construct reading of verse 1.
- תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ is an alliterating pair of nouns, with the ו before the second one pointed וָ because of the stress.
- רוּחַ is feminine, so the participle is feminine (מְרַחֶפֶת, Piel); the participle gives an ongoing scene, not a sequence.
- The zaqef qaton on תֹהוּ ... וָבֹהוּ and the etnaḥta on תְהוֹם divide the verse: the earth, then the darkness over the deep, then the wind of God over the waters.
- The repetition of עַל־פְּנֵי in the two halves (the deep, the waters) is a parallel; the ordinary reading takes תְהוֹם and מַיִם as the same body of water.

Translator's note: Rashi and Ibn Ezra both read verses 1-3 as one sentence in which verse 2 is a description of the earth before the first word of God, so verse 2 is not a main clause in English. For רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים Ibn Ezra takes the wind as God's agent that dries the water; Rashi has the throne of glory hovering by the breath of God's mouth. 'Wind of God' keeps both the wind and the divine source; 'the spirit of God' and 'a mighty wind' (Radak, אלהים as intensifier) are recorded in the apparatus.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:1:2:1` follows: Rashi: תֹּהוּ is a word of astonishment and desolation, that a person is astonished and appalled at the emptiness (בֹּהוּ) in it. The English 'formless and void' conveys the empty state.
- `rashi:1:2:2` follows: Rashi glosses תֹהוּ with the Old French word 'estordison' (astonishment). It confirms the sense of desolation.
- `rashi:1:2:3` follows: Rashi: בֹּהוּ is a word of emptiness and desolation (רֵקוּת וְצָדוּ). The English has 'void'.
- `rashi:1:2:4` follows: Rashi: 'over the face of the deep' means over the face of the water that was upon the earth. The English 'the deep' is the covering water.
- `rashi:1:2:5` records: Rashi: the throne of glory stands in the air and hovers over the face of the waters, by the breath of God's mouth and by his word, like a dove that hovers over the nest. The English keeps 'hovering' (the dove image) but renders רוּחַ as 'wind', not as the throne of glory.
- `ibn-ezra:1:2:1` not-applicable: Ibn Ezra: the word is strange, since the alef has a qamets after the he (and not a pataḥ) in every place, unlike הָאֶלֶף (Song 5:12). This is a note on the vowels of the article, not on the sense.
- `ibn-ezra:1:2:2` follows: Ibn Ezra rejects Saadia's derivation of תֹהוּ from תְהוֹם and the Sefer Yetzirah reading of 'green line'. He says the right sense is what the Aramaic translator gives, as in בְּתֹהוּ יְלֵל יְשִׁמֹן and אַחֲרֵי הַתֹּהוּ, 'that which has no substance'. The English 'formless' fits that sense.
- `ibn-ezra:1:2:3` follows: Ibn Ezra: בֹּהוּ is the brother of תֹהוּ, the ו standing for a ה. He says the sense of the two verses is that at the first creation of the firmament and dry land there was no habitation on the earth, since it was covered with water; and the ו of וְהָאָרֶץ is the weak connective of Arabic, as in ואד יעלה (Gen 2:6). This supports the construct reading of verse 1.
- `ibn-ezra:1:2:4` follows: Ibn Ezra: the רוּחַ is attributed to God because it is a messenger sent to do God's will, to dry the water. The English takes it as 'wind' (God's wind, the agent).
- `ibn-ezra:1:2:5` follows: Ibn Ezra: מַיִם is a plural word that is never singular, and it is in the form of a dual, because it includes male and female waters; it is found with a singular sense in לֹא זֹרַק עָלָיו. The English has 'waters'.
- `ibn-ezra:1:2:6` follows: Ibn Ezra: מְרַחֶפֶת means 'dwelling above, in the water', as in עַל גּוֹזָלָיו יְרַחֵף (Deut 32:11). The English 'hovering' adopts it.
- apparatus: רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים: 'spirit of God' (sense 9 of the lexicon) is a real alternative; Radak (non-normative) offers 'the will of God', 'the elemental air', or a 'mighty wind' with אֱלֹהִים as an intensifier (cf. עִיר גְּדוֹלָה לֵאלֹהִים).
- apparatus: תְהוֹם is feminine according to Radak (Ezek 26:19) but is also found as masculine (Ps 42:8); the verse gives no agreement to show it.
- apparatus: The pack parses 1:2.9 רוּחַ as feminine construct; the verb is feminine, so the data is consistent.

### Translation under review (1:3)

English: God said, "Let there be light," and there was light.

Literal: and-said God let-be light and-was light

| token | gloss | lex | sense | note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1:3.1 | and said |  | Qal 1 | wayyiqtol (וַיֹּאמֶר), the narrative verb form for a sequence of events in the past; the ו is rendered by the sequence of the sentence. |
| 1:3.2 | God |  | 3 |  |
| 1:3.3 | let there be |  | Qal III.1 | jussive of הָיָה (יְהִי), a short form that expresses a command or wish in the 3rd person. |
| 1:3.4 | light |  | 1 |  |
| 1:3.5 | and there was |  | Qal III.1 | wayyiqtol of הָיָה (וַיְהִי), the same root as the jussive; the narrator uses the same word to report the fulfilment. |
| 1:3.6 | light |  | 1 |  |

Phrases:
- 1:3.1 1:3.2 | God said | 'and-said God'; the ו marks the sequence and is not translated
- 1:3.3 1:3.4 | Let there be light | 'let-be light'; jussive
- 1:3.5 1:3.6 | and there was light | 'and-was light'; the fulfilment is stated in the words of the command

Grammar notes:
- וַיֹּאמֶר is a wayyiqtol; on the construct reading of 1:1 it is the main verb of the sentence that began in 1:1.
- יְהִי is a jussive, the short form of יִהְיֶה; וַיְהִי is the wayyiqtol of the same root. The command and its fulfilment use the same verb with the same word order.
- The etnaḥta on אוֹר divides the verse into the command and its execution.
- Ramban notes that the verse does not have וַיְהִי־כֵן, 'and it was so', as the later days do.

Translator's note: The English follows Ibn Ezra in taking the saying literally, as a way to say that the act cost God no effort. אוֹר is rendered 'light' throughout.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:1:3:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: Saadia said that וַיֹּאמֶר means 'he willed', but if so the text should have said 'light' alone; it is as it sounds, as in בִּדְבַר ה' שָׁמַיִם נַעֲשׂוּ and כִּי הוּא צִוָּה וְנִבְרָאוּ. The sense is a figure that the work was not done with effort, a parable of a king and his servants. The English keeps 'said'.
- `ibn-ezra:1:3:2` not-applicable: Ibn Ezra: this light was above the wind (רוּחַ). This is a statement about the position of the light in the cosmos and does not bear on the English.
- apparatus: Ramban (non-normative) reads אֲמִירָה as will or thought, 'God willed that there be light'; Ibn Ezra rejects the equation of 'said' with 'willed'.

### Translation under review (1:4)

English: God saw that the light was good, and God separated the light from the darkness.

Literal: and-saw God [object-marker]-the-light that good and-separated God between the-light and-between the-darkness

Grammar notes:
- וַיַּרְא ... כִּי־טוֹב: the object of 'saw' is the light (אֶת־הָאוֹר), and the content is the clause כִּי־טוֹב, a verbless clause 'that it was good'.
- וַיַּבְדֵּל is a Hiphil wayyiqtol (הִבְדִּיל, 'to cause to be apart'); the Hebrew idiom is 'separate between X and between Y'.
- The etnaḥta on טוֹב divides the verse into the seeing and the separating.
- הַחֹשֶׁךְ, with the article, is the darkness of verse 2 that has just been mentioned.

Translator's note: Both normative commentators read the seeing as approval, and Rashi's plain sense is that light and darkness were given each its own limit, one for day and one for night. Ibn Ezra takes the separation to be done by naming (see verse 5); the English keeps 'separated', which does not decide the question.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:1:4:1` follows: Rashi: his midrashic reading is that God saw that the light was not fit for the wicked to use and set it apart for the righteous in the future. By its plain sense: he saw that it was good, and it was not fitting that it and the darkness should be used mixed together, so he fixed a limit for the one by day and the other by night. The English follows the plain sense.
- `ibn-ezra:1:4:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: וַיַּרְא is like וְרָאִיתִי אֲנִי (Eccl 2:13, Dan 10:7), and it is in thought. The English 'saw' is kept, with the meaning of considering, and it says nothing of physical sight.
- `ibn-ezra:1:4:2` records: Ibn Ezra: the sense of וַיַּבְדֵּל is 'in the calling of the names', that is, the separation is made by naming day and night (verse 5). The English keeps the plain 'separated' and records his reading.
- apparatus: Ramban (non-normative) rejects Ibn Ezra: seeing is the continuing existence of what was spoken into being, and the darkness here is the absence of light, not the elemental fire of verse 2.
- apparatus: Radak (non-normative) says וַיַּבְדֵּל is by naming, because darkness is not a thing that can be separated from light.

### Translation under review (1:5)

English: God called the light day, and the darkness he called night. There was evening and there was morning, one day.

Literal: and-called God to-the-light day and-to-the-darkness he-called night and-was evening and-was morning day one

Phrases:
- 1:5.1 1:5.2 1:5.3 1:5.4 | God called the light day | 'and-called God to-the-light day'
- 1:5.5 1:5.6 1:5.7 | and the darkness he called night | 'and-to-the-darkness he-called night'; the fronted object marks a contrast

- 1:5.10 1:5.11 | and there was morning | 'and-was morning'

Grammar notes:
- וַיִּקְרָא ... לָאוֹר יוֹם: 'call X Y' is expressed with ל before the thing named and the name after it.
- וְלַחֹשֶׁךְ קָרָא לָיְלָה puts the object first and uses a qatal, which gives a contrast with the first half of the verse.
- Ibn Ezra notes that the ה of לַיְלָה is a stress-bearing addition and the word is always masculine, so it takes a masculine verb.
- The etnaḥta on לָיְלָה divides the verse between the naming and the formula of the day; the formula וַיְהִי־עֶרֶב וַיְהִי־בֹקֶר ends each day of the account.
- יוֹם אֶחָד has the cardinal 'one' where later days have ordinals (שֵׁנִי, שְׁלִישִׁי); the paragraph mark פ closes the first section.

Translator's note: The English 'one day' follows Rashi, Ibn Ezra, and the Hebrew, which says אֶחָד and not רִאשׁוֹן. Ibn Ezra insists the evening cannot be a 'day' since the light has been called 'day'; the clause is 'there was evening and there was morning, of one day', and the English line keeps the Hebrew order without extra words.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:1:5:1` follows: Rashi: by the order of the language of the passage, the Torah should have written 'first day', as the others have 'second, third, fourth'. It wrote אֶחָד ('one') because God was alone in his world, since the angels were created only on the second day (so it says in Genesis Rabbah). The English gives 'one day'; the reason is a midrash and is not in the English.
- `ibn-ezra:1:5:1` not-applicable: Ibn Ezra: the ה of לַיְלָה is an addition because the word has penultimate stress, and it is always masculine. This is a grammar note on the form of the word.
- `ibn-ezra:1:5:2` not-applicable: Ibn Ezra: עֶרֶב is close in sense to חֹשֶׁךְ and is so called because the forms are mixed together (נִתְעָרְבוּ) in it; בֹּקֶר is the opposite, because one can look carefully (לְבַקֵּר) among the forms. This is an etymology; it does not alter the English words 'evening' and 'morning'.
- `ibn-ezra:1:5:3` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'one day' refers to the motion of the sphere, and there is a secret in the midrash (שית אלפי). Since the light was called 'day', the evening cannot also be called 'day'; the meaning is that there was an evening and also a morning belonging to one day. If evening and morning together were one day, what would 'second day' mean? The English gives 'There was evening and there was morning, one day'.
- apparatus: Radak (non-normative): 'one' and not 'first' because there is no first without a second, and the second had not yet been made; or, with Moses ibn Ezra, because 'one' is the sign of a beginning that nothing precedes.
- apparatus: Ramban (non-normative): evening is the beginning of the night, so called because the forms are mixed; morning is the beginning of the day.

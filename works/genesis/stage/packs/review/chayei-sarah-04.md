### Translation under review (24:1)

English: Abraham was old and advanced in years, and the LORD had blessed Abraham in all things.

Literal: and-Abraham old came in-days; and-LORD blessed ACC-Abraham in-all

Grammar notes:
- The clause וְאַבְרָהָם זָקֵן בָּא בַּיָּמִים has a noun subject and a qatal, so it is background to the main clause, which is the blessing.
- The idiom בָּא בַּיָּמִים, 'come in the days', means advanced in years; the English keeps it as one phrase and gives no age.
- וַיהוָה בֵּרַךְ אֶת־אַבְרָהָם repeats the background pattern with the divine name as subject; 'had blessed' is that pluperfect.
- The verse ends with בַּכֹּל, the one place in Genesis 24 where the blessing has a scope: 'in all' (Ibn Ezra's reading, in the commentary).

Translator's note: The English gives the words only. What the blessing consisted of (length of days, wealth, honour, sons) is Ibn Ezra's gloss and is kept in the commentary. Rashi's numerical reading of בַּכֹּל is a midrash and is recorded, not followed.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:24:1:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'blessed in all' means in length of days, wealth, honour and sons, which he calls all of man's desire. He says the ב of בַּכֹּל is to be read as a beth that serves as 'in', so the phrase is 'in all'; the English follows that reading of the preposition, and his list of what the blessing was stays in the note.
- `rashi:24:1:1` not-applicable: Rashi: בַּכֹּל has the numerical value of בֵּן (son, 52), and since Abraham had a son he had to marry him off. A numerical reading and a reason for the errand; it does not bear on the English.
- apparatus: 24:1.5: the pack's base vowels for the divine name differ from the WLC pointing (יְהֹוָה in the base, יְהוָה in the WLC); the English is the same either way.

Grammar notes:
- The main clause is a wayyiqtol, 'and said'; the speech follows it directly with no second introduction until the servant speaks in 24:5.
- שִׂים נָא יָדְךָ תַּחַת יְרֵכִי is an imperative with the particle נָא, 'please'; the object is the hand, and its place is given by תַּחַת.
- The servant is described by a construct chain and two participial or relative phrases before the command; the English keeps that order.
- The gesture itself is the thing to be read: the hand is placed under the thigh of the one who speaks. The meanings given to it are the commentators' and are kept out of the English.

Translator's note: The gesture is described plainly. Three readings are recorded: an oath gesture, a sign of being under another's authority, and an allusion to circumcision. Ibn Ezra's choice is not adopted in the English because his explanation is what the gesture meant, which the English does not state.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:24:2:1` records: Ibn Ezra gives two readings. Some say the gesture alludes to circumcision; he answers that then the oath would be by the covenant of circumcision and not by the Name. His own view is that in those days a man put his hand under the thigh of one in his power, as in 'my hand is under your power'; he adds that the custom still exists in the land of Hodu. The English states the gesture only.
- `rashi:24:2:1` follows: Rashi: זקן is pointed as a bound form because it is joined to בית; the vowel is the construct, and the English reads 'the elder of his household'.
- `rashi:24:2:2` records: Rashi: an oath needs the hand on a holy object, such as a Torah scroll or tefillin; the thigh here is circumcision, the first commandment given to Abraham, which cost him pain and was dear to him. A midrashic-symbolic reading of the gesture; it is recorded, not followed.
- `radak:24:2:2` records: Radak: it was a custom that one who charges another puts the hand under his thigh to confirm the matter, as a hand under the thigh is under one's power; it was a strong custom for every agreement, like a covenant between two men.
- apparatus: 24:2: the gesture is read three ways in the commentary (oath, subjection, circumcision); the English line gives only 'put your hand under my thigh'.

Grammar notes:
- Three relative clauses, each with אֲשֶׁר, hang from 'the LORD, God of heaven': who took me, who spoke to me, who swore to me. The main clause begins with 'he will send'.
- לֵאמֹר לְזַרְעֲךָ אֶתֵּן אֶת־הָאָרֶץ הַזֹּאת is speech inside Abraham's speech, so it is in single quotes.
- The yiqtol הוּא יִשְׁלַח is read as a wish, 'may he send'; the next verse's 'if the woman will not be willing' supports Ibn Ezra's reading (see commentary).
- The weqatal וְלָקַחְתָּ continues the purpose of the sending: 'and you shall take a wife'.

Translator's note: The epithet 'God of heaven' stands alone here, unlike 24:3, where 'and God of earth' is added. Rashi explains the difference; the English keeps the words as they stand.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:24:7:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'he will send his angel' is a prayer, not a prophecy; if it were prophecy he would not say 'if the woman will not be willing' (24:5). The English reads the yiqtol as a wish, 'may he send'.
- `sforno:24:7:1` follows: Sforno reads the clause as a wish: may he send his angel from his holy dwelling in the heavens, since he took me from there, so that my son need not go there. This supports the wish reading.
- `rashi:24:7:1` not-applicable: Rashi explains why the epithet here is 'God of heaven' alone: when God took Abraham from his father's house he was 'God of heaven and not God of earth', because the people of the world did not know him and his name was not common in the land. A reason for the wording, not a reading of its grammar.
- `rashi:24:7:2` records: Rashi: 'my father's house' is Haran. The English keeps 'my father's house'.
- `rashi:24:7:3` records: Rashi: 'the land of my birth' is Ur of the Chaldeans. The English keeps 'the land of my birth'.
- `rashi:24:7:4` records: Rashi: לִי here means 'for my sake', 'about me' (like עָלַי), as with verbs of speaking; the English 'spoke to me' keeps the plain preposition.
- apparatus: 24:7.1: the epithet is 'God of heaven' without 'and God of earth' as in 24:3; the commentators' explanations are in the commentary.

Grammar notes:
- The conditional has a protasis (8.1 to 8.6), 'if the woman will not be willing', and an apodosis (8.7) introduced by the vav of נִקִּיתָ: 'then you shall be clear'.
- נִקִּיתָ is a nifal perfect of a III-he verb: the pattern is regular, and the English 'shall be clear' gives the nifal sense of freedom from an obligation.
- The last clause puts the object first: רַק אֶת־בְּנִי לֹא תָשֵׁב שָׁמָּה, 'only my son you shall not bring back there'. The restriction applies to the son alone.
- The speech closes here, at the end of the unit's last verse.

Translator's note: The English gives the plain sense of the hifil: the servant is not to bring the son back. Ibn Ezra's and Rashi's readings of this clause are recorded in the commentary and not followed.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:24:8:1` declines: Ibn Ezra: 'you shall not bring him back there' means that he (Isaac) is to remain in the land of Israel, and the negative serves Abraham, who is the principal. The English keeps the hifil with the servant as subject and Isaac as object, 'bring back', which the form requires; his reason is not a reading of the words, and 'remain' is not in the form.
- `rashi:24:8:1` declines: Rashi reads the release from the oath as permission to take a wife for Isaac from the daughters of Aner, Eshkol and Mamre. The Hebrew here names no family; the English keeps 'be clear of this oath'.
- `rashi:24:8:2` not-applicable: Rashi: רַק is a limit, 'my son does not return', and Jacob his grandson will in the end return. A later reading; the English 'only' is the same word.

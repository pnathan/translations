### Translation under review (12:9)

English: Abram set out, going on from stage to stage toward the Negeb.

Literal: and-set-out Abram going and-setting-out the-Negeb-ward

| token | gloss | lex | sense | note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12:9.1 | and set out |  | Qal 2.a | wayyiqtol; the verb is used of pulling up tent pegs and breaking camp |
| 12:9.2 | Abram |  |  |  |
| 12:9.3 | going |  | Qal II.2 | infinitive absolute of הלך; with the next word it gives continuing action |
| 12:9.4 | and journeying on |  | Qal 3 | infinitive absolute joined to the preceding one |
| 12:9.5 | toward the Negeb |  |  | הַ + נֶגֶב + directional ה; the Negeb is also the word for 'south' |

Phrases:
- 12:9.1 12:9.2 | Abram set out | 'and-set-out Abram'; wayyiqtol opens the narrative
- 12:9.3 12:9.4 | going on from stage to stage | 'going and-journeying'; the paired infinitives express a progressive journey in stages (Rashi: a month or more at each place)
- 12:9.5 | toward the Negeb | 'the-Negeb-ward'; directional he; also 'southward' (Ibn Ezra)

Grammar notes:
- וַיִּסַּע is a wayyiqtol; the munaḥ joins it to אַבְרָם before the zaqef.
- הָלוֹךְ וְנָסוֹעַ: two infinitives absolute in a row, here describing continued action (going and setting out), not commands.
- The ending ־ָה on הַנֶּגְבָּה is the directional he, 'toward'; the word means both the region Negeb and 'south'.
- The paragraph break (פ) after the verse closes the section.

Translator's note: The pair of infinitive absolutes is rendered 'going on from stage to stage' with Rashi and Radak ('from place to place'), who take it as repeated moves. The Negeb is kept as a place name while Ibn Ezra's 'south' is recorded.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:12:9:1` follows: Rashi: he stayed in each place a month or more, then pulled up and pitched his tent elsewhere, and all his journeys were toward the south (the Negeb) of the Land of Israel. The English follows the staged journey southward; the midrash on Judah's portion is not used.
- `ibn-ezra:12:9:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: הנגבה is the southern side, and the name is explained from Aramaic 'dry', since the south is hot and dry (cf. Josh 15:19). The English renders it as the Negeb, the southern region.
- apparatus: Radak and Sforno read the pair as travelling from place to place like shepherds; Onkelos has 'went and set out to the south'.

### Translation under review (12:10)

English: Now there was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to sojourn there, for the famine in the land was severe.

Phrases:
- 12:10.1 12:10.2 12:10.3 | there was a famine in the land | 'and-was famine in-the-land'; the etnaḥta ends the clause
- 12:10.4 12:10.5 12:10.6 | and Abram went down to Egypt | 'and-went-down Abram Egypt-ward'; directional he
- 12:10.7 12:10.8 | to sojourn there | 'to-sojourn there'; temporary residence (Radak, Sforno)
- 12:10.9 12:10.10 12:10.11 12:10.12 | for the famine in the land was severe | 'for heavy the-famine in-the-land'; literally 'heavy'

Grammar notes:
- וַיְהִי רָעָב opens with wayyiqtol הָיָה, 'there was'; the subject follows the verb.
- מִצְרַיְמָה carries the directional he, 'to Egypt'; the two pashta accents mark the word as a unit.
- לָגוּר is a bare infinitive of purpose with לְ; גּוּר means to live as a stranger without full rights.
- כִּי gives the reason, and the adjective כָּבֵד 'heavy' precedes its subject in the predicate.

Translator's note: 'Severe' renders 'heavy', which is the idiom for a famine. Rashi's reading that the famine was a test for Abram is recorded but does not change the English.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:12:10:1` records: Rashi: the famine was in that land alone, to test Abram, whether he would doubt God's words that sent him to Canaan, now that He makes him leave it. The English does not rest on this reading; it is a homiletic motive.
- `ibn-ezra:12:10:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'in the land of Canaan'. The English 'the land' is read as Canaan and is so noted.
- apparatus: Radak: Egypt had plenty even in famine, being watered by the Nile rather than rain; Ramban: Abram went down to keep himself alive.

Grammar notes:
- The construction וַיְהִי כַּאֲשֶׁר + qatal + לְ + infinitive is a time clause that leads into וַיֹּאמֶר.
- הִנֵּה־נָא יָדַעְתִּי: the perfect of יָדַע is a present state, 'I know', and carries the sense of 'I have long known'.
- יְפַת־מַרְאֶה is a construct chain, 'beautiful of appearance', the pronoun אָתְּ placed last for stress.
- The qadma and zaqef accents divide the verse into the arrival, the address and the content of the speech.

Translator's note: I follow Rashi's plain sense (the moment has come to worry about her beauty) and Ibn Ezra's gloss of נָא as 'now'. The verb הִקְרִיב is rendered intransitively.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:12:11:1` follows: Rashi gives a midrash (until now he had not looked at her, out of modesty) and then the plain sense: 'now it is time to worry about your beauty' as they come among dark people who are not used to beautiful women. The English follows the plain sense and does not impose the midrash.
- `ibn-ezra:12:11:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: הקריב may mean 'his camp' understood, or is one of the verbs that are both transitive and intransitive. The English takes it as intransitive, 'drew near'.
- `ibn-ezra:12:11:2` follows: Ibn Ezra: נא means 'now', as in 'now I am old' (27:2) and 'woe now to us' (Lam 5:16). The English renders 'Look now'.
- `ibn-ezra:12:11:3` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'I know' means that Sarai's beauty was such in her own land, but in Egypt and the Negev none was like her, since looks change with the climate. The English keeps the plain 'I know'; the climatic explanation goes in the note.

Commentary rulings:

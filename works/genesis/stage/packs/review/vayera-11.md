- 20:9.23 20:9.24 | You have done to me | 'you-did with-me': the verb comes after its object in the Hebrew

Grammar notes:
- Two wayyiqtol verbs carry the scene: וַיִּקְרָא 'then summoned' and וַיֹּאמֶר 'and said'. The speech that follows is two questions and one statement.
- The etnaḥta on גְּדֹלָה ends the first half of the verse, the complaint with its 'great' sin. The second half is the charge, with the object before the verb: מַעֲשִׂים אֲשֶׁר לֹא יֵעָשׂוּ עָשִׂיתָ עִמָּדִי.
- לֹא יֵעָשׂוּ is a yiqtol of the nifal with negation: the deeds are such as should not be done.
- The suffix on לָךְ is pointed as second feminine singular, though Abimelech speaks to Abraham; the English does not mark it.

Translator's note: The complaint is in two parts, a question about what Abraham did and a charge that the deeds are wrong. The English keeps both questions as questions.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:20:9:1` not-applicable: Ibn Ezra notes the vowel under מה: a patah, not a segol, because of the guttural that follows it (the ע of עָשִׂיתָ). He adds that the same holds for the word after it, but the comment breaks off there in the pack. It concerns the pointing, not the sense.
- `rashi:20:9:1` records: Rashi reads the deeds as a plague that had never before come on a creature: the stopping of all the bodily openings of the women and children, of seed, of the young, of the bowels, of ears and nose. The English does not adopt this; it keeps 'deeds' and the plague reading is recorded here.
- `radak:20:9:5` follows: Radak: the deeds are not fit to be done to a man like you, to bring people into the stumbling of sin. This matches 'ought not to be done'.
- apparatus: The pack's text of Ibn Ezra on this verse ends mid-sentence; only the complete part is used.

Grammar notes:
- כִּי begins the reason: 'Because I thought'. The reason for the act is given before its consequence.
- אָמַרְתִּי is qatal, first person, 'say' in the heart (Qal 2). The verb has no object; the sentence after it is what he thought.
- רַק is the emphatic 'surely' (BDB 2). Radak's 'only' is recorded in the commentary.
- וַהֲרָגוּנִי is weqatal, giving the consequence; the subject is impersonal. The etnaḥta on הַזֶּה divides the verse: the thought, then its consequence.

Translator's note: Abraham gives his reason, not an excuse to Abimelech. The English keeps 'surely' for רַק and 'fear of God' for יִרְאַת אֱלֹהִים.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:20:11:1` records: Rashi reads 'no fear of God' as the custom of a town that asks a stranger about his wife, not about food and drink (Bava Kamma). The English keeps Abraham's own reason; Rashi's reading of the people is recorded here.
- `radak:20:11:1` records: Radak reads רַק as 'only': the land is good, and only this is bad in it, that its people do not fear God, so they would kill him to make his wife free to them. The English reads 'surely'; the 'only' reading is recorded.
- `sforno:20:11:1` declines: Sforno takes 'fear' as fear of royal authority: the Philistine lords were not full kings whom the people feared, as Goliath's words show (1 Samuel 17:8). The English keeps 'fear of God' because the Hebrew names God; Sforno's reading is recorded here for the editor.
- apparatus: Sforno's reading of 'fear' as fear of royal power is not adopted in the English.

Grammar notes:
- Three clauses: the relation (my sister, daughter of my father, she), the exception (not the daughter of my mother), and the marriage (she became my wife).
- הִוא 'she' follows the predicate and is the least emphatic pronoun (BDB 3): 'she is my sister'.
- וְגַם ('and also') adds this statement to the claim of verse 11.
- וַתְּהִי לִי לְאִשָּׁה: 'become' with לְ before the person and the predicate (BDB Qal II.2.f): 'became to me for a wife'. The etnaḥta on אִמִּי divides the verse.

Translator's note: The English gives Abraham's claim as he states it. Ibn Ezra's reading that the statement is an evasion is recorded in the commentary, and the English does not adopt it; the editor should decide this point.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:20:12:1` declines: Ibn Ezra first says that 'my sister, daughter of my father' is an idiom like 'the God of my father Abraham'. He then gives his own view: Abraham put Abimelech off with words suited to the need of the moment, and he points to the parallel at Genesis 27:19. The English declines the evasion reading: the Hebrew states the relation as a plain assertion, and the next clause restates it. Editor's decision required.
- `rashi:20:12:1` records: Rashi: a daughter of the father is permitted to the sons of Noah, since there is no fatherhood among the nations; Abraham answered in this form to confirm his words. Rashi raises the objection that Haran's daughter was a niece, and answers that grandchildren are like children. The English keeps 'sister' in its plain sense; the legal reading is recorded.
- `radak:20:12:1` records: Radak reads 'sister' as a brother's daughter, a niece, and so Abraham did not lie: grandchildren are as children. The English reads the word as sister; Radak's reading is recorded.
- `ramban:20:12:1` records: Ramban finds the excuse weak: even if the relation was true, saying 'sister' to mislead is sin. He suggests Abraham's words to Abimelech's 'what did you see' were a plea of fear of the place. The English keeps the claim; Ramban's view of its purpose is recorded.
- apparatus: 'Sister' as a niece (Radak) and as a half-sister through the father (the plain sense) are both recorded in the commentary.

Grammar notes:
- וַיְהִי כַּאֲשֶׁר 'it came about when' opens the clause. The wayehi formula gives the background to the instruction.
- הִתְעוּ is hifil, third plural, with God as subject: a plural form for God (Rashi notes the plural). The English takes Ibn Ezra's sense, 'wander'.
- אִמְרִי־לִי 'say for me' is a second feminine singular imperative addressed to Sarah. The words that follow are the instruction in direct speech.
- The etnaḥta on עִמָּדִי divides the verse: first the wandering and the request, then the words to say in each place.

Translator's note: The English follows Ibn Ezra on the two points he decides: the wandering is travel without knowing the way, and 'say for me' means 'say for my sake'.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:20:13:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: התעו is sacred usage for travel from place to place without knowing where one goes (as Genesis 37:15, the man 'wandering' in the field), not a misleading of the heart. The English reads 'made me wander' in this sense.
- `ibn-ezra:20:13:2` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'אמרי לי' means 'say for my sake' (בשבילי), as in 'Pharaoh said about the children of Israel' (Exodus 14:3). The English reads 'say on my behalf'.
- `rashi:20:13:1` records: Rashi notes Onkelos' rendering and gives a plain reading of his own: when God took him out of his father's house to wander, he knew he would pass through wicked places. The English follows Ibn Ezra's sense of wandering; Rashi's note on the places is recorded.
- `onkelos:20:13:1` declines: Onkelos renders the verb as 'when the peoples went astray after their idols', making the nations the subject. The English keeps Abraham as the one made to wander, as the Hebrew does with אֹתִי.
- `rashi:20:13:3` follows: Rashi: the plural form is a plural of majesty, as for God's name in Elohim. The English takes God as the singular subject.
- `rashi:20:13:4` records: Rashi reads עָלַי ('about me') as in 'they asked the men of the place about his wife' (Genesis 26:7). The English reads 'on my behalf' with Ibn Ezra; the 'about me' reading is recorded.
- `radak:20:13:1` follows: Radak: התעו means 'exiled'; one driven from his place wanders on roads he does not know. The English 'made me wander' covers both the exile and the wandering.
- `radak:20:13:6` records: Radak: the words were not meant for every place, only the places where they would pass briefly among wicked men; in places where Abraham lived, the people knew and honoured him. The English keeps 'every place'; Radak's restriction is recorded.
- `radak:20:13:7` follows: Radak: 'אמרי־לי' means 'for my sake', as in 'the men of the place asked his wife' (Genesis 26:7).
- `sforno:20:13:1` follows: Sforno: because Abraham rejected idolatry, he was driven out of his father's house to an unknown place, not one of his own choosing; so he is called 'wandering'.
- apparatus: Onkelos' rendering (the nations went astray) is recorded but not adopted.

Grammar notes:
- The verse opens with the addressee, 'To Sarah he said'. The qatal אָמַר after the fronted object is a speech report.
- נָתַתִּי 'I have given' is a qatal in direct speech: a completed act, from the speaker's point of view.
- הִנֵּה ('look') is used twice, first for the gift and then for its meaning.
- הוּא 'he' follows הִנֵּה. The nearest masculine noun is 'your brother', so the English reads 'he' as Abraham, as Ibn Ezra does.
- וְאֵת כֹּל: the pack parses אֵת as the object marker. The English reads it as 'with' (Ibn Ezra), since a nifal participle has no object. The data is kept and the disagreement is recorded here.
- נוֹכָחַת is a nifal feminine participle and predicates the state: 'you are reproved'. The etnaḥta on אִתָּךְ divides the verse.

Translator's note: Three readings are recorded. 'He' is Abraham (Ibn Ezra) or the silver (Radak, Sforno, who take 'covering' as a garment). 'Despite all this' follows Ibn Ezra's reading of 'and with all'; his alternative 'with all people' is recorded. The root of נוכחת is יכח, against the Gaon's root נוכח.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:20:16:1` follows: Ibn Ezra takes 'he' (הוּא) as Abraham: he is 'a covering of the eyes for you' so that no man lifts his eyes to Sarah, and for the women with her, the wives of the servants. He rejects the readings that the silver is the covering or that it buys clothing. The English follows him on the covering and on 'and despite all this'.
- `ibn-ezra:20:16:2` records: Ibn Ezra's other reading of אֵת כֹּל: 'and with all people he is for you a covering of the eyes', that is, before everyone. The English adopts the 'despite all this' reading; this is recorded as his alternative.
- `ibn-ezra:20:16:3` follows: Ibn Ezra keeps the vav as the conjunction and takes the word from יכח. He rejects the Gaon's view that it comes from נוכח, which he calls not straight. The English takes the root יכח.
- `rashi:20:16:1` records: Rashi reads the speech as Abimelech's honour and payment to Sarah, to appease her: 'here I have done you this honour, and here is this money'. The English does not adopt this; it is recorded.
- `rashi:20:16:3` records: Rashi: the people will cover their eyes and will not mock Sarah. Had Abimelech sent her back with nothing, they would have said he had abused her and returned her; now the cost shows he returned her against his will, and by a miracle. This is recorded as an alternative to Ibn Ezra's covering.
- `rashi:20:16:4` records: Rashi: 'and with all' means with all who come into the world, that is, before all people. The English keeps 'despite all this'; Rashi's reading is recorded.
- `rashi:20:16:5` records: Rashi glosses נוכחת as 'you will have an opening to argue and to show these matters clear', taking the sense of proof or vindication; Onkelos renders it as 'you shall be seen'. The English reads 'reproved' with Ibn Ezra and Radak.
- `radak:20:16:1` records: Radak: besides the flocks and servants, the thousand silver was given so that Sarah could buy fine garments. This is the garment reading of 'covering'; it is recorded.
- `sforno:20:16:2` records: Sforno: the covering is a garment of many colours, worn by women for honour; the bridal payment shows Sarah was taken as a wife, not as a harlot or concubine. The garment reading is recorded.
- `ramban:20:16:1` records: Ramban: the flocks and servants were worth a thousand silver, and 'elef kesef' may mean thousands of silver, a great sum; the people will fear Sarah's protector and cover their eyes from looking at her. The English keeps 'a thousand pieces of silver'; the thousands reading is recorded.
- apparatus: הוּא (16.9): Abraham, the nearest masculine antecedent (Ibn Ezra), or the silver, with 'covering' as a garment (Radak, Sforno). The English reads Abraham.
- apparatus: נוֹכָחַת (16.18): nifal of יכח. The Gaon's root נוכח is rejected by Ibn Ezra.
- apparatus: The pack's parse of אֵת (16.16) as object marker is not followed in the English; see the note on the token.

### Translation under review (18:1)

English: The LORD appeared to him at the terebinths of Mamre while he was sitting at the entrance of the tent in the heat of the day.

Phrases:
- 18:1.1 18:1.2 18:1.3 | The LORD appeared to him | 'and-appeared to-him the-LORD': the verb comes first in the Hebrew, so the subject comes first in the English.
- 18:1.4 18:1.5 | at the terebinths of Mamre | 'in-terebinths-of Mamre': the preposition bet with the construct, here locating the scene.
- 18:1.6 18:1.7 | while he was sitting | 'and-he sitting': the conjunction and the participle give the background to the appearance.
- 18:1.8 18:1.9 | at the entrance of the tent | 'at-entrance-of the-tent': the construct chain; the English 'at' is supplied.
- 18:1.10 18:1.11 | in the heat of the day | 'like-heat-of the-day': kaf of comparison, here 'in' the heat.

Grammar notes:
- The verb is wayyiqtol (the narrative sequential form). Here it is niphal: the English 'appeared' is the stem's sense 'appear, be seen'.
- The clause 'while he was sitting' is circumstantial: the Hebrew joins a participle to the pronoun, which marks a background situation rather than a second action.
- Construct chains: 'the entrance of the tent' and 'the heat of the day'. Both are read as single English noun phrases.
- The etnahta on Mamre (18:1.5) divides the verse. The first part places the scene at the terebinths of Mamre; the second describes Abraham sitting at the tent in the heat.

Translator's note: The divine name is written YHWH in the Hebrew; the English is 'the LORD' per the decisions.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:18:1:1` records: Rashi glosses the verb as 'to visit the sick': the LORD came to ask after Abraham on the third day after his circumcision (Bava Metzia 86b). The English keeps 'appeared', the niphal sense, and the visit reading is recorded here.
- `ibn-ezra:18:1:1` follows: Ibn Ezra rejects the view that the three men are the Name itself, since the text also says 'the two angels came to Sodom' (19:1). He takes it that the LORD appeared to Abraham in a prophetic vision, after which he lifted his eyes and saw three angels: one to bring the news to Sarah, and two to Sodom, one to destroy and one to save Lot.
- `rashi:18:1:2` records: Rashi takes Mamre as a man, the one who gave Abraham counsel about the circumcision, so the LORD was revealed to Abraham in Mamre's portion. The English keeps Mamre as the place name (13:18, 14:13).
- `rashi:18:1:3` not-applicable: Rashi: the text is written 'sitting' because Abraham wanted to stand and the LORD told him to sit while the LORD stood; this is a sign for his children that God will stand among judges who sit (Ps. 82:1). A homily; the English 'sitting' is the plain word.
- `rashi:18:1:4` not-applicable: Rashi: he sat at the entrance to watch for passers-by and bring them into his house. This gives the purpose, not the sense of the words.
- `rashi:18:1:5` not-applicable: Rashi: the LORD brought out the sun from its sheath so as not to trouble Abraham with guests; seeing him grieve that no guests came, He sent the angels in the form of men. A homily on the heat of the day.
- `ibn-ezra:18:1:2` not-applicable: Ibn Ezra glosses 'and they ate' (18:8) as 'the bread was consumed', like 'which the fire consumes' (Lev. 6:3). It bears on 18:8, not on this verse; see the note there.
- apparatus: Ramban and Radak (not normative) read the appearance as a reward for the circumcision and as the LORD's favour to Abraham; not adopted here.

Grammar notes:
- Five wayyiqtols run in sequence (lifted, saw, ran, bowed down) and the second 'saw' repeats the first. Only the second is not a plain new action.
- Hinneh introduces what the subject sees: 'and look' in the English makes the same point without 'behold'.
- Standing (nitsavim) is a niphal participle used as a predicate. Its subject is the three men; the verb is plural.
- Le-qiratam is an infinitive construct with the 3mp suffix: 'to meet them'. The following wayyishtachu is a hitpa'el, 'bow down'.

Translator's note: The second wayyar is read by Rashi as 'understood' and by Radak as 'a second time'. The English follows Rashi.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:18:2:1` follows: Rashi: the three are three messengers with three tasks (one to tell Sarah, one to overturn Sodom, one to heal Abraham), since one angel does not do two missions; the text uses the plural throughout. The English keeps 'three men' and the plural verbs.
- `rashi:18:2:2` follows: Rashi: 'standing upon him' means 'standing before him'; it is a courteous expression used of the angels.
- `rashi:18:2:3` follows: Rashi: the first 'saw' is literal; the second means understanding. They stood still out of respect, and when they saw him loosing and tying his bandage they moved away from him, and at once he ran to them (Bava Metzia 86b). The English 'he understood' follows this.
- `radak:18:2:3` records: Radak: the second 'and saw' means 'another time', that he saw them again. The English does not adopt it.

Grammar notes:
- The speech has two parts: the offer (cohortative, imperatives and a yiqtol) and the reply (a short imperative with a qatal).
- The compound ki al-ken: 'since, for this reason'. The qatal 'you have passed' is the reason clause.
- The three men speak as a group (plural verbs), but address Abraham in the singular (you do, you have said).
- The etnahta on 'your servant' (18:5.13) divides the verse: the offer comes first, then the speech of the reply.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:18:5:1` follows: Rashi: 'sustain your hearts' refers to the bread that supports the heart (Judg. 19:5, Ps. 104:15). The spelling of 'your hearts' without the doubled letter shows that the evil impulse does not rule over angels (R. Hama). The English follows the sense of supporting the heart.
- `rashi:18:5:2` follows: Rashi: 'afterward you may go on' means afterwards you may go on your way. The English follows this.
- `rashi:18:5:3` follows: Rashi: 'I ask this of you, since you have passed by me to honour me.' The English 'since you have passed by your servant' follows the reason clause.
- `rashi:18:5:4` follows: Rashi: ki al-ken means 'like al asher', 'on account of which', as in Gen. 19:8, 33:10, 38:26 and Num. 10:31. The English renders it 'since'.
- `ibn-ezra:18:5:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'since you have passed', as our predecessors said: the phrase equals 'since' (hu'il), as in 'for this they came' (19:8). The English 'since' follows.
- `ibn-ezra:18:5:2` follows: Ibn Ezra: 'Do so, as you have said' is a manner of courtesy: a morsel of bread is enough. The English follows, with the guests accepting the small offer Abraham made.

### Translation under review (18:8)

English: He took cream and milk and the young bull which he had prepared, and set them before them, and he stood by them under the tree, and they ate.

Literal: and-took cream and-milk and-son-of the-bull which made and-set before-them and-he standing upon-them under the-tree and-ate

Phrases:
- 18:8.1 18:8.2 | He took cream | 'and-took cream'.
- 18:8.3 18:8.4 18:8.5 18:8.6 18:8.7 | and milk and the young bull which he had prepared | 'and-milk and-son-of the-bull which made': the relative clause gives the bull Abraham had prepared.
- 18:8.8 18:8.9 | and set them before them | 'and-set before-them': the object 'them' is the food; 'before them' the men.
- 18:8.10 18:8.11 18:8.12 18:8.13 18:8.14 | and he stood by them under the tree | 'and-he standing upon-them under the-tree'.
- 18:8.15 | and they ate | 'and-ate': the plain verb; Rashi's 'appeared to eat' is recorded in the commentary.

Grammar notes:
- The chain of wayyiqtols (took, set, ate) carries the meal forward; 'and he stood' is a circumstantial clause in the middle.
- The relative clause 'which he had prepared' is a qatal in the past relative to the setting of the food.
- No bread is named in the Hebrew, so the English names none. Rashi explains why (see the commentary).
- The etnahta on 'before them' (18:8.9) divides the verse: the serving comes first, the standing and the eating second.

Translator's note: Ibn Ezra, on 18:1, takes 'they ate' literally: the bread (food) was consumed. That is the basis of the English. The Rashi reading, that they appeared to eat, is recorded below.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:18:8:1` follows: Rashi: no bread is brought because Sarah had become a niddah that day, so the dough was made unclean. The Hebrew names no bread, so the English adds none.
- `rashi:18:8:2` follows: Rashi: hemah is the fat of the milk that gathers on its surface and is skimmed off. The English 'cream' follows this reading; BDB's 'curd' is the alternative.
- `rashi:18:8:3` records: Rashi: 'which he had prepared' means the first of the bulls he had readied, which he brought and set before them (cf. 18:7). The English keeps the singular, the young bull which he had prepared.
- `rashi:18:8:4` records: Rashi: 'and they ate' means they appeared to eat, and from this one learns not to change the custom of the house (Bava Metzia 87a). The English keeps the plain 'they ate', as Ibn Ezra does on 18:1.

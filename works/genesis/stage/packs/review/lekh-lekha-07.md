Grammar notes:
- The verse is a long apposition continuing verse 8: the four kings are listed with their lands, each as 'king of X', and the list ends with the totals 'four kings against the five'.
- אֵת is the preposition 'with' (compare עִם): Ibn Ezra glosses אֵת כְּדָרְלָעֹמֶר as 'with'. It is not the object marker here.
- The accents set each name-and-land pair apart: geresh/revia after Elam, pashta/zaqef qaton for the next two pairs, tipha and etnahta at Arioch and Ellasar; the merkha-tipha pair carries 'four kings' and silluq closes 'the five'.
- The numerals contrast four against five; the word order 'four kings against the five' sets the few against the many.

Translator's note: The pack parses the second אֶת־ (14:9.16) as the object marker; I keep that datum but note that 'against' is the sense required by the construction. Goim (גּוֹיִם) is left as a name, as the lexicon gives it; the word is also 'nations', and Onkelos translates it 'of the peoples' (דְעַמְמִין). Onkelos reads 'Babel' for Shinar.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:14:9:1` not-applicable: Rashi: the few nevertheless won, to show they were mighty, and Abram was not deterred from pursuing them. This is a moral about the numbers, not a reading of the words.
- `ibn-ezra:14:9:1` follows: Ibn Ezra glosses אֵת in אֵת כְּדָרְלָעֹמֶר as 'like עִם', that is, 'with'. The English adopts this.
- apparatus: Onkelos: 'with Chedorlaomer king of Elam, Tidal king of the peoples, Amraphel king of Babel, Arioch king of Ellasar, four kings against five.'

### Translation under review (14:10)

English: The Valley of Siddim was pit upon pit of bitumen, and the king of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, and they fell there, and the survivors fled into the hills.

Grammar notes:
- The doubled בֶּאֱרֹת בֶּאֱרֹת is idiomatic: 'pits and pits', that is, pit after pit. Both words are in the construct state, with חֵמָר 'bitumen' as the last member.
- The verb וַיָּנֻסוּ is plural although מֶלֶךְ־סְדֹם וַעֲמֹרָה looks like a single king 'of Sodom and Gomorrah'. The sense is the king of Sodom and the king of Gomorrah.
- הֶרָה and שָׁמָּה take the directional ה, a locative ending without a preposition. Rashi says הֶרָה means 'to a mountain' (indefinite), unlike הָהָרָה with the article, 'to the mountain'.
- The word order changes at the end: 'and the remaining ones, to-the-hills they fled', with the subject and place fronted to contrast with the two kings who fell.
- Zaqef qaton on חֵמָר ends the first clause; etnahta on שָׁמָּה ends the second.

Translator's note: I take חֵמָר as the lexicon gives it ('bitumen'); Rashi and Radak read 'clay, mud' for the building mortar, a reading the English does not adopt. וַיִּפְּלוּ־שָׁמָּה is kept open between 'fell into the pits' and 'threw themselves down there' (Ibn Ezra); the English line says only 'fell there'. הֶרָה is rendered 'into the hills', following Rashi's indefinite reading; Ibn Ezra's 'to the mountain' is recorded. Sforno takes the survivors to be the three remaining kings.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:14:10:1` follows: Rashi: there were many pits there from which earth was taken for building mortar; the aggadic midrash says that the mud-covered pits made a miracle for the king of Sodom, who came out of them. The English takes his 'many pits' but keeps the lexicon's 'bitumen' for the material.
- `rashi:14:10:2` follows: Rashi: הֶרָה is like לְהָר, 'to a mountain', with the ה replacing the לָמֶד at the front; it names no particular mountain, since each fled to whichever he found first, unlike הָהָרָה, which means a known mountain. The English adopts the indefinite reading.
- `ibn-ezra:14:10:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: בְּאֵר throughout Scripture means a flowing well, and its form here is altered because of the construct. The English reads the form as the construct plural 'pits of'.
- `ibn-ezra:14:10:2` follows: Ibn Ezra: וַיִּפְּלוּ שָׁמָּה means they fell there in their wish to escape, as in 'and he fell on his face' (Numbers 16:4). The English line keeps the neutral 'fell there'.
- `ibn-ezra:14:10:3` records: Ibn Ezra: הֶרָה means 'to the mountain' and is a strange word. The English instead follows Rashi's indefinite 'into the hills'; Ibn Ezra's definite reading is kept here.
- apparatus: WLC reads הַשִׂדִּים with a different vowel pointing; MAM reads הַשִּׂדִּים (14:10.2).
- apparatus: Onkelos: 'the plain of the field was pits, pits of clay; the king of Sodom and Gomorrah fled and fell there, and those who remained fled to the mountain.'

Grammar notes:
- וְהוּא שֹׁכֵן is a circumstantial clause (pronoun plus participle) about Abram.
- 'Brother of Eshcol and brother of Aner' stands in apposition to מַמְרֵא; the accents (revia, then mahpakh and pashta) attach it to Mamre, not to Abram.
- בַּעֲלֵי בְרִית is an idiom: 'masters of a covenant', that is, allies. The same construction pairs a person with a thing owned.
- הַפָּלִיט has the article: 'the fugitive', one particular person known to the story; הָעִבְרִי is its only appearance as a title for Abram.
- Etnahta on הָעִבְרִי separates the report from the circumstantial clause that follows.

Translator's note: Rashi's plain-sense identification of the fugitive as Og is recorded, not adopted in the English line. הָעִבְרִי is rendered 'the Hebrew'; Rashi's 'from across the river' is a gloss on the name, not an alternative translation. 'Eshcol' has a sense in its entry for a valley; here it is a personal name.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:14:13:1` records: Rashi: on the plain sense this is Og, who escaped the war (Deuteronomy 3), since Amraphel and his allies did not kill him when they struck the Rephaim at Ashteroth-karnaim. The English says only 'the fugitive'.
- `rashi:14:13:2` follows: Rashi, after Genesis Rabbah: הָעִבְרִי means one who came from across the river. The English keeps 'the Hebrew', which can carry that sense.
- `rashi:14:13:3` follows: Rashi: they had made a covenant with him. The English renders בַּעֲלֵי בְרִית as 'allies'.
- `ibn-ezra:14:13:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: הַפָּלִיט is one who ran away and escaped from the people of Sodom, as in 'the fugitive came to me from Jerusalem' (Ezekiel 33:21); there is also a midrashic way. The English uses 'the fugitive'.

Grammar notes:
- וַיִּשְׁמַע ... כִּי introduces the content of what Abram heard: 'that his brother was taken captive'. The Niphal נִשְׁבָּה is passive.
- וַיָּרֶק is Hiphil of רִיק: 'he emptied out'. It is used of drawing a sword (Leviticus 26:33) and so 'armed' is the usual sense; the object is 'his trained men',.
- חֲנִיכָיו is the Qere reading; Rashi quotes the Ketiv חנכו and reads it as 'initiated, trained'. The root חנך is that of 'dedicate, initiate'.
- The numeral 'eighteen' is written 'eight ten' and 'three hundred' with the feminine three and the plural 'hundreds'; the construction gives 'three hundred' only in the second half.
- Etnahta on אָחִיו divides the verse into the news and Abram's response.

Translator's note: For וַיָּרֶק I choose 'armed' on the combined view of Rashi (equipped, ready, like Onkelos וְזָרִיז) and Ibn Ezra's first reading ('gave them weapons'); his second reading, 'drew out the swords from the sheath', is kept in the commentary entry. The Ketiv of חניכיו is recorded in the apparatus. Rashi's midrash that the three hundred and eighteen are Eliezer alone is not adopted, and Rashi's remark on Dan (the later calf-idol) is not a reading of the words.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:14:14:1` follows: Rashi: וַיָּרֶק is 'ready', as the Targum has it (וְזָרִיז), like 'I will draw the sword after you' (Leviticus 26:33), meaning 'arm with my sword'. The English takes 'armed'.
- `rashi:14:14:2` follows: Rashi: the Ketiv is חנכו and the Qere חניכיו; the word is Eliezer, whom he trained in the commandments, and the root means initiating a person into a craft or duty, as in 'train up a child' (Proverbs 22:6). The English renders 'trained men'.
- `rashi:14:14:3` records: Rashi: our rabbis said it was Eliezer alone, which is the numerical value of his name. The English takes the number at face value; the midrash is kept here.
- `rashi:14:14:4` not-applicable: Rashi: there his strength failed, since he saw that his descendants would set up a calf there. This is a homily on the place name, not a reading of the words.
- `ibn-ezra:14:14:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: he gave them weapons of war, as in 'and draw out the spear' (Psalm 35:3); some say he drew the sword from its sheath, as in 'they empty their sacks'. The English adopts the first and records the second.
- `ibn-ezra:14:14:2` follows: Ibn Ezra: חֲנִיכָיו are those he had trained many times in battle; the gematria of Eliezer is only a homiletic way and the text speaks in its plain sense. The English adopts 'trained men'.
- apparatus: Ketiv חנכו, Qere חֲנִיכָיו (14:14.8), as Rashi notes.

Grammar notes:
- The singular verb וַיֵּחָלֵק is followed by הוּא וַעֲבָדָיו: the subject 'he and his servants' comes after the verb and the adverb 'by night', which is why Rashi says the verse is transposed.
- וַיִּרְדְּפֵם and וַיַּכֵּם have the suffix מ, 'them': the verbs are wayyiqtols and the verb 'pursue' is repeated from verse 14.
- מִשְּׂמֹאל לְדַמָּשֶׂק is 'from the left of Damascus' and names the north, taken from the person facing east.
- The qadma and darga lead into the tevir on לַיְלָה, which sets 'by night' apart from the pronoun that follows.

Translator's note: I follow Rashi's plain-sense reordering: 'he and his servants divided themselves against them by night', as pursuers split up after fugitives who scatter. His aggadic reading, that the night itself was divided in two, is not adopted. For מִשְּׂמֹאל I write 'north of', the sense 3 in the entry. Rashi's remark that no place is called Hobah and that Dan is meant is a midrash and is not adopted; the English keeps the name Hobah.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:14:15:1` follows: Rashi: on the plain sense the verse is transposed: 'he and his servants divided themselves against them at night', as pursuers split into parties after fugitives who flee in different directions. The English adopts this order.
- `rashi:14:15:2` records: Rashi: after dark he did not hold back from pursuing them; the aggadic midrash says the night was divided, with a miracle in the first half, and the second half was kept for the midnight in Egypt. The English does not adopt the midrash.
- `rashi:14:15:3` not-applicable: Rashi: no place is named Hobah; Dan is called Hobah because of the idolatry that would be there. This is a homily and the English keeps the name.

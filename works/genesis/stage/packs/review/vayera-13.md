### Translation under review (21:7)

English: Sarah said, "Who would have said to Abraham that Sarah has nursed children? For I have borne a son in his old age."

Literal: and-said who spoke to-Abraham has-nursed children Sarah for I-have-borne son to-his-old-age

| token | gloss | lex | sense | note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21:7.1 | and she said |  | Qal 1 | Wayyiqtol of אָמַר, third feminine singular: Sarah is the speaker, and the speech that follows is hers. |
| 21:7.2 | who |  | a | The interrogative מִי, used of a person. The clause is a question of wonder, not a request for information. |
| 21:7.3 | would have said |  |  | Piel perfect of מָלַל, third masculine singular. In a question with מִי the perfect is read as a rhetorical perfect: no one would have said such a thing. |
| 21:7.4 | to Abraham |  |  |  |
| 21:7.5 | that Sarah has nursed |  | Hiph | Hifil perfect of יָנַק, third feminine singular: the causative 'to give suck'. It is the content of the question. |
| 21:7.6 | children |  | 2 | The plural of בֵּן in the sense 'children'. The plural is explained in different ways in the commentary. |
| 21:7.7 | Sarah |  |  |  |
| 21:7.8 | for |  | 3 | כִּי introduces the reason and the proof: she has borne a son herself. |
| 21:7.9 | I have borne |  | Qal 1 | Qal perfect of יָלַד, first common singular. Of a mother the verb is 'bear, bring forth' (Qal 1), not 'beget' (Qal 2). |
| 21:7.10 | a son |  | 1 |  |
| 21:7.11 | in his old age |  |  | לִ with זְקֻנִים, a plural noun with the third masculine singular suffix: 'to his old ages', idiomatically 'in his old age'. The noun has no senses in the pack. |

Phrases:
- 21:7.1 | Sarah said | and-she-said: the wayyiqtol opens the speech
- 21:7.2 21:7.3 21:7.4 | Who would have said to Abraham | who spoke to-Abraham: an interrogative clause, read as a question of wonder
- 21:7.5 21:7.6 21:7.7 | that Sarah has nursed children? | has-nursed children Sarah: 'that' supplies the clause that the verb of saying takes
- 21:7.8 21:7.9 21:7.10 21:7.11 | For I have borne a son in his old age. | for I-have-borne son to-his-old-age: the reason, in the first person

Grammar notes:
- The speech of verse 7 is Sarah's. The wayyiqtol וַתֹּאמֶר (21:7.1) introduces it, and the first clause is a question of wonder: the interrogative מִי and the perfect מִלֵּל ask who could have said such a thing.
- הֵינִיקָה (21:7.5) is a hifil perfect of יָנַק, 'to give suck'. In the first clause Sarah speaks of herself in the third person; in the second, with כִּי, she speaks as 'I'.
- The clause כִּי יָלַדְתִּי בֵן לִזְקֻנָיו (21:7.8–11) gives the reason and the proof. The bearing is of a son of her own, the same act that the question sets against the nursing.
- The etnaḥta on שָׂרָה (21:7.7) divides the question from the reason that follows it.
- בָּנִים is plural. The English keeps 'children'; why the plural is used is disputed (see the commentary).

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:21:7:1` records: Rashi reads מִי as a word of praise: 'see who he is, and how great is the One who keeps His promise and fulfils it'. The English reads a question of wonder and does not adopt the praise sense; it is kept here.
- `rashi:21:7:2` declines: Rashi notes that the verse uses מִלֵּל and not דִּבֵּר, and that its numerical value is 100, meaning 'at the end of a hundred years for Abraham'. This is a homiletic reading of the letters, not the sense of the verb, which the English renders as 'said'.
- `rashi:21:7:3` records: Rashi asks why 'children' is plural: at the feast the women of rank brought their own sons, and Sarah nursed them, because they said she had not borne a son but had taken in a foundling from the market.
- `ramban:21:7:1` follows: Ramban first gives Rashi's praise sense, then holds that the question means that no one would have said to Abraham, even to comfort him, that Sarah would nurse children, since it never came into anyone's heart. The English follows this sense of the question.
- `radak:21:7:2` follows: Radak takes the question the same way: who would have spoken to Abraham that Sarah nurses children, so new a thing, were it not that God had done a wonder with him in giving him a son in his old age.
- `radak:21:7:4` records: Radak reads 'children' as nursing children in general, and allows that she nursed her own son and might nurse other children later. He also gives the midrashic reading that the women, doubting her, brought their sons, and she nursed them.
- `onkelos:21:7:1` records: Onkelos renders the question as 'Who would believe him who said to Abraham that Sarah would nurse sons?', reading it as a question of belief rather than of who spoke, and renders 'children' as 'sons'.
- apparatus: The sense of מִי is open: a question of wonder (the English) or a word of praise (Rashi, and Ramban's first reading).

### Translation under review (21:8)

English: The child grew up and was weaned, and Abraham made a great feast on the day Isaac was weaned.

Literal: and-grew the-child and-was-weaned and-made Abraham feast great in-day-of being-weaned (object marker) Isaac

Grammar notes:
- Three wayyiqtols (21:8.1, .3, .4) carry the narrative in sequence: the child grows and is weaned, and then Abraham makes the feast.
- The infinitive construct הִגָּמֵל (21:8.9) has Isaac as its subject, marked with the object marker (21:8.10–11). The literal is 'on the day of Isaac's being weaned'; the English reads the infinitive as a clause, 'the day Isaac was weaned'.
- The adjective גָּדוֹל follows מִשְׁתֶּה, as adjectives do in Hebrew; the English puts 'great' before the noun.
- The etnaḥta on וַיִּגָּמַל (21:8.3) divides the verse: the child's story ends with the weaning, and the feast begins the second clause.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:21:8:1` records: Rashi gives the weaning as the end of twenty-four months. The verse gives no age, and the English states none.
- `rashi:21:8:2` declines: Rashi, from Bereshit Rabbah, says the guests at the feast were the great men of the generation, Shem, Eber and Abimelech. This is midrash, not the plain sense of 'a great feast', and the English does not adopt it.
- `radak:21:8:1` records: Radak says the nursing ended twenty-four months after birth, the usual time for a child to nurse. The English gives no age.
- `radak:21:8:2` not-applicable: Radak gives the reason for the feast: either it was the custom of the time, or Abraham made it for joy in Isaac; a child who has finished nursing begins to speak and is taught. This is an explanation, not a reading of the words.
- `onkelos:21:8:1` records: Onkelos renders the feast as 'a great drinking' (mishtiyā rabbā) and the child as 'the boy' (ravyā). The English keeps 'feast'.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:21:9:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: this is the usual play of a boy, and Sarah envied him for being large of build. The English 'playing' follows him. The idolatry, bloodshed and illicit sex that Rashi and Ramban find in the word are not taken here.
- `rashi:21:9:1` declines: Rashi lists three senses of the word from other verses (idolatry, bloodshed, illicit sex), and then reads it as a quarrel with Isaac over the inheritance, the son claiming the firstborn's double portion and shooting arrows at Isaac in the field. The English declines these: the verse says only 'playing', and the quarrel is not in the words.
- `ramban:21:9:1` records: Ramban gives Rashi's inheritance quarrel and the three senses, and cites a Tosefta in which Rabbi Akiva reads the word as idolatry. Ramban himself holds that the word here means only a claim to the inheritance. The English does not adopt these readings.
- `radak:21:9:1` records: Radak: as if he mocked Isaac, who was born to the old. The English keeps 'playing' and does not say whom the boy mocked.
- `sforno:21:9:1` not-applicable: Sforno gives Sarah's thought: she supposed the mockery came from what the boy had heard from his mother, as the saying goes that a child's talk in the street is what he hears at home. This is an explanation of the cause, not a reading of the word.
- `sforno:21:9:2` records: Sforno reads the mockery as aimed at the feast in Abraham's house, the boy saying that Sarah had conceived by Abimelech. He heard such talk later from the scoffers of the time, and if he had mocked at Isaac's birth, Sarah, busy then, did not notice.
- `onkelos:21:9:1` records: Onkelos renders the participle as 'laughing' (mechayyekh). The English keeps 'playing', as Ibn Ezra does.
- apparatus: The piel מְצַחֵק: 'playing' (Pi 2, the English, on Ibn Ezra's reading), 'jest' (Pi 1), 'mocking' (Rashi, Radak, and Sforno's second reading), 'laughing' (Onkelos). The choice rests on the normative commentator, Ibn Ezra.

English: The matter was very displeasing in Abraham's eyes concerning his son.

Grammar notes:
- The verb is a wayyiqtol with a noun subject, 'the matter'. The order is verb, subject, adverb: 'was displeasing, the matter, very'.
- 'In Abraham's eyes' is an idiom for what seems good or bad to a person. The phrase is the plural construct of עַיִן with ב.
- The noun אוֹדֹת stands with עַל. Its sense is taken from דָּבָר, 'matter' (Ibn Ezra); the English 'concerning his son' keeps that sense.
- The etnaḥta on אַבְרָהָם (21:11.5) divides the clause before 'concerning his son'.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:21:11:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: אוֹדוֹת is 'like' דָּבָר, a matter or affair, so 'concerning his son' means 'the matter of his son'. The English adopts this reading.
- `rashi:21:11:1` records: Rashi: the matter is that Ishmael had gone into bad company (Bereshit Rabbah); the plain sense, he says, is that the matter concerned Sarah's request that he be sent away. The English leaves the matter as the verse states it.
- `ramban:21:11:1` records: Ramban: the verse honours Abraham. He was not greatly displeased for love of the maidservant, and he would have done Sarah's will had she asked only for the maidservant to be sent away; he was grieved for his son. God told him not to be displeased over the boy or the maidservant at all.
- `radak:21:11:1` records: Radak: Abraham loved Ishmael as his firstborn, had taught him the way of the LORD, and was grieved to send him from home. He bore his wife's quarrel until the word came to him.
- `onkelos:21:11:1` records: Onkelos renders the matter as 'the word' (pitgamā) and keeps the sense of its being grievous to Abraham.
- apparatus: The lexicon gives the root of אוֹדָה as 'cause, circumstance'. Ibn Ezra's 'matter' (דָּבָר) is the reading the English follows.

Grammar notes:
- The verse is a sequence of wayyiqtols (21:14.1, .4, .8, .16, .17, .18): an early start, the provision, the dismissal, and the wandering.
- The participle שָׂם (21:14.11) is a subordinate act: 'gave, putting on her shoulder'. The bread and the skin are the things placed.
- 'And the boy' (21:14.14–15) is ambiguous. It may be the object of 'gave' (Ramban, Sforno) or a thing placed on her shoulder with the bread and the skin (Rashi). The English keeps both readings open.
- The etnaḥta on וַֽיְשַׁלְּחֶ֑הָ (21:14.16) closes the dismissal and divides the verse before the wandering.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:21:14:1` records: Rashi gives the reason for bread and water rather than silver and gold: Abraham hated the boy for going out into bad company. The English gives the list without a motive, and Rashi's motive is recorded here.
- `rashi:21:14:2` records: Rashi reads 'and the boy' as also placed on her shoulder, the grammatical reading, and adds a midrash (Bereshit Rabbah): Sarah had cast an evil eye on the boy, and the heat seized him so that he could not walk. The midrash is not the plain sense; the English keeps 'and the boy' open between the readings.
- `rashi:21:14:3` declines: Rashi, from Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, says that 'she wandered' means she went back to the idols of her father's house. The English gives the plain sense of wandering, and the idols are not in the words.
- `ibn-ezra:21:14:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: the חֵמָה is a vessel, which may be of skin or of wood (cf. Habakkuk 2:15, מְסַפֵּחַ חֲמָתְךָ). The English follows the vessel and takes it as a skin, the material the lexicon gives. His remark on those who wonder at Abraham's obedience is not a reading of the words.
- `ibn-ezra:21:14:2` follows: Ibn Ezra: the bread and the water were enough to reach Beersheba, for Abraham was then in Gerar. She went and did not know the way, and wandered. The English follows this. He says that silver and gold may have been given, but the verse does not say so, and the English does not say it.
- `ramban:21:14:1` records: Ramban: 'and the boy' goes back to 'gave': he gave to Hagar, and also the boy, so that the boy went with her wherever she went.
- `radak:21:14:1` not-applicable: Radak: Abraham rose early to do God's command. This is a reason, not a reading of the words.
- `radak:21:14:2` records: Radak: he gave food for a day or two, and water. She was going back toward Egypt or to her kin, since she was an Egyptian. He also gave silver and gold, though the verse does not say so. The English states only the bread and the water.
- `radak:21:14:4` records: Radak: the boy was fifteen and walked ahead of her; when he tired she carried him on her shoulder or in her lap. The English gives no age and no order of walking.
- `radak:21:14:5` records: Radak: 'sent her away' means that he sent her from his house, or that he escorted her out of the city. The English keeps the verb's plain sense, 'sent her away'.
- `radak:21:14:6` not-applicable: Radak: she did not know the way once she had entered the wilderness. This explains why she wandered.
- `sforno:21:14:2` records: Sforno: the skin of water is given as a mark of her status as a maidservant (cf. 16:9), but the righteous man did not withhold what they needed. After they strayed, the only thing they lacked was water.
- `sforno:21:14:3` records: Sforno: he also gave her the boy. 'And the boy' is the object of 'gave'.
- `sforno:21:14:4` records: Sforno: he 'escorted' her out of his great kindness, as in 'and Abraham went with them to send them' (18:16). The English keeps 'sent her away'.
- `onkelos:21:14:1` follows: Onkelos renders the verse the same way: bread, a skin of water (raqbā), placed on her shoulder with the boy, and she went and strayed in the wilderness of Beersheba. The English follows.
- apparatus: חֵמָה: the pack lists 'waterskin' (BDB); Ibn Ezra says 'a vessel, perhaps of skin or of wood'. The English gives a skin.
- apparatus: 'And the boy' (21:14.14–15): object of the giving (Ramban, Sforno) or of the placing (Rashi). The English keeps both open.

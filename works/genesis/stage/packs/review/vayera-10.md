### Translation under review (20:1)

English: Abraham journeyed from there toward the land of the Negev, and he dwelt between Kadesh and between Shur, and he sojourned in Gerar.

Literal: and-journeyed from-there Abraham to-land the-Negev and-dwelt between Kadesh and-between Shur and-sojourned in-Gerar

| token | gloss | lex | sense | note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20:1.1 | and journeyed |  | Qal 3 | wayyiqtol of נָסַע; 'journey, march' fits a move toward a region. |
| 20:1.2 | from there |  |  | The text does not name the place 'there'; 18:1 places Abraham at Mamre. |
| 20:1.3 | Abraham |  |  |  |

Phrases:
- 20:1.1 20:1.2 20:1.3 | Abraham journeyed from there | and-journeyed from-there Abraham: the verb leads the clause in Hebrew; the subject follows it
- 20:1.4 20:1.5 | toward the land of the Negev | to-land the-Negev: the directional he gives 'toward'
- 20:1.6 20:1.7 20:1.8 | he dwelt between Kadesh | and-dwelt between Kadesh
- 20:1.9 20:1.10 | and between Shur | and-between Shur: the preposition repeated before the second place
- 20:1.11 20:1.12 | and he sojourned in Gerar | and-sojourned in-Gerar

Grammar notes:
- Three wayyiqtol verbs (וַיִּסַּע, וַיֵּשֶׁב, וַיָּגָר) carry the narrative in sequence: a move, a stay, and a longer residence.
- The repeated preposition in בֵּין־קָדֵשׁ וּבֵין שׁוּר gives two separate points, with Kadesh and Shur as the ends of the place where Abraham dwelt.
- The accents divide the verse at the etnaḥta on שׁ֑וּר, so the first half gives the journey and the stay and the second gives the residence in Gerar.
- The place-names carry no article: קָדֵשׁ, שׁוּר and גְּרָר are proper names; the lexicon's first sense for קָדֵשׁ is the Kadesh of the south.

Translator's note: The narrative of 20:1 moves without comment on the reason for the journey; the commentators supply motives (see commentary), which are not in the English.

Commentary rulings:
- `radak:20:1:2` follows: Radak: the Negev is the southern part of the land of Israel, since the inheritance of Judah lay to the south. The English keeps 'the land of the Negev' as a part of the land.
- `radak:20:1:1` records: Radak takes 'from there' as the oaks of Mamre, where Abraham lived, and suggests he went into the land of the Philistines, which is within the land God gave him. The English keeps 'from there' and does not name the place.
- `radak:20:1:3` records: Radak: 'dwelt' and 'sojourned' are one matter restated in a doubled clause; Abraham may first have dwelt by the well of Lahai, between Kadesh and Shur, and then come to Gerar. The English keeps the two verbs apart as the Hebrew does.
- `sforno:20:1:1` records: Sforno reads Kadesh and Shur as two great cities, and says Abraham settled between them to call on the name of the LORD, El Olam. The English takes both as places and adds nothing about cities.
- `onkelos:20:1:1` records: Targum Onkelos renders Kadesh as Rekem and Shur as Hagra, and keeps Gerar. The English keeps the Hebrew names, since those identifications are not settled.
- `rashi:20:1:1` not-applicable: Rashi gives a motive for leaving: the towns were ruined and travellers stopped, and Abraham moved away because of the bad name Lot had after his deeds with his daughters. This is a reason for the move, not the sense of the words.

### Translation under review (20:2)

English: Abraham said of Sarah his wife, "She is my sister." Abimelech king of Gerar sent and took Sarah.

Phrases:
- 20:2.1 20:2.2 | Abraham said | and-said Abraham
- 20:2.3 20:2.4 20:2.5 | of Sarah his wife | concerning Sarah his-wife: Ibn Ezra's 'like על'
- 20:2.6 20:2.7 | She is my sister | my-sister she: the subject follows the predicate; no copula in Hebrew
- 20:2.8 20:2.9 20:2.10 20:2.11 | Abimelech king of Gerar sent | and-sent Abimelech king of-Gerar: the subject is placed before the verb in English
- 20:2.12 20:2.13 20:2.14 | and took Sarah | and-took (object) Sarah

Grammar notes:
- Three wayyiqtol verbs (וַיֹּאמֶר, וַיִּשְׁלַח, וַיִּקַּח) carry the narrative. Abraham's words are set without quotation marks in the Hebrew.
- The statement אֲחֹתִי הִוא has no verb: the feminine pronoun agrees with Sarah, and the English supplies 'is'.
- The etnaḥta on הִ֑וא ends Abraham's words, and the revia on וַיִּשְׁלַ֗ח begins the report of the king's act.
- The object marker אֶת before שָׂרָה has no English counterpart, so it is given as '-' in the phrase.

Translator's note: The preposition אֶל before a person is read by Ibn Ezra as 'concerning', like על, and the English takes that reading. The name of Sarah's relation is Abraham's word; the English keeps the statement in the form the Hebrew gives it.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:20:2:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: אל here means על, 'about, concerning', as in 1 Samuel 1:27 'concerning this boy' (אֶל הַנַּעַר הַזֶּה). So Abraham said of Sarah his wife that she was his sister.
- `rashi:20:2:2` follows: Rashi: אל means על here, as in the two parallel uses in 1 Samuel 4 (the taking of the ark and the death of her father-in-law). The English agrees.
- `rashi:20:2:1` records: Rashi on 'and Abraham said': he did not ask her leave, and spoke against her will and not for her good, since she had already been taken into Pharaoh's house. The English does not say whether she consented.
- `radak:20:2:1` follows: Radak: he said this of Sarah his wife, in answer to those who asked him about her; the preposition marks the subject of the question.
- `radak:20:2:3` follows: Radak: אל stands for על, as in 1 Samuel 17:49. He said this on coming to Gerar, because he judged the people there wicked, as Abimelech's own words in 20:11 show.
- `ramban:20:2:1` records: Ramban: this was not as in Egypt. The king of Gerar was honest and upright, and his men were good; Abraham suspected them without cause and said to all, 'She is my sister.'
- `radak:20:2:4` records: Radak on 'and sent': after hearing of her beauty, the king had her brought and took her as his wife. The English 'took' keeps the verb open.
- `onkelos:20:2:1` follows: Targum Onkelos renders the phrase as 'concerning Sarah his wife', taking the preposition as 'about'. The English agrees.

Grammar notes:
- The clause opens with the verb (וַיָּבֹא), then its subject, then the preposition to the king: the English order changes only the placing of the subject.
- The etnaḥta on הַלָּ֑יְלָה ends the clause of the dream. The dream is בַּחֲלוֹם הַלָּיְלָה: 'in a dream of the night'.
- הִנְּךָ מֵת: a demonstrative with a participle gives the present state, 'you are as one dead'.
- The relative clause אֲשֶׁר לָקַחְתָּ refers back to הָאִשָּׁה; the qatal names the act. The last clause, וְהִוא בְּעֻלַת בָּעַל, explains why the taking is a wrong.

Translator's note: The subject is the plural אֱלֹהִים with a singular verb. The Hebrew allows 'God' or a divine messenger; Ibn Ezra reads the messenger (המלאך), and by the rule that the normative commentator decides among the readings the Hebrew allows, the English follows him. The honour and the contrast with Pharaoh that he gives are commentary and stay out of the English.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:20:3:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: the angel came in a dream to Abimelech for the honour of Abraham. The word for 'taking' (לקיחה) is used here as with Pharaoh, but Abimelech was not struck as Pharaoh was, since he was more righteous than Pharaoh (כִּי צַדִּיק הָיָה מִפַּרְעֹה). The English takes the angel as the subject; the honour and the contrast stay in the commentary.
- `radak:20:3:1` follows: Radak: a prophetic dream comes to the nations for the sake of the righteous, but Pharaoh did not deserve one, and messengers (שְׁלוּחִין) from God came to him. The messenger reading finds support here.
- `radak:20:3:2` follows: Radak: she is forbidden to Abimelech because a man must not take another man's wife, which the sons of Noah were also bound by. The English 'belongs to a husband' states the same prohibition.
- `radak:20:3:3` records: Radak: the text does not say אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ; the words בְּעֻלַת בָּעַל mean a woman who has been had by her husband, and a betrothed woman is not liable until she has been had by the betrothed. The English 'belongs to a husband' leaves this open.
- `sforno:20:3:1` records: Sforno: as with Laban and Balaam, no appearance of God is described, only a voice (קוֹל) came to him. The English follows the messenger reading and does not say whether a voice or a figure came.
- `sforno:20:3:2` records: Sforno reads הִנְּךָ מֵת as 'you are going and being consumed by the illness that began in you', so the death is in progress and not yet complete. The English gives the state of a dead man.
- `onkelos:20:3:1` records: Targum Onkelos makes the subject 'a word (memra) from before the LORD' that came to Abimelech in a dream. The English keeps the Hebrew subject, as Ibn Ezra reads it.
- apparatus: The subject of 20:3 is the plural אֱלֹהִים with a singular verb. The English reads an angel of God on Ibn Ezra's ruling; in 20:6 the same speaker is 'God' (הָאֱלֹהִים), as the decisions set for that verse. The two are not reconciled in the English, and the editor may wish to review them together.

Grammar notes:
- The qatal לֹא קָרַב states a completed negative act; the wayyiqtol וַיֹּאמַר then takes up the narrative.
- The interrogative הֲ with גּוֹי makes the whole of 20:4 a question; גַּם צַדִּיק is concessive, 'even a righteous one'.
- The zaqef gadol on וַאֲבִימֶ֕לֶךְ and on אֲדֹנָ֕י marks the two halves: the king's refusal, and his speech.
- The speech is addressed to God, and the 2ms yiqtol תַּהֲרֹג has God as its subject: the English 'will you kill' keeps the addressee.

Translator's note: The vav of וַאֲבִימֶלֶךְ marks contrast with the man in danger: the king had not come near her. Ibn Ezra's reading of הַגּוֹי as the nation (not 'man') decides the English 'a nation'. His motives for the king's plea are commentary.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:20:4:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: the sense of 'the nation, also the righteous, you would kill' is 'on behalf of his soul, his house and his kingdom' (בְּעַד נַפְשׁוֹ וּבֵיתוֹ וּמַמְלַכְתּוֹ). He takes הַגּוֹי as the nation, and warns against the reading that makes גּוֹי 'man', which changes one word for another. The English takes 'a nation'.
- `radak:20:4:2` follows: Radak: 'if you kill me, it is as if my people die with me, since I am a king who leads them in justice'. He calls himself righteous, since he took her in good faith and was not guilty of a capital crime. The English 'a nation' and 'a righteous one' match this.
- `onkelos:20:4:1` follows: Targum Onkelos renders הַגּוֹי as 'the people' (הֲעָם), so 'will you kill the people, even a righteous one?' The English agrees on 'a nation'.
- `sforno:20:4:1` follows: Sforno: is it fitting that the nation be destroyed by killing its king, and that a righteous king be killed for what he did not do? He reads גּוֹי as the people; the English follows.
- `rashi:20:4:1` records: Rashi: the angel held him back, as God says, 'I did not let you touch her.' The English says only that he had not approached her; who held him back is not in the words.
- `rashi:20:4:2` records: Rashi, in his midrash: 'even if he is righteous, will you kill him? Is it your way to destroy nations for nothing? You did so to the generation of the flood and to the generation of the Dispersion.' This is an objection, not the plain sense; it is kept because a reader may take it.
- `radak:20:4:1` not-applicable: Radak: the desire was held back by God's will. This is a reason for the verb, not its sense.
- apparatus: אֲדֹנָי with qamats: the pack has no Ibn Ezra comment on this word. 'My Lord' is taken as address to God, the speaker of the dream. The other reading, 'my lord' for a man, is recorded here; it would make the king address the messenger.

Grammar notes:
- The sequence is qatal (ידעתי), wayyiqtol (ואחשך), qatal (נתתיך): knowledge, the restraint that followed, and the consequence in the last clause.
- The infinitive לִנְגֹּעַ has the ל prefix and follows the negative qatal: 'did not let you (touch)'.
- The form מֵחֲטוֹ is an infinitive construct of the lamed-alef root חטא, with a vav for the alef (Radak).
- The silluq on אֵלֶֽיהָ ends the speech; the darga on וָאֶחְשֹׂ֧ךְ marks the restraint as the main clause.

Translator's note: The speaker is God (הָאֱלֹהִים), as the decisions set for this verse. The speaker of 20:3 is called 'an angel of God' on Ibn Ezra's reading, so the two verses differ in the English; see the apparatus. The 'letting' of 20:6 translates נתן with 'give' (leave), and Rashi's reading 'I did not give you the power' is adopted.

Commentary rulings:
- `ibn-ezra:20:6:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: לנגוע is a verbal noun (שֵׁם פֹּעַל), like לנטוע (Isaiah 51:16) and לטעת (Ecclesiastes 3:2), and all such forms are of this kind. The English 'to touch' keeps the infinitive, which does not change the sense of the word.
- `rashi:20:6:2` follows: Rashi: 'I did not let you' means 'it was not from you that you did not touch her; I held you back and did not give you the power' (כֹּחַ). The English 'did not let you' takes נתן in this sense of giving leave.
- `rashi:20:6:1` records: Rashi: 'you did not sin from the start, but cleanness of hands is not here', because the taking was done. The English keeps 'integrity of heart' and says nothing of the hands in this verse.
- `radak:20:6:2` follows: Radak: God knew the sin, since he had commanded Noah's sons about another man's wife; the sin is against God, so 'against me' is the right sense.
- `radak:20:6:3` follows: Radak: the vav stands in place of the alef of the lamed-alef verb in מֵחֲטוֹ. The English reads the infinitive of 'sin' as the form requires.
- `radak:20:6:6` follows: Radak: לִנְגֹּעַ covers any touch short of intercourse, kissing and embracing included, so God kept the king from all intimacy. The English 'touch' keeps this breadth.
- `onkelos:20:6:1` records: Targum Onkelos: 'I also withheld you from sinning before me' (קָדָמַי, in my sight). The English 'against me' keeps the Hebrew dative; Onkelos's 'before me' is a near reading.
- `radak:20:6:1` not-applicable: Radak: the text does not say 'cleanness of hands', because the heart is known only to God. A reason for the wording, not a sense of the words.
- `radak:20:6:4` not-applicable: Radak: God held the king back from desire, and this was a kindness done in this way. A reason for the 'therefore', not a sense of the words.
- apparatus: The speaker of 20:3 is 'an angel of God' on Ibn Ezra's reading, and the speaker of 20:6 is 'God' by the decision for that verse. The English does not harmonize them; a reader meets the difference that the Hebrew allows.

Grammar notes:
- The imperative הָשֵׁב and the yiqtol וְיִתְפַּלֵּל lead to the promise of the imperative וֶחְיֵה: the clause of consequence.
- The clause כִּי נָבִיא הוּא gives the reason for the command: he is a prophet, and so his prayer will work.
- The conditional וְאִם־אֵֽינְךָ מֵשִׁיב is an open condition; the participle מֵשִׁיב is a present or future.
- The infinitive absolute מוֹת before the yiqtol תָּמוּת gives emphasis: 'you will surely die'. The etnaḥta on וֶֽחְיֵ֑ה ends the first part.

Commentary rulings:
- `onkelos:20:7:1` follows: Targum Onkelos renders the command as 'return the man's wife' (אֲתֵיב אִתַּת גַּבְרָא). The English agrees.
- `radak:20:7:1` not-applicable: Radak: God will not forgive until the prophet prays, so Abimelech must make his peace with Abraham even though he sinned in good faith. A reason for the prayer, not a sense of the words.
- `radak:20:7:2` records: Radak: וֶחְיֵה means 'be healed' (וְתֵרָפֵא), as in 'until they were healed' (Joshua 5:8) and 'he recovered' (Isaiah 38:21). The English 'you will live' keeps the basic sense.
- `sforno:20:7:1` records: Sforno: 'now' means before you are consumed by the illness. The English gives 'now' and says nothing of the illness.
- `sforno:20:7:2` records: Sforno: 'live' means 'be healed', as in 'until they were healed' (Joshua 5:8). The English keeps 'live'.
- `sforno:20:7:3` records: Sforno: 'all that is yours' means the children in the wombs of your wife and of your maidservants. The English keeps the general phrase.

English: Abimelech rose early in the morning and called all his servants and told all these words in their hearing, and the men were greatly afraid.

Literal: and-rose-early Abimelech in-the-morning and-called to-all his-servants and-told (object) all the-words these in their-ears and-feared the-men greatly

Grammar notes:
- The wayyiqtol forms (וַיַּשְׁכֵּם, וַיִּקְרָא, וַיְדַבֵּר, וַיִּירְאוּ) continue the story; the change of subject is at the verb for the men.
- The merkha on וַיִּירְאוּ divides the clause; the silluq on מְאֹֽד ends the verse, with the adverb as the last word.
- The verb וַיַּשְׁכֵּם, from שָׁכַם, is the idiom 'to rise early', which the English keeps.
- The noun phrase הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה has the article on both noun and demonstrative, so the demonstrative stands in the sense 'these'.

Translator's note: The idiom 'in their ears' (בְּאׇזְנֵיהֶם) is rendered 'in their hearing', which keeps the idiom. Radak's identification of the men as the servants who had praised Sarah is kept in the commentary.

Commentary rulings:
- `radak:20:8:2` records: Radak: the men are the servants who had agreed to take the woman and had praised her to the king so that he would take her. The English keeps the general word 'the men'.
- `radak:20:8:1` not-applicable: Radak: Abimelech rose early because his heart was anxious about the dream, and he would not wait for the kings to wake. A gloss on the reason, not a sense of the words.

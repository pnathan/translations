### Translation under review (13:5)

English: Also, Lot, who went with Abram, had flocks and herds and tents.

Literal: and-also to-Lot the-going with-Abram was flock-and-herd and-tents

| token | gloss | lex | sense | note |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| 13:5.2 | to Lot |  |  | the lamed marks possession |
| 13:5.3 | who went |  | Qal II.2 | article plus participle, 'the one going', a relative clause |
| 13:5.4 | with |  |  | 'et as preposition 'with', not the object marker |
| 13:5.5 | Abram |  |  |  |
| 13:5.6 | there was |  | Qal II.2.h | hayah le-, 'X had'; the verb is singular and agrees with the nearest noun |
| 13:5.7 | flocks |  |  |  |
| 13:5.8 | and herds |  |  |  |
| 13:5.9 | and tents |  | 1 | vocalised with holem on the aleph, see Ibn Ezra |

Phrases:
- 13:5.1 | Also | 'and-also'
- 13:5.2 | Lot | 'to-Lot'; the lamed marks possession
- 13:5.3 13:5.4 13:5.5 | who went with Abram | 'the-going with-Abram'
- 13:5.6 13:5.7 13:5.8 13:5.9 | had flocks and herds and tents | 'was flock-and-herd and-tents'; hayah le- is 'have'

Grammar notes:
- הָיָה with לְ expresses possession; the singular verb agrees with the first of the things possessed, צֹאן.
- הַהֹלֵךְ אֶת־אַבְרָם: participle with the article as a relative clause; אֶת here is the preposition 'with' (not the object marker).
- The etnaḥta on אַבְרָם divides the verse: the description of Lot, then what he had.
- אֹהָלִים has holem after the aleph (Ibn Ezra: singular-type pattern like גּוֹרָל, or like קֹדֶשׁ with the plural באֳהָלִים).

Translator's note: The phrase 'who went with Abram' is kept as a relative clause because it is the cause of Lot's wealth in Rashi and Radak. The verse goes on to say that the land could not hold both. The English puts the possession as 'had'.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:13:5:1` follows: Rashi asks who caused him to have this, and answers: his going with Abram. The English keeps 'who went with Abram' so that the cause is visible.
- `ibn-ezra:13:5:1` follows: Ibn Ezra takes אֹהָלִים as the plural of a singular of the qodesh pattern, comparing בָּאֳהָלִים (Jer 35:7), or else as a singular like גּוֹרָל. This concerns the form only; the sense is plain 'tents'.
- `radak:13:5:1` records: Radak: 'also' adds to what was said in 13:2, that Abram was very rich.
- `radak:13:5:2` records: Radak: Lot's good fortune was in the merit of Abram, because he went with him and obeyed him.
- `radak:13:5:3` records: Radak: Abram had many chattels and household members and needed many tents; he also discusses the vocalisation of אֹהָלִים.
- `onkelos:13:5:1` follows: Onkelos: 'and also Lot who went with Abram had sheep and oxen and dwellings', agreeing with the plain sense.
- apparatus: Onkelos renders אֹהָלִים by מַשְׁכְּנִין, 'dwellings'.

Grammar notes:
- נָשָׂא takes אֶרֶץ as a masculine subject (Radak compares נָעַם אָרֶץ in Isa 9:18); Rashi reads it as a short expression, 'the land did not bear them (its pasture)'.
- יַחְדָּו is repeated at both ends of the verse, framing the problem.
- The etnaḥta on יַחְדָּו ends the first clause; the revia' and zaqef on רָב set off the reason.

Translator's note: Rashi says the expression is short and needs 'with the pasture of the land'. The English keeps the short form 'support them' and does not supply the word. Ibn Ezra reads יַחְדָּו as related to יָחִיד, 'as one'; the English uses 'together'.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:13:6:1` follows: Rashi: the land could not provide enough pasture for their livestock; the expression is short and must be completed, 'did not bear them (the pasture of) the land', which is why the verb is masculine. The English 'support them' reflects this.
- `ibn-ezra:13:6:1` records: Ibn Ezra: יַחְדָּו is a word of both singular and plural sense (Exod 19:8), a rare form, not equal to יַחַד but like יָחִיד. The English 'together' leaves this open.
- `radak:13:6:1` records: Radak: אֶרֶץ appears here as masculine, as in נָעַם אָרֶץ (Isa 9:18).
- `radak:13:6:2` records: Radak: the pasture was too narrow for their livestock together with the livestock of the people of the land.
- `radak:13:6:3` records: Radak: therefore the herders quarrelled, each saying 'we will graze here'.
- `onkelos:13:6:1` follows: Onkelos: the land could not hold them to dwell together, for their possessions were many.
- `sforno:13:6:1` follows: Sforno: the pasture of the land was not enough for both of them, and therefore (the quarrel in verse 7).

Translator's note: Rashi: the quarrel was because Lot's herders let the animals graze in others' fields and Abram's herders reproved them for theft; this is a midrash of the Rabbis (Gen. Rabbah). Ibn Ezra reads only of the Perizzites as possibly one of Canaan's sons with two names. The English follows the plain sense of the quarrel over grazing, and records the midrashic reason.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:13:7:1` records: Rashi gives the cause of the quarrel as the wickedness of Lot's herders, who grazed in others' fields; Abram's herders reproved them, and they replied that the land was given to Abram, who has no heir, so Lot inherits; the verse says the Canaanite was still in the land. The English does not state this cause; it is kept as a midrashic reading.
- `ibn-ezra:13:7:1` follows: Ibn Ezra: the Perizzite may be one of the sons of Canaan, one of those named, with two names. The English lists the two peoples as in the text.
- `ramban:13:7:1` records: Ramban questions Rashi, since the gift was to Abram's seed, and gives a plain reading of a quarrel over pasture.
- `radak:13:7:1` not-applicable: Radak refers to his earlier comment on the Canaanite.
- `radak:13:7:2` records: Radak: the Perizzite also lived in this land, where Bethel and Ai were.

Grammar notes:
- אַל + jussive (תְהִי) is a mild prohibition; נָא softens it.
- The repeated בֵּין is distributive: me and you, my herders and your herders.
- אֲנָשִׁים אַחִים is literally 'men, brothers', an idiom for close kin (Rashi: קרובים; the midrash: alike in appearance).
- The munaḥ and zaqef divide the persons from the herders, as the quarrel moves from the herders to the masters.

Translator's note: Rashi reads 'men, brothers' as kinsmen (Lot was Abram's nephew); the English 'kinsmen' follows this. The midrash that they resembled each other is recorded in the commentary.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:13:8:1` follows: Rashi: 'men, brothers' means relatives; the midrash aggadah: alike in the features of the face. The English 'kinsmen' follows the plain sense.
- `radak:13:8:1` records: Radak: if the herders quarrel, we cannot avoid quarrelling ourselves.
- `onkelos:13:8:1` follows: Onkelos: 'for we are brothers' (גוּבְרִין אַחִין), agreeing with the plain sense.
- `sforno:13:8:1` records: Sforno: 'between me and you' refers to the future, when one of us chooses a pasture.
- `sforno:13:8:2` records: Sforno: let there be no quarrel, so that his friend too will be content.
- `sforno:13:8:3` records: Sforno: 'now, as we live here'.
- apparatus: 13:8.10: WLC reads וּבֵינֶיךָ, the base וּבֵינֶךָ; the sense is the same.

Grammar notes:
- אֵימִנָה and אַשְׂמְאִילָה are denominative Hiphil verbs from יָמִין and שְׂמֹאל, 'to take the right / the left'; both cohortatives.
- The first form lacks the expected yod of the Hiphil prefix (אֵימִנָה for אַיְמִינָה); Rashi compares לְהֵמִין in 2 Sam 14:19.
- The conditional clauses are elliptical: 'if (you take) the left, then I (will take) the right'.
- Radak reads שְׂמֹאל as north and יָמִין as south, since a person faces east; the English keeps 'left' and 'right' as in the Hebrew.

Translator's note: The English supplies 'you go' in the conditional clauses for clarity, which the Hebrew lacks. Rashi's 'I will turn myself to the right' is kept in the literal rendering. Ibn Ezra's note on the aleph of שמאל is a grammatical remark about the root and does not affect the English.

Commentary rulings:
- `rashi:13:9:1` follows: Rashi: wherever you settle, I will not go far from you and will stand by you as protector and helper; in the end he needed him (Gen 14:14). The English leaves the promise as Abram's offer of a free choice.
- `rashi:13:9:2` follows: Rashi: אַיְמִין אֶת עַצְמִי, 'I will turn myself to the right', like וְאַשְׂמְאִילָה, 'I will turn myself to the left'; and the form without yod is like לְהֵמִין (2 Sam 14:19). The English renders both as 'I will go right' and 'I will go left'.

Grammar notes:
- כִּי introduces the reason Lot saw it as attractive: all of it was irrigated.
- לִפְנֵי שַׁחֵת יְהֹוָה: a time phrase with a Piel infinitive; the subject, the LORD, follows the verbal noun (Ibn Ezra: 'before he destroys', infinitive of the intensive stem).
- כְּגַן יְהֹוָה and כְּאֶרֶץ מִצְרַיִם are two similes; the first echoes Eden (2:10), the second Egypt's irrigated fields.
- בֹּאֲכָה צֹעַר: 'as you come to Zoar', with a 2ms suffix that the plain sense treats as a general address (Radak: the speaker addresses the reader who knows the place); Rashi: 'as far as Zoar'.

Translator's note: Rashi and Ibn Ezra agree that כִּכָּר is a plain/region of vegetation. The English 'plain' follows Rashi and the Targum. Rashi takes 'garden of the LORD' as trees and 'land of Egypt' as crops; the English leaves both similes as written. The clause 'before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah' is the narrator's aside and is kept in place. Rashi (Ramban records the other) takes בֹּאֲכָה as 'as far as'; the English 'as you come to Zoar' keeps the Hebrew's form and the sense 'up to'.

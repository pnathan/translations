2:1

Rabbi says: Which is the upright path that a person should choose for himself? Whatever is a glory to those who do it and a glory to him from people. Be as careful with a light commandment as with a weighty one, for you do not know the reward given for the commandments. Reckon the loss from a commandment against its reward, and the gain from a transgression against its loss. Look at three things and you will not come to transgression: know what is above you: an eye that sees and an ear that hears, and all your deeds are written in a book.

Rabbi saying, which-is path upright that-he-chooses for-him the-person: all that-it glory to-its-doers and-glory to-him from the-person. And-be careful in-commandment light like-in-weighty, that-there-is-not you knowing giving-of reward-of-them of commandments. And-be reckoning loss-of commandment against reward-its, and-reward-of transgression against loss-its. And-look in-three things and-not you coming to-hands-of transgression: know what above from-you: eye seeing and-ear hearing and-all deeds-your in-the-book being-written.

Grammar
  • אֵיזוֹהִי is the Mishnaic interrogative 'which is it', agreeing with the feminine דֶרֶךְ.
  • The saying is built of parallel imperatives with וֶהֱוֵי: be careful (זָהִיר), be reckoning (מְחַשֵּׁב); then the closing triad, introduced by וְהִסְתַּכֵּל.
  • שֶׁיָּבֹר is a relative clause with a modal yiqtol: 'that a person should choose'; יָבֹר is from the geminate root ברר.
  • The last clause is a nominal series: 'an eye that sees, an ear that hears, and all your deeds written in a book': these are the three things.

'Glory' renders תִפְאֶרֶת in both parts; both normative commentators read the path as the middle one, which is a glory to the doer and to people. 'Upright' for יְשָׁרָה: 'straight, right'. The reckoning clauses follow Bartenura (what a commandment costs against its reward; the pleasure of a sin against what it costs); Rambam's reading is recorded in the apparatus. The three things: the eye, the ear and the book of deeds, as Rabbeinu Yonah counts them: he asks why three are named when on the plain reading they are one, and answers by dividing them into deeds, speech and memory.

Rambam: the upright path is the good actions, the middle traits, by which a person acquires a worthy disposition and good conduct with people; this is 'a glory to the doer and a glory to him from people'. Be as careful with a commandment thought light (rejoicing on a festival, learning the holy tongue) as with one known to be weighty, because the Torah states the punishments of the prohibitions in eight grades but not the reward of the positive commandments. He reads 'reckon the loss of a commandment against its reward' as inferring a positive commandment's reward from the penalty for neglecting it, and 'the reward of a transgression' as the reward for refraining from it, measured by its punishment.

2:2

Rabban Gamliel, son of Rabbi Yehuda the Patriarch, says: Torah study is excellent together with a worldly occupation, for the toil of both makes sin forgotten. And any Torah study that has no work with it in the end comes to nothing and draws sin along. And all who labor with the community should labor with them for the sake of Heaven, for the merit of their fathers supports them and their righteousness stands forever. And you, I credit to you a great reward as if you had done it.

And-all Torah that-there-is-not with-it work, end-its void and-dragging iniquity. Grammar
  • יָפֶה תַלְמוּד תּוֹרָה: the predicate comes first, 'excellent is Torah study'.
  • שֶׁיְּגִיעַת ... מְשַׁכַּחַת: a שֶׁ-clause of reason, 'for the toil of both ... makes forgotten'.
  • The middle saying turns on יִהְיוּ עֲמֵלִים 'let them labor' (jussive) and the participles מְסַיַּעְתָּן and עוֹמֶדֶת.
  • וְאַתֶּם ... מַעֲלֶה אֲנִי: the speaker turns to address the labourers directly: 'and as for you, I count to you ...'.

דֶּרֶךְ אֶרֶץ is translated 'a worldly occupation' following Bartenura's gloss (work or trade) and Rambam (earning a living). 'Their fathers' are the fathers of the community, per Bartenura. In the last clause the English leaves the object of 'you had done' unexpressed, as the Hebrew does. The title נָשִׂיא is 'the Patriarch'.

2:4

He used to say: Do His will as if it were your will, so that He will do your will as if it were His will. Set aside your will before His will, so that He will set aside the will of others before your will. Hillel says: Do not separate yourself from the community, and do not trust yourself until the day of your death, and do not judge your fellow until you reach his place, and do not say a thing that cannot be heard, for in the end it will be heard. And do not say, 'When I am free I will study': perhaps you will not be free.

He he-used saying: do will-his like-your-will, so-that he-will-do your-will like-his-will. Annul your-will from-before his-will, so-that he-will-annul will-of others from-before your-will. Hillel saying: not you-separate from the-community, and-not you-trust in-yourself until day-of your-death, and-not you-judge [object-marker] your-fellow until that-you-reach his-place, and-not you-say thing that-not possible to-hear that-its-end to-be-heard. And-not you-say when-I-am-free I-will-study, lest not you-will-be-free.

Grammar
  • הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר: a perfect of הָיָה with a participle, the habitual past ('used to say'); the unnamed subject is the speaker last named, Rabban Gamliel (2:2).
  • עֲשֵׂה ... בַּטֵּל: two imperatives, each followed by a כְּדֵי שֶׁ purpose clause with the roles of God and the speaker reversed.
  • Hillel's sayings are a string of אַל + yiqtol prohibitions; the verbs תִּפְרֹשׁ, תַּאֲמִין, תָּדִין, תֹּאמַר are all second masculine singular.
  • שֶׁסּוֹפוֹ לְהִשָּׁמַע: the Nifal infinitive after ל gives 'is bound to be heard'.

'He' in the first saying is the speaker last named, Rabban Gamliel of 2:2. The suffix of רְצוֹנוֹ is God, per Bartenura. The saying of Hillel on 'a thing that cannot be heard' is ambiguous. The English, 'for in the end it will be heard', gives Bartenura's second reading: do not say a secret thinking no one can hear it, for in the end it will be heard. Bartenura says this reading goes with the base text's שֶׁסּוֹפוֹ. His first reading, which is Rambam's, goes with his lemma וְסוֹפוֹ: do not say what cannot be understood at first hearing. It is recorded in the apparatus.

2:5

He used to say: No boor fears sin, and no unlearned person is pious, and no shy person learns, and no short-tempered person teaches, and not everyone who does much business becomes wise. And in a place where there are no men, strive to be a man.

He he-used saying: there-is-not boor fearing-of sin, and-not people-of the-land pious, and-not the-shy learning, and-not the-hot-tempered teaching, and-not all the-increasing in-business making-wise. And-in-place that-there-are-not men, strive to-be man.

Grammar
  • Five parallel negations, one with אֵין and four with וְלֹא: 'no boor ..., no unlearned person ..., no shy person ..., no short-tempered person ...'; the fifth, וְלֹא כָל, is 'not everyone who ...': it limits, it does not deny.
  • אֵין בּוּר יְרֵא חֵטְא: the existential negative אֵין governs the first clause; the rest use וְלֹא.
  • יְרֵא חֵטְא is a construct chain, 'one fearing of sin'.
  • The closing line shifts to an imperative, הִשְׁתַּדֵּל, with a play on אֲנָשִׁים and אִישׁ.

עַם הָאָרֶץ is 'an unlearned person' by the decisions; the literal 'people of the land' is given here. בּוּר is 'boor' (empty, uncultivated). Bartenura reads בּוּר as worse than the unlearned person: empty of everything, even of business sense. אִישׁ in the last clause is 'a man' in the sense of a person of worth; Bartenura takes it as sitting at the head and giving rulings.

Also he he-saw skull one that-floating upon face-of the-water. He-said to-her: because that-you-made-float, they-made-float-you; and-end-of your-floaters they-will-float.

Grammar
  • The second half of the mishnah is Aramaic: עַל דַּאֲטֵפְתְּ אַטְפוּךְ וְסוֹף מְטִיפַיִךְ יְטוּפוּן. The Aramaic relative דְּ replaces Hebrew שֶׁ.
  • The skull is feminine (גֻלְגֹּלֶת), so the speaker addresses it with the feminine suffixes -תְּ, -ךְ and -ַיִךְ.
  • The saying uses the root טוּף four times as a play: the Afel (causative) in אֲטֵפְתְּ 'you made float', אַטְפוּךְ 'they made you float' and מְטִיפַיִךְ 'those who made you float', then the Peal (simple) יְטוּפוּן 'they will float'.
  • אַף הוּא opens the mishnah as a link to the preceding sayings of Hillel.

The Aramaic clause is a measure-for-measure saying: Bartenura reads the skull as that of a wicked man who threw people into the river; Rambam gives it the moral that evil deeds return on the head of the doer. The English 'drowned' stands for the Aramaic 'made float', which Bartenura glosses as casting people on the river. The last verb is read as passive in sense: 'will be drowned'.

2:7

He used to say: The more flesh, the more worms. The more property, the more anxiety. The more wives, the more witchcraft. The more maidservants, the more lewdness. Grammar
  • מַרְבֶּה is a hifil masculine singular participle ('one who increases'). The saying runs on one pattern, מַרְבֶּה X מַרְבֶּה Y, and the English gives it as 'the more X, the more Y'; the participle carries no 'if' or 'then'.
  • The first five pairs set a possession against its cost (flesh and worms, property and anxiety, wives and witchcraft, maidservants and lewdness, slaves and robbery); the next four set a good against its reward (Torah and life, study and wisdom, counsel and understanding, charity and peace). The list is closed by two sentences with קָנָה, the perfect used for a general truth.
  • דְאָגָה (anxiety), כְשָׁפִים (sorceries, plural of כֶּשֶׁף) and זִמָּה (lewdness) are all biblical words (Prov 12:25; 2 Kgs 9:22; Lev 18:17); the pack's BDB entries for זִמָּה and עֶבֶד are proper names with the same consonants, and Jastrow's entry for רָבָה is only a cross-reference.
  • In קָנָה לוֹ, the preposition with suffix לוֹ is a dative of advantage ('for himself'); in לְעַצְמוֹ the noun 'bone, self' is used as the reflexive.
  • הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא is a noun with an article plus a participle with an article, 'the world that comes'; decisions.md fixes it as 'the world to come'.

מַרְבֶּה is given as 'the more ...' to keep the brevity of the sayings. בָּשָׂר is rendered 'flesh' as in Bartenura (one who eats and drinks until he is fat); the English does not decide whether the worms of the grave or of the body are meant. יְשִׁיבָה: 'sitting', that is, sitting in session or in the academy (Bartenura: many disciples gather to hear his reasoning); I keep the literal word. In this reading the clause is 'more sitting, more wisdom', and Bartenura notes that others read 'one who increases yeshivah increases wisdom', which is what our text has. צְדָקָה: 'charity' (giving), the sense of the later usage; Bartenura quotes Isaiah 32:17, where the word is 'righteousness', so the English keeps 'charity' while the wider sense is recorded here.

Grammar
  • קִבֵּל (piel of קבל) is the technical verb of transmission: 'received (the tradition)'. It has no stated object in the mishnah.
  • אִם לָמַדְתָּ תּוֹרָה הַרְבֵּה: a conditional with the perfect (אִם plus qatal), and הַרְבֵּה, the hifil infinitive absolute of רבה, used as an adverb after the object; אַל תַּחֲזִיק is a negative command with hifil yiqtol of חזק.
  • חֲמִשָּׁה תַלְמִידִים הָיוּ לוֹ: possession is expressed by הָיָה plus ל ('five disciples were to him'); the following לְרַבָּן יוֹחָנָן בֶּן זַכַּאי repeats the owner by name.
  • הוּא הָיָה מוֹנֶה שִׁבְחָן and הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר: הָיָה with a participle is the habitual past ('used to enumerate', 'used to say'); the suffix -ָן in שִׁבְחָן is the Mishnaic 3rd person plural, used for masculine as for feminine referents: 'their praises'.
  • Each praise is a nominal clause without a verb: name, then predicate (בּוֹר סוּד ..., אַשְׁרֵי יוֹלַדְתּוֹ, חָסִיד, יְרֵא חֵטְא, מַעְיָן הַמִּתְגַּבֵּר); יְרֵא חֵטְא is a construct chain of adjective and noun.
  • מַכְרִיעַ אֶת כֻּלָּם: hifil participle of כרע, 'tilts the scale against them all'; the weighing is expressed by a conditional with the plural יִהְיוּ and the dual מֹאזְנַיִם, 'a pair of scales'.

קִבֵּל מֵהִלֵּל וּמִשַּׁמָּאי: the English leaves the object unsaid, as in the Hebrew; the sense is 'received the tradition'. אַל תַּחֲזִיק טוֹבָה לְעַצְמְךָ: 'do not claim credit for yourself' (Bartenura: do not say 'I have learned much Torah'; he left no Scripture, Mishnah, halakhah or aggadah unlearned). The literal 'do not hold goodness for yourself' is kept in the literal line. בּוֹר סוּד: סוּד is the passive participle of סוד 'to plaster': 'a plastered cistern'. Bartenura says that this is the reading, not בּוֹר סִיד 'a cistern of lime' (the Vilna spelling), and explains 'like a cistern coated with lime'; Rambam: 'like a lined cistern that does not lose its water'. The English follows the base text as both explain it. חָסִיד: 'a pious man'; Bartenura: one who acts beyond the line of the law. יְרֵא חֵטְא: 'a fearer of sin'; Bartenura: one who is strict with himself and forbids himself permitted things for fear of sin. מַעְיָן הַמִּתְגַּבֵּר: 'a spring gathering strength': Bartenura says his heart is wide and he adds arguments and reasons from his own mind. אַבָּא שָׁאוּל אוֹמֵר מִשְּׁמוֹ: Bartenura reads 'in his name' as in the name of Rabban Yohanan; and he writes that Abba Shaul does not dispute the first teacher, but both sayings are the Rabban's, and both are true, since for knowledge and memory Rabbi Eliezer outweighs all and for sharpness and argument Rabbi Elazar son of Arach does.

Grammar
  • The question is asked twice: אֵיזוֹהִי דֶרֶךְ יְשָׁרָה שֶׁיִּדְבַּק בָּהּ הָאָדָם and אֵיזוֹהִי דֶרֶךְ רָעָה שֶׁיִּתְרַחֵק מִמֶּנָּה הָאָדָם. אֵיזוֹהִי is 'which is' (the feminine of the Mishnaic interrogative, agreeing with דֶרֶךְ, which is feminine); שֶׁיִּדְבַּק and שֶׁיִּתְרַחֵק are relative clauses with the imperfect (qal of דבק, hitpael of רחק) of purpose or fitness.
  • The answers are nominal clauses in a fixed frame: name + אוֹמֵר + noun + adjective (עַיִן טוֹבָה, חָבֵר טוֹב, שָׁכֵן טוֹב, לֵב טוֹב), and the opposites with רָע/רָעָה. The adjective agrees with the noun in gender, so עַיִן, which is feminine, takes טוֹבָה and רָעָה.
  • הָרוֹאֶה אֶת הַנּוֹלָד: 'the one who sees the born' has a qal participle with the article and a nifal participle with the article (the object marker אֶת before it); the idiom means to see what will come to be. Its answer-partner is הַלֹּוֶה וְאֵינוֹ מְשַׁלֵּם, a participle plus a negative participial clause with אֵין and a suffix.
  • רוֹאֶה אֲנִי אֶת דִּבְרֵי אֶלְעָזָר ... מִדִּבְרֵיכֶם: the participle comes before the pronoun, the usual Mishnaic order ('I see'); the comparative מִן means 'rather than'. שֶׁבִּכְלָל דְּבָרָיו דִּבְרֵיכֶם is a nominal clause with a fronted prepositional phrase.
  • Rabbi Shimon's second saying quotes Psalm 37:21 (לֹוֶה רָשָׁע וְלֹא יְשַׁלֵּם, וְצַדִּיק חוֹנֵן וְנוֹתֵן), introduced by שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר, 'as it is said'; the verse is in biblical Hebrew with participles (חוֹנֵן, 'gracious', and נוֹתֵן, 'giving').

עַיִן טוֹבָה / עַיִן רָעָה: a 'good eye' is contentment with what one has (Bartenura, Rambam); Rabbeinu Yonah, who is not normative, reads it as generosity. The English keeps the idiom and gives the sense here. הָרוֹאֶה אֶת הַנּוֹלָד: 'one who sees what is to come': Bartenura says he looks at what will be and weighs the loss of a commandment against its reward, and the gain of a transgression against its loss; Rambam says he reflects on the outcome of his affairs. The literal is 'one who sees that which is born'. רוֹאֶה אֲנִי: 'I prefer', literally 'I see'; Rambam says it means 'chooses and discerns'. אֶחָד הַלֹּוֶה ... כְּלֹוֶה: 'one who borrows ... is as one who borrows': the comparison makes the debt to a person a debt to God. הַמָּקוֹם: 'the Omnipresent', literally 'the Place' (decisions.md). וְצַדִּיק חוֹנֵן וְנוֹתֵן: in Psalm 37:21 this is the righteous person who gives to others; Bartenura (and Rambam) read the 'righteous one' as God, who is gracious to the lender and gives him what the borrower did not repay, so that the borrower remains a debtor to the Omnipresent. The English follows them: 'the Righteous One'. Bartenura (2:9:6) explains why the evil way is asked about separately: the opposite of every good trait is not necessarily evil, so a second question was needed.

Grammar
  • הֵם אָמְרוּ שְׁלשָׁה דְבָרִים: 'they' are the five disciples, each of whom said three things (Rabbeinu Yonah; Bartenura: three things on conduct and character, always on their lips); the three of Rabbi Eliezer follow (Bartenura counts them in 2:10:2; Rambam holds that the third, on the sages' fire, is a saying he reported from another).
  • יְהִי and תְּהִי are jussive and imperfect of הָיָה used as imperatives; וֶהֱוֵי is the imperative with a conjunction. The pairs וֶהֱוֵי מִתְחַמֵּם and וֶהֱוֵי זָהִיר govern a participle and an adjective: 'be warming yourself', 'be careful'.
  • שֶׁלֹּא תִכָּוֶה: שֶׁלֹּא with the nifal imperfect of כוה, 'that you not be burned'.
  • The ending -ָן in גַּחַלְתָּן, נְשִׁיכָתָן, עֲקִיצָתָן, לְחִישָׁתָן and אוּרָן (and שִׁבְחָן in 2:8) is the 3rd person plural suffix of Mishnaic Hebrew ('their'), used for masculine as for feminine referents; here it refers to the sages (חֲכָמִים).
  • Each of the three comparisons is a construct chain, 'the bite of a fox', 'the sting of a scorpion', 'the whisper of a saraph', in which the second member names a creature whose attack is feared.

הֵם: the disciples of Rabban Yohanan, each of whom said three things (Rabbeinu Yonah; Bartenura's 'these three things were always on their lips'); the English keeps 'They'. אוּרָן: אוּר 'fire' (with shureq), distinct from אוֹר 'light'; the English 'fire' is the plain sense. שׁוּעָל: 'fox' (Bartenura describes its teeth as thin and crooked, so the bite is hard to heal). לְחִישָׁה: 'whisper, spell'; Bartenura glosses it as דִּבּוּרָן, 'their speech'. שָׂרָף: 'saraph, fiery serpent'. כְּנֶגֶד, rendered 'at', is literally 'opposite'; Rabbeinu Yonah glosses 'near'. The English takes שֶׁלֹּא תִכָּוֶה as 'lest you be burned' and the following שֶׁ clause as the reason.

2:11

Rabbi Yehoshua says: eye the-evil, and-impulse the-evil, and-hatred-of the-creatures, remove-they ACC the-person from the-world

Grammar
  • The three subjects are joined by 'and' and take the plural participle מוֹצִיאִין (hifil of יצא, with -ין, the Aramaic-style plural of Mishnaic Hebrew): 'they take a person out of the world'.
  • עַיִן הָרָע and יֵצֶר הָרָע both have the adjective with the article after a noun without it (the noun is definite by its qualifier); contrast עַיִן רָעָה in 2:9, with no article, 'an evil eye'.
  • שִׂנְאַת הַבְּרִיּוֹת is a construct chain: 'the hatred of the creatures'; whether the genitive is subjective (others' hatred) or objective (one's hatred of them) is open, as Bartenura's two readings show.
  • מִן הָעוֹלָם: 'from the world' means out of life; Rambam reads it as a death before one's time.

עַיִן הָרָע: Bartenura gives two readings, the same as 'an evil eye' (discontent with one's lot) or malevolent gaze at a friend's money or children that causes harm. The English keeps the ambiguity of 'the evil eye'. שִׂנְאַת הַבְּרִיּוֹת: 'hatred of people' is likewise ambiguous: baseless hatred (Bartenura), loathing human company (Rambam, whom Bartenura reports) or the hatred a harsh man brings on himself (Bartenura's own hearing). יֵצֶר הָרָע: 'the evil impulse'; 'inclination' is equally correct.

And when you pray, do not make your prayer a fixed routine, but a plea for mercy and supplication before the Omnipresent, blessed be He, as it is said (Joel 2): “For He is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger, abounding in kindness, and relenting of the evil.” And do not be wicked in your own eyes.

Grammar
  • הֱוֵי and תְּהִי are both forms of הָיָה: the contracted imperative 'be!' and the jussive-type imperfect after אַל, 'do not be'.
  • אַל תַּעַשׂ is a prohibition: אַל with the imperfect. אֶלָּא 'but' sets the right prayer against the wrong one, with the verb 'make' understood.
  • קֶבַע is the opposite of תַּחֲנוּנִים: a fixed act versus a plea; רַחֲמִים וְתַחֲנוּנִים are the two things the prayer should be.
  • The mishnah quotes Joel 2:13 as it stands there: כִּי חַנּוּן וְרַחוּם הוּא, without אֵל (the Vilna text adds אֵל, as in Jonah 4:2). אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם is a construct chain: אֶרֶךְ is the construct of the adjective אָרֵךְ 'long', 'long of nostrils (anger)', meaning patient.
  • חַנּוּן, רַחוּם, אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם, רַב חֶסֶד and the nifal participle וְנִחָם are a series of attributes of God, all predicates of הוּא.
  • בִּפְנֵי עַצְמְךָ, literally 'in the face of your self', is an idiom whose sense is disputed; see the note.

The last clause is ambiguous. Bartenura gives three readings: do not do what will make you condemn yourself; Rambam's 'do not be wicked in your own eyes', that is, do not regard yourself as wicked, or you will turn wholly to evil; and his own, do not separate from the community and stand 'by yourself'. The English follows Rambam, who is the commentator also listed as normative, and keeps the Hebrew idiom 'in your own eyes'. The sense of the pause on 'a plea for mercy' is that prayer is a petition, not a duty to be discharged.

2:14

Rabbi Elazar says: Be diligent in learning Torah, and know what to answer an Epicurean. And know before whom you toil. And faithful is the master of your work to pay you the reward of your labor.

Rabbi Elazar says be diligent to-learn Torah, and-know what-that-you-answer to-Epicurean. And-know before whom you toiling. And-faithful he master-of your-work that-he-will-pay to-you reward-of your-labor.

Grammar
  • Three imperatives in a row (הֱוֵי, וְדַע, וְדַע) give the saying its three parts; the second and third are in the same form, 'know!'.
  • מַה שֶּׁתָּשִׁיב is 'what you should answer': מַה plus the relative שֶׁ and the imperfect used as a duty.
  • לִפְנֵי מִי is 'before whom', with מִי as an interrogative inside the object clause.
  • בַּעַל מְלַאכְתְּךָ is a construct chain 'master of your work'; the word בַּעַל is the owner, and the image is of an employer who pays a worker.
  • The last clause begins with וְנֶאֱמָן הוּא, a predicate placed first for emphasis.

אֶפִּיקוֹרוֹס is kept as 'Epicurean' with its sense a scoffer at the Torah: Bartenura derives it from הֶפְקֵר, 'ownerless, license', as one who treats the Torah as ownerless. Rambam understands it of idolaters, Rabbeinu Yonah of one who denies the oral Torah; the translation does not narrow it. The English wording of the last sentence follows the Hebrew order; the שֶׁ clause is read as 'to pay'.

2:15

Rabbi Tarfon says: The day is short, the work is great, the laborers are lazy, the reward is abundant, and the master of the house is pressing.

Rabbi Tarfon says the-day short, and-the-work abundant, and-the-laborers lazy, and-the-reward much, and-master-of the-house pressing.

Grammar
  • The saying is five short nominal clauses with no verb 'to be': subject then predicate, each joined by וְ.
  • הַרְבֵּה is a hifil infinitive absolute that works as an adverb or predicate, 'much'; here it stands for the amount of the reward.
  • מְרֻבָּה is a pual participle, feminine to agree with מְלָאכָה.
  • בַּעַל הַבַּיִת is 'master of the house', a householder who hires workers; as a metaphor it stands for God.

The saying is a parable (so Rambam). The day is a man's short life (Bartenura), the work is the Torah, which is long, and the master of the house is God, who presses the worker. The English keeps the parable's images and does not spell out the interpretation.

  • הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר is the formula 'he used to say' (the habitual past: הָיָה plus participle); the speaker is Rabbi Tarfon.
  • לֹא עָלֶיךָ ... לִגְמֹר: 'not upon you to finish'; the infinitive construct with ל gives the duty or burden. The second clause has the same negative with 'free'.
  • בֶן חוֹרִין is an idiom: 'free man'; לִבָּטֵל is the nifal infinitive, 'to be idle, to be exempt'.
  • נוֹתְנִים is a participle plural with no subject, an impersonal 'they give'.
  • לֶעָתִיד לָבֹא, literally 'for the future that comes', is the mishnaic term for the world to come.

לֶעָתִיד לָבֹא is translated 'in the world to come', since Bartenura and Rambam both say it means לָעוֹלָם הַבָּא; the literal sense is 'for the time that is to come'. נוֹתְנִים 'they give' is an impersonal plural: 'one gives you', that is, you are given. The last clause is a nominal clause, the object of וְדַע, with לֶעָתִיד לָבֹא as its predicate: 'that the giving of their reward, of the righteous, is in the time to come'; the English supplies 'that' and 'is'.

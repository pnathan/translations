6:1

The sages taught in the language of the Mishnah; blessed is He who chose them and their teaching. Rabbi Meir says: Whoever engages in Torah for its own sake merits many things. Not only that, but the entire world is worthwhile for his sake. He is called friend, beloved, one who loves the Omnipresent, one who loves creatures, one who gladdens the Omnipresent, one who gladdens creatures. It clothes him in humility and awe, it fits him to be righteous, devout, upright and faithful, it keeps him far from sin and draws him near to merit. People derive from him the benefit of counsel and sound wisdom, understanding and strength, as it is said (Proverbs 8): "Mine are counsel and sound wisdom; I am understanding; mine is strength." It gives him kingship and dominion and the probing of judgment; the secrets of Torah are revealed to him; he becomes like a spring that grows ever stronger and like a river that never ceases; he is modest and patient, and he forgives insult done to him; it makes him great and raises him above all the works.

Grammar
  • This opening sentence is the standard introduction to the "baraita of the Sages of the Mishnah" (the Kinyan Torah): the phrase בִּלְשׁוֹן הַמִּשְׁנָה marks what follows as Mishnaic (Tannaitic) teaching, and בָּרוּךְ שֶׁבָּחַר is a short doxology.
  • The long run of participles in the third clause (אוֹהֵב, מְשַׂמֵּחַ ...) is a list of descriptive titles after נִקְרָא; מְשַׂמֵּחַ is a Piel participle, "one who causes gladness".
  • From וּמַלְבַּשְׁתּוֹ to וּמְרוֹמַמְתּוֹ the feminine singular participles (מַלְבֶּשֶׁת, מַכְשֶׁרֶת, מְרַחֶקֶת, מְקָרֶבֶת, נוֹתֶנֶת ...) have the feminine noun תוֹרָה as their unstated subject, with the masculine suffix -ו referring to the one who engages in Torah.
  • נֶהֱנִין and מְגַלִּין are masculine plural participles with no expressed subject, i.e. impersonal ("people benefit", "they reveal" = "are revealed").
  • Mishnaic Hebrew uses שֶׁ- for "that", "who", "for"; so שֶׁכָּל הָעוֹלָם כֻּלּוֹ ... is "that the whole world ..." after אֶלָּא "but".

כְדַי הוּא לוֹ means roughly "it is enough, it is worth it, for him": the whole world is (worth the) trouble or existence for his sake; the Hebrew does not say who benefits. The subject of the feminine participles is the Torah (see grammar). וֶהֱוֵי is an imperative ("be!") standing in a run of third-person descriptions; the English follows the sense ("he is modest and patient") and the imperative is recorded in the apparatus. הַמַּעֲשִׂים, "the works", can mean God's creations or deeds; the English keeps the literal "works".

Grammar
  • בַּת קוֹל, literally "daughter of a voice", is a technical term for a heavenly voice that makes God's will known after prophecy has ceased; it is in the construct state, and יוֹצֵאת, מַכְרֶזֶת and אוֹמֶרֶת are feminine participles agreeing with it.
  • וָיוֹם repeats יוֹם with a conjunction for emphasis: "day and day", i.e. every single day.
  • מֵעֶלְבּוֹנָהּ שֶׁל תּוֹרָה uses the Mishnaic genitive particle שֶׁל; the feminine suffix -הּ anticipates תּוֹרָה (a "split construct"): "from her insult, of Torah". The insult is the one Torah suffers, when people neglect her.
  • The citation אֵין לְךָ ... אֶלָּא מִי שֶׁ- is the Mishnaic idiom "you have no X except one who ..."; אַל תִּקְרָא ... אֶלָּא is a formula of reading-variation (a midrashic re-vocalization), here exploiting the two readings חָרוּת / חֵרוּת of the same consonants חרות.
  • Numbers 21:19 is a list of stopping-places; the mishnah relies on the meanings of the names, mattanah "gift", naḥaliel "inheritance (or portion) of God", bamot "heights", to describe a rise by stages. The English translates the verse as place names, as the Hebrew has them.

Vilna's text differs at 6:2.71-72 (בתורה תדיר, "constantly in Torah"). נָזוּף is the passive participle of נזף, "rebuked, censured". סָרַת טָעַם: טַעַם is "taste, discernment, good sense"; the verse says the woman has turned aside from it. The English for בַּת קוֹל gives the meaning "heavenly voice" and the literal sense is in the phrase literal. הֲרֵי זֶה (lit. "behold this one") is rendered "such a person".

6:3

One who learns from his fellow a single chapter, or a single halakhah, or a single verse, or a single saying, or even a single letter, must treat him with honor. For so we find with David, king of Israel, who learned from Ahithophel only two things, yet called him his teacher, his companion and his familiar, as it is said (Psalms 55): "And you, a man of my own rank, my companion and my familiar." Is this not an argument from the lesser to the greater? If David, king of Israel, who learned from Ahithophel only two things, called him his teacher, his companion and his familiar, then one who learns from his fellow a single chapter, or a single halakhah, or a single verse, or a single saying, or even a single letter, how much more so must he treat him with honor. And there is no honor but Torah, as it is said (Proverbs 3): "The wise shall inherit honor," and (Proverbs 28): "and the blameless shall inherit good"; and there is no good but Torah, as it is said (Proverbs 4): "For I have given you good teaching; do not forsake my Torah."

Grammar
  • הַלּוֹמֵד is a participle with the article used as a noun, "the one who learns", the subject of the whole sentence; צָרִיךְ לִנְהוֹג בּוֹ כָבוֹד is "must conduct honor toward him" (נָהַג + בְּ-).
  • שֶׁכֵּן מָצִינוּ ("for so we found") is a formula introducing a proof from precedent; שֶׁלֹּא ... אֶלָּא means "who ... only".
  • וַהֲלֹא דְבָרִים קַל וָחֹמֶר is the formula of the a fortiori argument, "light and heavy": what holds in the lighter case (two things learned) holds all the more in the weightier (a chapter, a law ...); the second half is closed by עַל אַחַת כַּמָּה וְכַמָּה, "how much more so".
  • אֵין ... אֶלָּא ("there is no X but Y") is a definitional formula: "honor" in the verse means Torah, "good" in the verse means Torah, which links the three verses.
  • The citations are all verses with direct speech: Psalms 55:14 is addressed to a friend (2nd person), Proverbs 3:35 and 28:10 are third-person statements, and Proverbs 4:2 is God's address (1st person) to the people.

אַלּוּף is "tame; friend, intimate; chief"; the English "companion" follows the sense of Psalm 55 and the sequence rabbo-alufo-meyudda'o (teacher, companion, familiar), but "chief, guide" is possible (see lexicon sense 3). כְּעֶרְכִּי is "according to my rank/estimate", i.e. "my equal". The English "halakhah" keeps the technical term for a received rule of law. בִּלְבַד "only". תּוֹרָתִי in the quoted verse is "my teaching", a gloss of "my Torah"; the English keeps "Torah" as the decisions require.

Grammar
  • כַּךְ הִיא דַּרְכָּהּ שֶׁל תּוֹרָה: the feminine suffix -הּ again anticipates תּוֹרָה (שֶׁל construction); כַּךְ is "so, thus", and the sentence reads "so is the way of Torah".
  • The imperfects תֹּאכַל, תִשְׁתֶּה, תִּישַׁן, תִּחְיֶה are 2nd masculine singular with a force between a command and a description ("you shall"); חַיֵּי צַעַר תִּחְיֶה is a cognate accusative, "live a life of hardship".
  • בִּמְשׂוּרָה is "by measure": a small measured quantity; the phrase pairs with בְּמֶלַח to describe a bare diet.
  • The verse Psalms 128:2 has אַשְׁרֶיךָ וְטוֹב לָךְ; the mishnah then splits the pair between this world and the world to come, repeating אַשְׁרֶיךָ.

וּבַתּוֹרָה אַתָּה עָמֵל puts the object of effort first for emphasis ("and in Torah you toil"); the explicit pronoun אַתָּה with a participle is the Mishnaic present. The verse is read as describing the reward, "happy are you" (אַשְׁרֶיךָ) being the Hebrew exclamation of blessedness.

6:5

Do not seek greatness for yourself, and do not covet honor; do more than you learn. Do not crave the table of kings, for your table is greater than their table, and your crown is greater than their crown, and the master of your work is faithful to pay you the reward of your labor.

Grammar
  • אַל + imperfect (תְּבַקֵּשׁ, תַּחְמֹד, תִּתְאַוֶּה) is the negative command, "do not ..."; the one positive command is the bare imperative עֲשֵׂה.
  • יוֹתֵר מִלִּמּוּדְךָ עֲשֵׂה has the comparative phrase before the verb: "more than your learning, do".
  • לְשֻׁלְחָנָם שֶׁל מְלָכִים has a pleonastic suffix ("their table, of kings") with the genitive particle שֶׁל, as in 6:2 and 6:4.
  • The two comparisons שֶׁשֻּׁלְחָנְךָ גָדוֹל מִשֻּׁלְחָנָם and וְכִתְרְךָ גָדוֹל מִכִּתְרָם use מִן with an adjective for "greater than"; the "table" and "crown" are those of Torah (the scholar's own).

יוֹתֵר מִלִּמּוּדְךָ עֲשֵׂה is ambiguous: "act more than you study" (let practice exceed learning), or "do more than your learning requires". The English "do more than you learn" keeps the compression. בַּעַל מְלַאכְתְּךָ is "the master (employer) of your work"; by context it is God, and the English leaves it literal. שֶׁיְּשַׁלֵּם after נֶאֱמָן is "faithful ... to pay".

6:6

Grammar
  • נִקְנֵית is a Nifal passive participle, "is acquired (bought)"; the three acquisitions, kingship, priesthood and Torah, are measured by a number of מַעֲלוֹת "degrees" (kingship 30, priesthood 24, with מַעֲלוֹת understood in the second clause) and דְבָרִים "things" (Torah 48).
  • The long list is a series of nouns, each with the preposition בְּ- "by, with, in", then a run of participles (הַמַּכִּיר, הַשָּׂמֵחַ, הָעוֹשֶׂה ...), with the article, describing the person who acquires Torah; the participles are in the nominative absolute with no verb, listing qualities rather than steps.
  • בְּמִעוּט ("with little of ...") repeats six times; בְּ- with an abstract noun gives the means or manner. The pairs that name states of mind (אֵימָה, יִרְאָה, עֲנָוָה, שִׂמְחָה, טָהֳרָה) are plain abstract nouns with בְּ-.
  • מִתְרַחֵק, מִתְיַשֵּׁב are Hitpael participles (reflexive: "distances himself", "settles"); לִבּוֹ is the subject of מִתְיַשֵּׁב and the object of מֵגִיס ("makes his heart proud, puffs up").
  • הָא לָמַדְתָּ ("so you have learned") closes the series by drawing a conclusion; שֶׁכָּל הָאוֹמֵר ... מֵבִיא is a general principle proved by Esther 2:22.

דֶּרֶךְ אֶרֶץ in בְּמִעוּט דֶּרֶךְ אֶרֶץ is "worldly occupation" (lit.   • נָתַן שָׁלוֹם is an idiom for giving a greeting; הֶחֱזִיר שָׁלוֹם, "returned a greeting".
  • אֵינִי דָר אֶלָּא "I dwell only/nowhere but" is a negative with אֶלָּא: exclusive sense.
  • אֵין מְלַוִּין is a plural participle with no stated subject (impersonal), followed by a list governed by לֹא ... וְלֹא.
  • The verse is quoted twice: first whole, then split into its three clauses, each tied to a world (this world, the grave, the world to come).
  • מֵאַלְפֵי זָהָב וָכָסֶף: "than thousands of gold and silver", with an understood unit of coin.

מֵעִיר גְּדוֹלָה שֶׁל חֲכָמִים: the particle שֶׁל is mishnaic; the dinars of gold are dinars (דִּינָר). The sentence about what accompanies a person is impersonal in Hebrew ("they do not accompany him"); the English gives the sense of the plural participle without a subject.

Grammar
  • קִנְיָן is a noun of the form קִנְיָן "acquisition, possession", from קנה; the mishnah reads the verb קנה in each verse as "acquire".
  • מִנַּיִן is the formula that asks for the proof text: "from where [do we learn it]?"; דִּכְתִיב "for it is written" introduces the answer.
  • וְאֵלּוּ הֵן, "and these are they", introduces the list.
  • Heaven and earth form one possession: שָׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ is the paired phrase, with both nouns under one קִנְיָן אֶחָד.
  • In the verses, qal participle קֹנֵה (construct) governs שָׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ; Exodus 15:16 repeats עַד יַעֲבֹר and זוּ is a relative pronoun.

In Prov 8:22 קָנָנִי may also mean "created me"; the mishnah reads "acquired", and the English follows it. For Gen 14:19 קֹנֵה may be "maker" or "possessor"; "possessor" matches the point of the mishnah. The pack marks לְאֵל (Gen 14:19) and אֶל (Ps 78:54) with the lemma אַל; the data is kept, and the words are the noun "God" and the preposition "to".

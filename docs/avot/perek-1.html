1:1

Moses received Torah from Sinai and handed it down to Joshua; Joshua to the elders; the elders to the prophets; and the prophets handed it down to the members of the Great Assembly. They said three things: Be deliberate in judgment; raise up many disciples; and make a fence for the Torah.

Moses received Torah from-Sinai, and-handed-it to-Joshua, and-Joshua to-elders, and-elders to-prophets, and-prophets handed-it to-men-of assembly the-great. They said three things: Be deliberate in-the-judgment, and-raise-up disciples many, and-make fence to-the-Torah.

Grammar
  • The chain of tradition has one verb, מָסַר 'hand over', and drops it after the first link: 'Joshua to the elders, the elders to the prophets'.
  • מְסָרוּהָ (3rd plural with the 3rd feminine singular suffix, 'handed it') states the verb again in the last link, as it was stated for Moses: the chain opens and closes with מָסַר.
  • הֵם אָמְרוּ שְׁלשָׁה דְבָרִים introduces three plural imperatives: הֱווּ (Qal of הָיָה, Mishnaic form), הַעֲמִידוּ (Hifil of the I-guttural root עמד, 'cause to stand') and עֲשׂוּ (Qal of the III-ה root עשה).
  • קִבֵּל is Piel of קבל, 'received', and in Mishnaic Hebrew is the standard verb for receiving a tradition.
  • The third saying is a metaphor: סְיָג is a hedge round a field or a vineyard.

'Torah' is without the article, as in the Hebrew, and not 'the Torah'. 'From Sinai' is kept literal; Bartenura glosses it as 'from the One who was revealed at Sinai'. 'Members of the Great Assembly' follows decisions.md for אַנְשֵׁי כְנֶסֶת הַגְּדוֹלָה. 'Be deliberate' renders מְתוּנִים, from a root meaning 'slow, patient'; both Bartenura and Rambam read it as waiting before deciding a case.

Bartenura: unlike the other tractates of the Mishnah this one is not built on a commandment but on ethics and character traits; the sages of the nations also wrote such books. 1:2

Shimon the Righteous was of the remnants of the Great Assembly. He used to say: On three things the world stands: on the Torah, and on the Temple service, and on acts of kindness.

Shimon the-Righteous was from-remnants-of assembly the-great. He was saying: On three things the-world stands: on the-Torah, and-on the-service, and-on bestowal-of kindnesses.

Grammar
  • הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר: the pronoun, the past of הָיָה and a participle express a habitual saying ('he used to say').
  • עַל ... עוֹמֵד: the sentence is a list of three pillars; each is introduced by עַל, and the last two by וְעַל.
  • גְּמִילוּת חֲסָדִים is a construct chain: 'the bestowing of kindnesses'.
  • מִשְּׁיָרֵי has a partitive מִ: 'one of the remnants', and not 'from (them)'.

הָעֲבוֹדָה is rendered 'the Temple service'; Bartenura (עֲבוֹדַת הַקָּרְבָּנוֹת) and Rambam (the sacrifices) agree. 'Stands' keeps the Hebrew verb; Bartenura glosses it 'the world was created only for these three'.

1:3

Antigonos of Sokho received from Shimon the Righteous. He used to say: Do not be like servants who serve the master on condition of receiving a gratuity, but be like servants who serve the master not on condition of receiving a gratuity; and let the fear of Heaven be upon you.

Antigonos man-of Sokho received from-Shimon the-Righteous. He was saying: Not be-you like-servants the-serving [acc.] the-master on condition-of to-receive gratuity, but be-you like-servants the-serving [acc.] the-master that-not on condition-of to-receive gratuity, and-let-be fear-of Heaven upon-you.

Grammar
  • The saying is built on antithesis: אַל תִּהְיוּ ... אֶלָּא הֱווּ, with the same words repeated, the second time with שֶׁלֹּא.
  • עַל מְנָת (לְ) is an idiom for 'on condition (of)'; לְקַבֵּל is a Piel infinitive.
  • הַמְשַׁמְּשִׁין is Piel participle plural with the article, and את marks the definite object הָרַב (a Mishnaic use with שמש).
  • וִיהִי is the jussive 'and let there be'; the Mishnaic Hebrew uses it with a subject מוֹרָא שָׁמַיִם 'fear of Heaven'.
  • The verb forms: תִּהְיוּ is imperfect used as a prohibition with אַל; הֱווּ is the imperative.

פְּרָס is rendered 'gratuity' because both commentators define it against שָׂכָר 'wage': a gift from one who owes nothing. אִישׁ סוֹכוֹ is 'of Sokho' (literally 'man of Sokho').

Grammar
  • Pairs (זוּגוֹת): from Yose ben Yoezer on, teachers are named two by two, so קִבְּלוּ is plural; Bartenura says the first of each pair was the nasi and the second av beit din.
  • בֵּית וַעַד is 'a house of meeting' for the sages; בֵּית is construct and וַעַד an absolute noun.
  • מִתְאַבֵּק is a Hithpael participle ('covering oneself in dust'); with וֶהֱוֵי it makes a continuing action: 'be (continually) covered'.
  • וֶהֱוֵי: the conjunction וְ before a ḥatef vowel takes the matching full vowel, so וֶ before הֱ; the saying has three parallel commands (יְהִי, וֶהֱוֵי, וֶהֱוֵי).
  • אֶת marks the definite object דִּבְרֵיהֶם, as in Biblical Hebrew; the word order puts בְצָמָא first, before the object.

מִתְאַבֵּק is rendered as the Hebrew image, 'covered in the dust of their feet', and not as the metaphor 'sit at their feet'. Bartenura gives two readings of it: (1) follow them closely, so that you are covered with the dust they raise; (2) sit on the ground at their feet while the teacher sits on the bench. The English keeps the image and takes (1), the first and more literal.

1:5

Yose son of Yochanan of Jerusalem says: Let your house be open wide; and let the poor be members of your household; and do not talk too much with the woman. They said this of one's own wife; how much more so of the wife of his fellow. From here the sages said: Whenever a man talks too much with the woman, he brings evil upon himself, and neglects words of Torah, and in the end inherits Gehinnom.

Yose son-of Yochanan man-of Jerusalem says: Let-be your-house open to-breadth, and-let-be poor sons-of your-house, and-not multiply talk with the-woman. Of-his-wife they-said, light and-heavy of-wife-of his-fellow. Grammar
  • The mishnah has a saying of Yose (three imperatives) and then a comment in the voice of the sages ('they said', 'from here the sages said'). Bartenura notes that the comment is Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's, who edited the mishnayot.
  • קַל וָחֹמֶר is the argument a fortiori ('light and heavy'), here without a verb: 'how much more so'.
  • עַל / בְּ: בְּאִשְׁתּוֹ אָמְרוּ uses ב to mean 'concerning'; the verb is plural with no stated subject ('they said' = 'people said / the sages said').
  • אָדָם is 'a person', but the context is a man speaking with a woman; the English says 'a man'.
  • גּוֹרֵם, בּוֹטֵל and יוֹרֵשׁ are participles that state a general result: 'is causing, is neglecting, is inheriting'.
  • שִׂיחָה is the direct object of תַּרְבֶּה / מַרְבֶּה (Hifil of רבה, 'make much'): 'make much talk', i.e. talk too much; it is indefinite, so it has no אֶת.

הָאִשָּׁה is rendered 'the woman', with the article, because Bartenura reads the article as the reason the warning is first about one's own wife (בְּאִשְׁתּוֹ). Rambam says talk with women is mostly about sexual matters, and gives that as the reason; Bartenura gives the cases of Korach and the wife's contempt. The two do not conflict on the English, and the English does not choose between them. 'Neglects words of Torah' follows both commentators: he is drawn to idle things and wastes his time. 'Open wide' (לִרְוָחָה) is the wide-open house; Bartenura: open to the four directions, as Abraham's was.

1:6

Yehoshua son of Perachya and Nittai the Arbelite received from them. Yehoshua son of Perachya says: Make for yourself a teacher; and acquire for yourself a friend; and judge every person favorably.

Yehoshua son-of Perachya and-Nittai the-Arbelite received from-them. Yehoshua son-of Perachya says: Make for-you teacher, and-acquire for-you friend, and-be judging [acc.] all the-person to-scale-of merit.

Grammar
  • קִבְּלוּ is plural because there are two teachers, again named as a pair.
  • עֲשֵׂה and קְנֵה are imperatives of final-ה verbs; לְךָ repeated after each verb is an ethical dative ('for yourself').
  • וֶהֱוֵי דָן is the imperative of הָיָה with a participle: 'be one who judges', a continuing practice.
  • לְכַף זְכוּת is an idiom: the scale of a balance tipped toward merit; the English 'favorably' gives the sense.
  • The three commands are short and parallel, each with an object: רַב, חָבֵר, כָּל הָאָדָם.

'Make for yourself a teacher' is taken in Rambam's sense: treat him as your teacher even if he is not fit. Bartenura quotes Rambam and then gives his own hearing of the words, that one should take one regular teacher; it is recorded. 'Acquire' is kept: Rambam stresses that the verb is קנה 'buy', not 'make'. 'Judge every person favorably' is the idiom; both commentators limit it (for an unknown man; a known wicked man may be judged otherwise), but the saying is kept in its brevity.

Grammar
  • הַרְחֵק is a Hifil imperative: 'make yourself distant'.
  • Two prohibitions use אַל with the imperfect (Hitpael): אַל תִּתְחַבֵּר and אַל תִּתְיָאֵשׁ.
  • The three clauses are parallel and short; the saying moves from neighbor to companion to the wicked's fate.
  • הָאַרְבֵּלִי is a gentilic: 'the man of Arbel'.

  • The saying has two parallel temporal clauses with וּכְשֶׁ: 'when they stand ... when they depart'.

Grammar
  • אֱהֹב and שְׂנָא are two imperatives (Qal) with the object marker אֶת: two commands in parallel, then a prohibition.
  • רַבָּנוּת is a Mishnaic abstract noun from רַב: 'mastery, rabbinate'.

1:11

Avtalyon says: Sages, be careful with your words, lest you incur the liability of exile and be exiled to a place of evil waters, and the disciples who come after you drink and die, and so it turns out that the name of Heaven is profaned.

Avtalyon says sages be-careful in-your-words lest you-incur liability-of exile and-you-be-exiled to-place-of waters the-evil and-drink the-disciples the-coming after-you and-die and-is-found name-of Heaven being-profaned

Grammar
  • הִזָּהֲרוּ is a Nifal imperative plural: the נ of the Nifal is assimilated into the doubled ז.
  • After שֶׁמָּא 'lest' a chain of consequences follows: תָחוּבוּ, then four verbs joined by וְ (וְתִגְלוּ, וְיִשְׁתּוּ, וְיָמוּתוּ, וְנִמְצָא), and last the participle מִתְחַלֵּל.
  • חֲכָמִים is a vocative: 'O sages'.
  • תָחוּבוּ חוֹבַת is a cognate object, verb and noun from the root חוב ('incur a liability'); גָּלוּת וְתִגְלוּ then repeats the root גלה.
  • נִמְצָא plus participle: 'it turns out that (the name of Heaven) is being profaned'.
  • מַיִם הָרָעִים: figurative: the evil waters are heretical teachings that the disciples would 'drink'.

מַיִם הָרָעִים: the English keeps the metaphor and gives the sense in the note; Bartenura and Rambam both take the 'evil waters' as heresy (מִינוּת, אפיקורסות). Bartenura sees the exile as the way the sages' words fall among heretics, who mis-read them.

Grammar
  • הֱוֵי מִתַּלְמִידָיו שֶׁל אַהֲרֹן: מִ is partitive, 'one of'. תַּלְמִידָיו 'his disciples' carries a suffix that anticipates the possessor named by שֶׁל: 'his disciples, of Aaron', a common Mishnaic construction.

  • שָׁלוֹם is repeated: 'love peace and pursue peace': a pair of verbs of increasing effort.
  • הַבְּרִיּוֹת is 'creatures': here human beings generally, not only Israel; מְקָרְבָן has the feminine suffix -ן on 'them'.

הַבְּרִיּוֹת: literally 'the creatures'; rendered 'people' because the context (bringing them to the Torah) is of human beings. מְקָרְבָן: the suffix ן is a feminine plural form on a collective noun (בְּרִיּוֹת is feminine plural).

1:13

He used to say: One who spreads his name far loses his name; and one who does not add will cease; and one who does not learn deserves to die; and one who makes use of the crown passes away.

he was saying: drawing-out name, lost his-name. and-who-not adding, will-cease. and-who-not learning, death liable. and-who-makes-use of-crown, passes-away.

Grammar
  • This saying is in Aramaic, except for the opening formula הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר; the Aramaic relative is דְּ- (Hebrew שֶׁ-) and the emphatic ending is -ָא (שְׁמָא, קְטָלָא, תָּגָא).
  • Four short clauses, each a relative clause (a participle in the first three, the perfect אִשְׁתַּמֵּשׁ in the last) followed by its consequence; the first and the last are an inverted pair (name drawn out, name lost; crown used, one passes).
  • וּדְלֹא מוֹסִיף יָסֵף: a sound-play between two roots, מוֹסִיף from יסף 'add' and יָסֵף, which Bartenura reads as יָסוּף from סוף 'come to an end' (or, in his second reading, יֵאָסֵף 'be gathered (to his people)', from אסף): 'who does not add will cease'.
  • קְטָלָא חַיָּב: the adjective חַיָּב is predicate with the noun it governs, 'liable (to) death'.

נָגֵד is taken as 'draw out, extend', so one who stretches his name afar through power and the title of rabbi. Bartenura 1:13:3 gives a second reading, יֵאָסֵף ('be gathered to his people', i.e. die before his time); the English takes the first, יָסוּף ('cease'). In the last clause the English takes the first of Bartenura's explanations: the one who uses the crown of Torah as a tool. The jastrow lemma for 4 is a verb with a general sense ('draw a line') and the sense paths given for 2, 3, 15 and 17 are the nearest the pack offers.

1:14

He used to say: If I am not for myself, who is for me? And when I am for myself, what am I? And if not now, when?

he was saying: if not-there-is I for-me, who for-me. and-when I for-myself, what I. and-if not now, when.

Grammar
  • Three rhetorical questions, each with a conditional first half; the answer to each is understood.
  • אֵין אֲנִי לִי: אֵין negates the existence of the subject 'I' for the benefit 'to me'; the verbless clause has no verb 'to be'.
  • לִי comes twice in the same sense, 'for me': 'if I am not for me, who is for me?'. The English renders the first 'for myself' because its subject is also 'I'; לְעַצְמִי 'for my self' in the next clause sharpens it.
  • וּכְשֶׁאֲנִי לְעַצְמִי: כְּשֶׁ- is the Mishnaic 'when', compounded from כ + שֶׁ.
  • אֵימָתַי is the Mishnaic interrogative 'when', and עַכְשָׁיו the Mishnaic 'now'.

The English keeps the three clauses short and unpaired. 'Who is for me?' is left open: Bartenura reads it as 'who will earn merit for me' and Rambam as 'who will rouse my soul', so the English does not choose between them. אֵימָתַי is read by Bartenura as 'after death' and in the other explanation 'in old age'; the English leaves it as plain 'when'.

1:15

Shammai says: Make your Torah fixed. Say little and do much, and receive every person with a pleasant countenance.

Shammai saying: make your-Torah regular. say little and-do much, and-be receiving [object-marker] all-of the-person with-countenance-of faces pleasant.

Grammar
  • Three imperatives, each with a different construction: עֲשֵׂה and אֱמֹר are plain imperatives, וֶהֱוֵי with a participle gives a continuous 'be receiving'.
  • עֲשֵׂה תוֹרָתְךָ קֶבַע: a double accusative of result, 'make your Torah (into) something fixed'.
  • אֱמֹר מְעַט וַעֲשֵׂה הַרְבֵּה: an antithesis, with מְעַט and הַרְבֵּה as adverbs.
  • בְּסֵבֶר פָּנִים יָפוֹת: סֵבֶר is in the construct before פָּנִים; the adjective is feminine plural, as פָּנִים takes feminine adjectives.

קֶבַע is rendered 'fixed' in the sense of a permanent, not a passing, occupation. Bartenura also records a second reading: that you hold yourself to the same standard in your Torah as for others. סֵבֶר פָּנִים is a phrase for a look or expression of the face; 'pleasant countenance' is Rambam's 'gently and with welcome words'.

Grammar
  • Three sayings in one mishnah, with the same three-part structure: personal testimony, then a negative form (וְלֹא ... אֶלָּא), then a general rule.
  • וְלֹא מָצָאתִי לַגּוּף טוֹב אֶלָּא שְׁתִיקָה: a negated clause with אֶלָּא, 'nothing ... except'; לַגּוּף 'for the body' is the person.
  • וְלֹא הַמִּדְרָשׁ הוּא הָעִקָּר: the pronoun הוּא stands as copula between subject and predicate.
  • וְכָל הַמַּרְבֶּה: כָּל with the articular participle makes a general statement, 'every one who multiplies'.
  • The last clause echoes Proverbs 10:19, בְּרֹב דְּבָרִים לֹא יֶחְדַּל פֶּשַׁע, as Rambam notes.

לַגּוּף: 'body' is read as the person; Bartenura's gloss is the person who hears his own shame and is silent. The mishnah's מִדְרָשׁ is rendered 'study', and Rambam and Bartenura both read it as speech about Torah that must lead to action. The last clause is read by Rambam as about speech in general and by Rabbeinu Yonah as about Torah rulings; the English leaves the words general.

Grammar
  • עַל שְׁלשָׁה דְבָרִים הָעוֹלָם עוֹמֵד: the preposition עַל means 'upon' (the world rests on); the participle עוֹמֵד is 'stands, endures'.
  • The three items are each introduced with עַל and the article: הַדִּין, הָאֱמֶת, הַשָּׁלוֹם.
  • שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר introduces the verse (Zech 8:16) as a proof text; the mishnah reads the verse as naming the three things, truth, judgment, and peace.
  • אֱמֶת וּמִשְׁפַּט שָׁלוֹם שִׁפְטוּ: the two nouns are the objects of the plural imperative שִׁפְטוּ 'judge', put before it for emphasis; מִשְׁפַּט is in the construct, 'a judgment of peace'.
  • בְּשַׁעֲרֵיכֶם: 'in your gates', the place of the court.

הָעוֹלָם עוֹמֵד: Bartenura says that here 'the world' means the settlement of human beings, not the world of the previous mishnah (1:2). The English keeps 'the world'. Rambam reads the three things as the political order (justice), the intellectual virtues (truth), and the moral virtues (peace); this is recorded but not adopted. The verse is translated from the Hebrew of the mishnah, the three items answering to 'truth', 'judgment', 'peace'.

5:1

By ten utterances the world was created. What does the text teach in saying so? Could it not have been created by a single utterance? Rather, it was to exact punishment from the wicked, who destroy the world that was created by ten utterances, and to give good reward to the righteous, who sustain the world that was created by ten utterances.

in-ten utterances was-created the-world; and-what teaching to-say; and-is-it-not by-utterance one able to-be-created; but to-exact-punishment from the-wicked who-destroy [object-marker] the-world that-was-created in-ten utterances; and-to-give reward good to-the-righteous who-sustain [object-marker] the-world that-was-created in-ten utterances

Grammar
  • The saying has the form of a question and answer: 'what is the teaching... could it not...? but...'. וַהֲלֹא introduces a rhetorical question expecting 'yes'.
  • שֶׁמְּאַבְּדִין and שֶׁמְּקַיְּמִין are Piel participles with the mishnaic plural ending -ין; they describe habitual action ('who destroy', 'who sustain').
  • Two parallel clauses, each ending with the same refrain שֶׁנִּבְרָא בַעֲשָׂרָה מַאֲמָרוֹת: the wicked destroy, the righteous sustain, the same world.
  • לְהִפָּרַע (Nifal) is passive in form but means 'to exact payment'; compare לִתֵּן שָׂכָר, 'to give reward', its counterpart.

The Hebrew leaves the subject of 'What does the text teach' unstated; 'the text' (Genesis 1) is supplied because the question asks why Scripture says ten utterances. מַאֲמָרוֹת is rendered 'utterances' (the nine 'And God said' of Genesis 1 plus the opening word, per Bartenura and Rambam).

5:2

Ten generations from Adam to Noah, to make known how patient He is, for all the generations kept on provoking Him until He brought upon them the waters of the Flood. Ten generations from Noah to Abraham, to make known how patient He is, for all the generations kept on provoking Him until Abraham came and received upon himself the reward of them all.

Grammar
  • The mishnah is built on two parallel halves, each beginning with the number ten and each ending with a figure who changes the course: the Flood in the first, Abraham in the second.
  • מַכְעִיסִין וּבָאִין is a pair of participles: 'provoking and coming (on)', that is, they went on provoking.
  • אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם is the biblical idiom for patience (Exodus 34:6, 'slow to anger'); לְפָנָיו marks it as God's patience.
  • שֶׁהֵבִיא is a Hifil with the relative שֶׁ, 'that He brought'; עַד שֶׁ- 'until that' introduces the end of the long forbearance.
  • וְקִבֵּל עָלָיו: Piel of קבל; 'to receive upon oneself' means to take on as one's own, hence 'received the reward of them all'.

The Hebrew שְׂכַר כֻּלָּם is 'the reward of all of them'. Bartenura explains it as the reward Abraham earned by doing good in the measure the whole generations should have done; the English leaves the compressed wording, and the explanation is in the commentary.

5:3

Abraham our father, peace be upon him, was tested with ten trials and stood firm in all of them, to make known how great was the love of Abraham our father, peace be upon him.

ten trials was-tried Abraham our-father upon-him the-peace and-stood in-all-of-them; to-make-known how his-love of Abraham our-father upon-him the-peace

Grammar
  • נִסְיוֹנוֹת נִתְנַסָּה: the verb and its noun share a root (נסה): 'was tried with trials'.
  • עָלָיו הַשָּׁלוֹם is a formula of respect, 'peace be upon him', added after the name of Abraham in both halves of the mishnah.
  • חִבָּתוֹ שֶׁל אַבְרָהָם: the possessive is doubled, 'his love of Abraham', a mishnaic construction with שֶׁל instead of the construct state.
  • The Hebrew infinitive לְהוֹדִיעַ, 'to make known', has no stated subject; it is the purpose of the ten trials and of Abraham's standing firm.

חִבָּה: 'love, affection'. Rabbeinu Yonah reads the trials as revealing to people that Abraham feared God and was whole in all his traits, so the 'love' shown is Abraham's own devotion; the English keeps the construction 'the love of Abraham', which allows both readings.

Grammar
  • The mishnah has three parallel statements of ten: miracles in Egypt and at the sea, plagues in Egypt and at the sea, trials in the wilderness. The second sentence explains the first.
  • נִסִּים (miracles, from נסס) and נִסְיוֹנוֹת (trials, from נסה) come from different roots but sound alike; the mishnah plays on the likeness: God's miracles, the people's testing.
  • In the first two sentences the second 'ten' is elliptical: וַעֲשָׂרָה עַל הַיָּם, 'and ten at the sea', the noun is understood from the first half.
  • הֵבִיא ... עַל is the idiom 'to bring upon' (a plague); הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא is the subject, separated from the verb.
  • The proof text is Numbers 14:22, וַיְנַסּוּ אֹתִי זֶה עֶשֶׂר פְּעָמִים, introduced by שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר 'as it is said'. The parenthesized (במדבר יד) is the printed edition's reference, not the mishnah's; Vilna adds the verse number כב.

הַמָּקוֹם, of God, is rendered 'the Omnipresent', with the literal sense 'the Place' here recorded. The verse is quoted from the Hebrew of the mishnah, not from any translation. 'Plagues' renders מַכּוֹת, 'blows'.

Grammar
  • The mishnah opens with the heading 'ten miracles' and lists ten items. The first begins with לֹא, the others with וְלֹא, 'and not', except the eighth (standing pressed together and bowing with room), which is stated positively, one miracle in two halves.
  • הִפִּילָה (Hifil of נפל) is literally 'caused to fall'; with אִשָּׁה as the subject the sense is miscarriage. The verb is feminine singular because the subject is.
  • קֶרִי is the legal term for a seminal emission; the high priest on the Day of Atonement could not be unclean.
  • עוֹמְדִים צְפוּפִים וּמִשְׁתַּחֲוִים רְוָחִים is a contrast of two participles with adverbial adjectives: the miracle is that they were crowded when standing and spacious when bowing.
  • The last item quotes direct speech: צַר לִי הַמָּקוֹם, 'the place is too narrow for me', an echo of Isaiah 49:20, where הַמָּקוֹם is not God but the physical place; here the same word is 'the place'.
  • שֶׁאָלִין is the relative שֶׁ with the Qal imperfect 1st person singular of לין, 'to spend the night, lodge': 'that I should lodge'. Bartenura notes the variant כְּשֶׁאָלִין, 'when I lodge'.

צְפוּפִים: the plain sense is 'crowded, pressed together' (Rambam: each stood beside his fellow). Bartenura derives it from צף 'floating' and says the crowd was so dense their feet were lifted from the ground; the English keeps the plain sense and records Bartenura's. רְוָחִים: Bartenura says each worshipper had four cubits of room, so as not to hear his fellow confessing; Rambam says they did not press upon each other at the prostration. The English says 'with room to spare', which agrees with both. The Hebrew of the last item has no object for הִזִּיק; 'anyone' is supplied. The subject of 'they stood pressed together' is the people in the Temple court.

Grammar
  • The mishnah lists ten created things in an elliptical series of nouns after the heading 'and these are they'; each after the first is joined with וְ-. No verb is repeated.
  • בֵּין הַשְּׁמָשׁוֹת, literally 'between the suns', is the idiom for twilight; the mishnah means twilight of the first Friday evening, at the close of creation.
  • וְיֵשׁ אוֹמְרִים, 'and there are those who say', is the mishnaic form for an anonymous alternative opinion; here it adds three further items, then another.
  • The Hebrew uses שֶׁל ('of') for possession in קְבוּרָתוֹ שֶׁל מֹשֶׁה and אֵילוֹ שֶׁל אַבְרָהָם: the suffix on the noun is doubled by the phrase with שֶׁל.
  • צְבָת בִּצְבָת עֲשׂוּיָה gives the reason of those who say it: tongs are made with tongs, so the first tongs must have been made by Heaven. Bartenura adds that the Gemara (Pesachim 54) answers that the first could have been cast in a mould.

The ten are: the mouth of the earth, the mouth of the well, the mouth of the donkey, the bow, the manna, the staff, the shamir, the script, the inscription, the tablets; the other lists add the demons, the burial of Moses, Abraham's ram, and the tongs. The pack's lexicon links וְהַמָּן to מִן; the manna is meant. הַכְּתָב and הַמִּכְתָּב: Bartenura takes the script as the form of the letters engraved on the tablets and the inscription as the writing readable from all four sides; Rambam reads the script as the Torah written before God and the inscription as the writing on the tablets. The English 'script' and 'inscription' keeps both. הַמַּזִּיקִין is rendered 'the demons' after Bartenura (the Hebrew is literally 'the harmful ones').

5:7

Grammar
  • שִׁבְעָה דְבָרִים: the numeral שִׁבְעָה (feminine form) stands with the masculine noun, as is usual for the numbers 3 to 10.
  • The seven traits of the wise person are a string of present-tense clauses built on participles (מְדַבֵּר, נִכְנָס, נִבְהָל, שׁוֹאֵל, מֵשִׁיב, אוֹמֵר, מוֹדֶה); the negative is אֵינוֹ + participle.
  • The relative שֶׁ appears twice: מִי שֶׁהוּא גָדוֹל ('one who is great') and מַה שֶּׁלֹּא שָׁמַע ('what he did not hear').
  • The doubling רִאשׁוֹן רִאשׁוֹן ... אַחֲרוֹן אַחֲרוֹן is distributive: each item in its own place.
  • The mishnah gives a list of seven, then closes with the rule וְחִלּוּפֵיהֶן בַּגֹּלֶם: the opposite of each trait belongs to the golem.

גֹּלֶם is kept as 'the unformed person' with the literal sense (an unfinished vessel) in the token note; it is not 'golem' of later legend. חָכָם is 'the wise person', the type, not תַּלְמִיד חָכָם. הֲלָכָה is 'the law' (the settled rule). מִנְיָן is left as 'number': years, numbers of disciples and numbers of those who study are all possible, and neither Bartenura nor Rambam decides it. The lexicon entry offered for שִׁבְעָה (Shebah) is the wrong homograph; the word is the numeral.

5:8

Seven kinds of calamity come to the world for seven principal transgressions. When some of them tithe and some do not tithe, a famine of drought comes: some go hungry and some are sated. When they have resolved not to tithe, a famine of tumult and of drought comes. And for not taking the dough-offering, a famine of annihilation comes. Pestilence comes to the world for the death penalties stated in the Torah that were not delivered to the court, and for the produce of the seventh year. Grammar
  • The mishnah is built on a refrain: 'X comes to the world for Y'. The verb is the participle throughout: בָּאִין (masculine plural), בָּאָה (feminine singular) and בָּא (5:8.41, masculine singular with דֶּבֶר). בָּא has the same letters as the perfect, but the parallel clauses show it is the participle.
  • שֶׁל is the genitive particle of Mishnaic Hebrew (רָעָב שֶׁל בַּצֹּרֶת 'a famine of drought'), where biblical Hebrew would use the construct state.
  • מִקְצָתָן ... וּמִקְצָתָן: 'some of them ... and some of them', a distributive pair; מִקְצָתָן is מִן + קֶצֶת + suffix.
  • The conditions of the first three famines are elliptical: 'some tithe and some do not', 'they resolved not to tithe', 'and not to take the dough-offering' (the verb of the earlier clause is carried over). The English fills in only what the verb requires.
  • הַמּוֹרִים is a Hifil participle of ירה, 'teach, direct' (the root of תּוֹרָה); the line plays on the word 'Torah'.

פֻרְעָנוּת is 'calamity'; גּוּפֵי עֲבֵרָה is 'principal transgressions' (literally 'bodies of transgression'). The mishnah's list is elliptical; the English does not spell out a conditional for the dough-offering clause ('And for not taking the dough-offering'). The pack's first lexicon entry for בָּא is Jastrow 'בָּא I' (father), a wrong homograph; the tokens are assigned to Jastrow 'בָּא III' (the participle of בוא, 'coming'). The bdbs entries for בַּיִת (a place name), שִׁבְעָה (Shebah) and תּוֹרָה ('law of man') are likewise wrong; Jastrow's entries are used.

Grammar
  • This mishnah has two parts: a second list of calamities (wild beast, exile), then the four times at which pestilence increases, each with its reason.
  • חַיָּה רָעָה, 'a wild beast', is a noun with an attributive adjective; the participle בָאָה agrees with the feminine noun.
  • עוֹבְדֵי עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה: a participle in the construct state, 'worshippers of foreign worship'.
  • The four times are given first in a bare list (בָּרְבִיעִית, וּבַשְּׁבִיעִית ...), then each is explained with מִפְּנֵי 'because of' (literally 'from the face of'). The repeated forms have the preposition בְּ + the feminine ordinal as a noun ('in the fourth', 'in the seventh'), with 'year' understood.
  • וְשָׁנָה: 'every year and year', a doubling that makes the phrase distributive: every single year.

'The Name' (הַשֵּׁם) stands for God; no decision on this phrase, so the Hebrew word is kept in English capitalised. עֲרָיוֹת is rendered 'forbidden sexual relations' (literally 'nakednesses'). הַשְׁמָטַת הָאָרֶץ is 'the release of the land'; the English adds 'violating' following Bartenura (they plough and sow in the sabbatical year). The 'fourth' and 'seventh' are years of the seven-year sabbatical cycle, supplied from the explanation that follows (the poor man's tithe falls in the third and sixth years). The bdbs entries for מוֹצָא (Moza, a personal name), מַתָּנָה (Mattanah), שֵׁם (Shem) and שָׁנָה (the verb 'repeat') are wrong homographs; the tokens are assigned to Jastrow's מוֹצָא III, מַתָּנָה and שֵׁם II and to bdbs:שָׁנֶה 'year'.

5:10

There are four dispositions among people. One who says, "What is mine is mine, and what is yours is yours": this is a middling disposition; and some say, this is the disposition of Sodom. "What is mine is yours, and what is yours is mine": an unlearned person. "What is mine is yours, and what is yours is yours": a pious person. "What is mine is mine, and what is yours is mine": a wicked person.

four dispositions in-the-person. the-saying mine mine and-yours yours, this disposition middling; and-there-are saying, this disposition-of Sodom. mine yours and-yours mine, people-of the-land. mine yours and-yours yours, pious. mine mine and-yours mine, wicked.

Grammar
  • The four dispositions are given as formulas with no verb: שֶׁלִּי שֶׁלִּי 'mine (is) mine'. שֶׁל + suffix (שֶׁלִּי, שֶׁלְּךָ) is the Mishnaic possessive 'of me, of you', the equivalent of 'mine, yours'.
  • The clauses are nominal sentences: subject 'mine', predicate 'yours' or 'mine'; the English supplies 'What is ... is ...'.
  • וְיֵשׁ אוֹמְרִים, 'and some say', introduces a dissenting opinion on the first disposition; the three forms of זוֹ (feminine singular demonstrative) agree with מִדָּה.
  • The first disposition is a middle one and the other three are the extremes: 'unlearned' (generous in exchange), 'pious' (generous without return), 'wicked' (takes both).

עַם הָאָרֶץ is rendered 'an unlearned person', as decided; the literal sense is 'people of the land'. Bartenura connects the title with the settling of the land: he seeks the land's good order but has no wisdom to distinguish the proper ways. Rambam does not comment on the title here; at 5:7 he defines the am ha'aretz as one who has good conduct but no learning, fit for the settling of the land. מִדָּה is 'disposition', not 'trait', to allow 'a middling disposition' and 'the disposition of Sodom'. The bdbs entries for מִדָּה ('tribute') and אָדָם (the name Adam) are wrong homographs; the tokens are assigned to Jastrow's מִדָּה ('measure') and אָדָם ('man').

5:11

There are four dispositions in temperaments. Easy to anger and easy to appease: his gain is outweighed by his loss. Hard to anger and hard to appease: his loss is outweighed by his gain. Hard to anger and easy to appease: a pious person. Easy to anger and hard to appease: a wicked person.

Grammar
  • The adjectives נוֹחַ 'easy' and קָשֶׁה 'hard' take the infinitive with לְ: נוֹחַ לִכְעֹס 'easy to be angry'. The two infinitives are of Qal כעס and רצה.
  • יָצָא שְׂכָרוֹ בְהֶפְסֵדוֹ is an accounting idiom: the reward 'goes out' (is set off) against the loss. Bartenura reads it so that in the first case the loss is greater, and in the second the gain is greater.
  • The four dispositions are four combinations of two pairs: quick or slow to anger, quick or slow to be appeased. The 'mixed' cases get an evaluation (gain/loss); the other two get titles (pious, wicked).
  • The titles חָסִיד and רָשָׁע are in the same position as in 5:10; they are predicate nouns with no verb.

יָצָא שְׂכָרוֹ בְהֶפְסֵדוֹ is rendered 'his gain is outweighed by his loss', following Bartenura's reading of which side is larger: in the first case the loss, in the second the gain. Bartenura notes that some read the opposite order (וְאִית דְּגַרְסֵי אִפְּכָא) and states that he prefers his reading; the English follows him. 'Temperaments' for דֵּעוֹת is chosen from the context: Rambam speaks of the quality of anger. The bdbs entries for דֵּעָה ('knowledge (of God)'), נוֹחַ (Jastrow's noun 'rest'), כַּעַס (the noun 'anger') and שָׂכָר (the name Sacar) are replaced by Jastrow's דֵּעָה, נוֹחַ II 'easy', the verb כָּעַס and שָׂכָר 'wages, reward'.

5:12

There are four dispositions in disciples. Quick to hear and quick to forget: his gain is outweighed by his loss. Hard to hear and hard to forget: his loss is outweighed by his gain. Quick to hear and hard to forget: a wise person. Hard to hear and quick to forget: this is a bad lot.

Grammar
  • The adjectives מַהֵר ('quick') and קָשֶׁה ('hard') take the infinitive with לְ, as in 5:11. The infinitive לְאַבֵּד is Piel of אבד ('make lost'), used for losing what one has learned.
  • The wording of the first two cases (יָצָא שְׂכָרוֹ בְהֶפְסֵדוֹ ...) repeats 5:11, as does the pattern of four combinations.
  • The last two cases do not give a character label but one a skill word (חָכָם) and one a lot in life (חֵלֶק רָע), because, as Bartenura and Rambam say, these are not matters of choice.
  • The demonstrative זֶה is the subject of the last clause, with חֵלֶק רָע as predicate: 'this is a bad lot'.

לְאַבֵּד is rendered 'to forget' (literally 'to lose'), which is the Mishnaic sense of letting something go from memory. חֵלֶק רָע is 'a bad lot' (literally 'an evil portion'): Bartenura and Rambam both say the fault is not within a person's choice. The words חָסִיד and רָשָׁע are not used here for that reason. חָכָם here is 'a wise person', not the compound 'disciple of the sages'; Rambam names it an intellectual virtue. The bdbs entries for חֵלֶק (the name Helek), זֶה ('lamb') and שָׂכָר (the name Sacar) are wrong homographs; Jastrow's entries are used.

Grammar
  • מִדּוֹת here means 'types, patterns'; the series 'four types' recurs in 5:14-15 (and 5:10-12).
  • The participle הָרוֹצֶה with the relative שֶׁ is a headless relative: 'the one who wants that ...'.
  • The imperfects יִתֵּן / יִתְּנוּ after שֶׁ express what is wanted; after the first clause the verb 'wants' is understood.
  • עֵינוֹ רָעָה, 'his eye is evil', is the idiom for grudgingness; בְּשֶׁל is the Mishnaic genitive 'in that which is of'.
  • The four types are given as bare predicates (חָסִיד, רָשָׁע): the first two have an evil eye, the third is pious, the fourth wicked.

The mishnah gives the first two types no label beyond 'evil eye'; the English keeps this. In the second, third and fourth types the Hebrew has bare imperfects, and הָרוֹצֶה is understood from the first clause. The English supplies 'wants' in all three, as Rambam reads the third (the pious man is not content that he alone shows mercy but wants others to show it too). The pack's BDB entries for אַרְבַּע (Arba, a name), מִדָּה (tribute), עַיִן (Ain, a place) and אַחַר (the adverb 'after') are the wrong words. 5:14

There are four types in going to the house of study. One who goes but does not do: the reward of going is in his hand. One who does but does not go: the reward of doing is in his hand. One who goes and does is pious. One who neither goes nor does is wicked.

four types in-those-going to-house-of the-study; going and-not-he doing, reward-of going in-his-hand; doing and-not-he going, reward-of deed in-his-hand; going and-doing, pious; not going and-not doing, wicked

Grammar
  • Pattern as in 5:13: four types, each defined by a pair of participles (הוֹלֵךְ, עוֹשֶׂה); the participles are subjects ('one who ...').
  • וְאֵינוֹ is אֵין with a 3rd masculine singular suffix: 'and he is not'; it negates the participle that follows.
  • הֲלִיכָה (a Mishnaic verbal noun, 'going') is contrasted with מַעֲשֶׂה, 'deed, doing'.
  • שְׂכַר ... בְיָדוֹ: 'the reward ... is in his hand', i.e. he holds it, it is his.

The English keeps 'does' because the Hebrew word is עוֹשֶׂה. Bartenura (normative) reads 'does' in the first clause as 'learns, reviews and understands' (the one who goes to hear but does not study) and the second as one who studies at home; Rabbeinu Yonah (not normative) reads it as practising the commandments, the first as one who does not seek them out. The English leaves the word open; the reading is recorded rather than adopted.

5:15

There are four types among those who sit before the sages: a sponge, a funnel, a strainer, and a sieve. A sponge, because it absorbs everything. A funnel, because it takes in through this and lets out through that. A strainer, because it lets the wine out and retains the dregs. four types in-those-sitting before sages: sponge, and-funnel, strainer, and-sieve; sponge, that-it absorbing [obj] the-all; funnel, that-bringing-in in-this and-sending-out in-that; strainer, that-sending-out [obj] the-wine and-retaining [obj] the-dregs; and-sieve, that-sending-out [obj] the-meal and-retaining [obj] the-choice-flour

Grammar
  • The four types are first listed as nouns (סְפוֹג, מַשְׁפֵּךְ, מְשַׁמֶּרֶת, נָפָה) and then each is explained by a clause with שֶׁ ('because it ...').
  • The explanations use participles (סוֹפֵג, מַכְנִיס, מוֹצִיא, קוֹלֶטֶת) to describe a habitual action.
  • בְּזוֹ ... בְזוֹ, 'in this ... in that', is the Mishnaic use of זוֹ (feminine) as demonstrative pronoun; the antecedent is the two openings (or ends) of the funnel.
  • אֶת marks the definite objects of מוֹצִיאָה and קוֹלֶטֶת; it has no English counterpart.
  • מְשַׁמֶּרֶת (filter) and נָפָה (sieve) are fem. nouns, hence the fem. participles מוֹצִיאָה, קוֹלֶטֶת.

The mishnah gives only the utensils; the commentators (Bartenura, Rambam) supply the application to the disciple: the sponge cannot tell truth from falsehood, the funnel forgets, the strainer keeps the worthless, the sieve keeps the valued. The English keeps the images without the application. On קֶמַח and סֹלֶת: for Bartenura and Rambam the קֶמַח that passes through the sieve is the fine powder and the סֹלֶת kept is the coarse, choice part, so the English says 'fine meal' and 'choice flour'. The 'this/that' of the funnel is rendered literally.

Any love that depends on a thing: when the thing ceases, the love ceases. And that which does not depend on a thing never ceases. Which is the love that depends on a thing? This is the love of Amnon and Tamar. And that which does not depend on a thing? This is the love of David and Jonathan.

Grammar
  • The first saying is a conditional without a particle: בָּטֵל דָּבָר, בְּטֵלָה אַהֲבָה, '(when) the thing ceases, the love ceases'. Each predicate stands before its subject.
  • בְּטֵלָה is the feminine singular of the stative participle (verbal adjective) בָּטֵל, 'ceasing, void', and agrees with אַהֲבָה. The feminine perfect would be בָּטְלָה. The masculine בָּטֵל agrees with דָּבָר; it can be read as the same participle or as the stative perfect 'has ceased'.
  • וְשֶׁאֵינָהּ is 'and that which is not' (ש + אֵין + 3rd fem. sing. suffix): the headless relative stands for 'a love which ...'.
  • Question and answer: אֵיזוֹ הִיא ... זוֹ ..., 'which is ...? This is ...'; אֵיזוֹ is the feminine of אֵיזֶה, 'which'.
  • אַהֲבַת is the construct of אַהֲבָה: 'love of'.

דָּבָר is kept as 'a thing' because it is general: the commentators explain it as the cause or object on which the love depends (beauty in Amnon's case; a lasting purpose for David and Jonathan). The pack's BDB entry for זֶה ('lamb') is the wrong word, so the demonstratives point to Jastrow's זֶה, 'this'. For בָּטֵל, Jastrow's sense 2 ('to cease to exist') is the right one; Jastrow cites this mishnah there. The BDB entry for תָּמָר has David's daughter at sense 4.

5:17

all dispute that-it for-sake-of Heaven, its-end to-endure; and-that-not-it for-sake-of Heaven, not its-end to-endure; which it dispute that-it for-sake-of Heaven, this dispute-of Hillel and-Shammai; and-that-not-it for-sake-of Heaven, this dispute-of Korah and-all his-company

Grammar
  • סוֹפָהּ לְהִתְקַיֵּם: 'its end is to endure/be established'; the infinitive with לְ gives the outcome ('is destined to'), and סוֹף 'end' can mean outcome or goal.
  • Negative counterpart: אֵין סוֹפָהּ לְהִתְקַיֵּם, 'it is not its end to endure'; אֵין negates the nominal clause.
  • לְשֵׁם שָׁמַיִם, 'for the name/sake of Heaven': 'Heaven' is the Mishnaic reverent name for God.
  • מַחֲלֹקֶת (Vilna מחלוקת) is a Mishnaic feminine noun, 'dispute', from the root of 'division'; the pack's BDB entry glosses it as 'division, course', the nearest sense.
  • Question and answer: אֵיזוֹ הִיא ... זוֹ ...; the second case reuses the headless relative וְשֶׁאֵינָהּ.

סוֹפָהּ is rendered 'in the end' so that the English keeps the ambiguity the commentators discuss: for Bartenura's first reading the disputants endure (Hillel's and Shammai's disciples did not perish, Korah's company did); in his second reading the aim of the dispute, the truth, is what endures. The English 'will in the end endure' allows both. Korah's dispute: Numbers 16.

Grammar
  • זִכָּה (Piel/causative of זָכָה) 'made meritorious, caused to have merit' contrasts with the Qal זָכָה 'was meritorious': Moses was meritorious and made others so.
  • הַמַּחֲטִיא (Hiphil of חָטָא) 'one who causes to sin' parallels הַמְזַכֶּה; both are participles with the article used as 'whoever'.
  • אֵין מַסְפִּיקִין בְּיָדוֹ is an impersonal plural: 'they do not enable him' (Heaven does not supply him the means); the verb is Hiphil of ספק, 'supply, suffice', here 'give the opportunity'.
  • תָּלוּי בּוֹ, 'hangs on him', means 'depends on him / is credited to him'; it is the passive participle of תָּלָה.
  • The two proof-texts are Deuteronomy 33:21 and 1 Kings 15:30; the Hebrew's brackets give the chapter (and the optional name 'son of Nebat'). The Vilna text has the references in the margin as כא and ל.

'Merit' (זְכוּת) is kept as one English word through the mishnah for the pun on זָכָה / זִכָּה. תָּלוּי בּוֹ is rendered 'rests on him' to keep the sense 'depends on, is attributed to'; 'hangs' is given in the literal line. The impersonal 'they do not give him the means' is kept as an impersonal plural, as in the Hebrew. The pack lists no entry for the verb חָטָא or for the Hiphil of ספק, so those tokens carry no sense.

5:19

Whoever has these three things is of the disciples of Abraham our father; and three other things, of the disciples of Balaam the wicked. A good eye, a humble spirit, and a modest soul: of the disciples of Abraham our father. An evil eye, a haughty spirit, and a broad soul: of the disciples of Balaam the wicked. What is the difference between the disciples of Abraham our father and the disciples of Balaam the wicked? The disciples of Abraham our father eat in this world and inherit in the world to come, as it is said: "To bequeath to those who love me substance, and I will fill their treasuries." But the disciples of Balaam the wicked inherit Gehinnom and go down to the pit of destruction, as it is said: "And You, God, will bring them down to the pit of destruction; men of blood and deceit shall not live out half their days; but I will trust in You."

Grammar
  • The mishnah first states the matter in general terms (whoever has these three things) and then names the things; Rabbeinu Yonah draws from this that these three things are the whole of what makes one a disciple of Abraham or of Balaam.
  • The two lists are exact opposites, member for member: good eye / evil eye, lowly spirit / high spirit, low soul / broad soul. The two sets are given in parallel clauses with no verb: 'a good eye ... of the disciples of Abraham'.
  • מִתַּלְמִידָיו שֶׁל is the Mishnaic genitive with שֶׁל ('of') and a pronominal suffix anticipating the owner: lit. 'from his disciples, of Abraham'. The מִ is partitive.
  • The two proof texts are Prov 8:21 and Ps 55:24. In the second the verse is cut after וַאֲנִי אֶבְטַח בָּךְ, which does not describe Balaam's disciples but ends the verse as written.
  • Aramaic-style plural participles in -ין (אוֹכְלִין, נוֹחֲלִין, יוֹרְשִׁין, יוֹרְדִין) are normal Mishnaic Hebrew; the mishnah is Hebrew, not Aramaic.

Translation choices. עַיִן טוֹבָה / רָעָה are kept as the literal 'good eye' / 'evil eye', with the commentators' definitions (Bartenura, Rambam: contentment vs. greed) given in the notes. רוּחַ נְמוּכָה 'humble spirit', נֶפֶשׁ שְׁפָלָה 'modest soul' (Bartenura: restraint and abstinence from desires; Rambam: restraint (זְהִירוּת), above all in sexual matters), נֶפֶשׁ רְחָבָה 'broad soul' (great craving). אֹהֲבַי is 'those who love me' (Bartenura reads the verse as naming Abraham 'the lover', Isa 41:8). בְּאֵר שַׁחַת is 'the pit of destruction'; שַׁחַת is both 'pit' and 'destruction/corruption'. Gehinnom is left in Hebrew form per the decisions.

5:20

Judah son of Tema says: Be bold as a leopard, light as an eagle, swift as a deer, and strong as a lion, to do the will of your Father in Heaven. He used to say: The bold-faced to Gehinnom, and the shamefaced to the Garden of Eden. Grammar
  • The four animal similes are parallel, each of the form adjective + כְּ + animal: bold / leopard, light / eagle, swift / deer, strong / lion. A single purpose phrase, 'to do the will of your Father in Heaven', governs all four.
  • The second saying is an elliptical antithesis with no verb: 'bold of face (goes) to Gehinnom, shame of face to the Garden of Eden'. עַז פָּנִים and בֹּשֶׁת פָּנִים are construct chains: עַז is the adjective 'strong, bold' in the construct ('bold of face'), בֹּשֶׁת the noun 'shame' in the construct.
  • יְהִי רָצוֹן מִלְּפָנֶיךָ is the standard petition formula ('may it be Your will') and shifts the form to second person address to God: 'Your city, Your Torah'.
  • שֶׁתִּבְנֶה is a purpose/content clause: 'that You build'. In the Mishnaic petition, the verb is in the imperfect after the formula; חֶלְקֵנוּ בְתוֹרָתֶךָ 'our portion in Your Torah' is the second request.
  • The words עַז כַּנָּמֵר, וְקַל כַּנֶּשֶׁר, וְרָץ כַּצְּבִי, וְגִבּוֹר כָּאֲרִי pair a trait with its image; the first plays on עַז as both 'fierce' and 'brazen' (cf. עַז פָּנִים below).

עַז פָּנִים is rendered 'bold-faced' and בֹּשֶׁת פָּנִים 'shamefaced' to keep the paired construct chains; the sense is 'the brazen' and 'the bashful'. Rambam and Bartenura read the first saying as boldness in learning and in rebuke for the sake of heaven; the second as the condemned shamelessness. The Tetragrammaton is rendered 'Lord'.

he was saying: son-of five years to-Scripture, son-of ten to-Mishnah, son-of thirteen to-the-commandments, son-of fifteen to-Talmud, son-of eighteen to-the-canopy, son-of twenty to-pursue, son-of thirty to-strength, son-of forty to-understanding, son-of fifty to-counsel, son-of sixty to-old-age, son-of seventy to-grey-hair, son-of eighty to-might, son-of ninety to-stoop, son-of hundred as-if dead and-passed and-ceased from-the-world.

Grammar
  • Each stage is an elliptical clause of the form 'son of N (years) to X': the verb 'is fit' is understood. The ל prefix is 'for' (fit for, ready for).
  • The ages count by tens from twenty to a hundred; בֶּן שָׁנִים is a fixed idiom for age. Gender of the numerals follows the noun they do not name: שָׁנָה (feminine) takes חָמֵשׁ, עֶשֶׂר, שְׁלשׁ עֶשְׂרֵה, etc.
  • Each ל-noun is a stage of life or a pursuit: מִקְרָא (Bible), מִשְׁנָה (oral teaching), מִצְוֹת (commandments), תַּלְמוּד (study), חֻפָּה (marriage), רדף (pursuit), כֹּחַ (strength), בִּינָה, עֵצָה, זִקְנָה, שֵׂיבָה, גְּבוּרָה, שׁוּחַ.
  • The last clause is a full sentence: כְּאִלּוּ מֵת וְעָבַר וּבָטֵל מִן הָעוֹלָם, with three Qal perfects (מֵת is the perfect of the stative מות): 'as if he had died and passed and ceased from the world'.

Translated with the plain sense of each stage. Bartenura derives the first stages from the law of orlah (Lev 19:23-25) and the Levites (Num 8). For לִרְדֹּף I keep 'pursuit' and record both of his readings (livelihood; or being pursued by the heavenly court) in the notes. לָשׁוּחַ is 'stooping' (Bartenura: walking bent and bowed).

Grammar
  • The saying mixes Hebrew and Aramaic: הֲפֹךְ בָּהּ וַהֲפֹךְ בָּהּ is Hebrew; דְּכֹלָּא בָהּ וּבָהּ תֶּחֱזֵי וְסִיב וּבְלֵה בָהּ וּמִנַּהּ לֹא תָזוּעַ is Aramaic; שֶׁאֵין לְךָ מִדָּה טוֹבָה הֵימֶנָּה is Hebrew again.
  • The feminine בָּהּ refers back to the Torah, as the commentators say (the mishnah does not name it: it follows the previous saying).
  • The imperatives הֲפֹךְ, סִיב, בְּלֵה are paired: turn it over and over; grow old and wear out in it.
  • The negation לֹא תָזוּעַ is a prohibition in Aramaic style ('do not move'); the final clause gives the reason with שֶׁאֵין לְךָ 'for you have not'.
  • מִדָּה 'measure, quality'; the English 'quality' also allows 'virtue' or 'standard'.

The pronoun 'it' is the Torah (Bartenura, Rambam). The Aramaic words are translated without comment in the English; the notes and grammar record where the Aramaic begins and ends.

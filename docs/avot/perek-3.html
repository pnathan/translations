3:1

Akavya son of Mahalalel says: Consider three things and you will not come to the point of transgression. Know where you came from, and where you are going, and before whom you are destined to give account and reckoning. Where you came from: from a putrid drop. And where you are going: to a place of dust, maggot and worm. And before whom you are destined to give account and reckoning: before the King of the kings of kings, the Holy One, blessed be He.

Grammar
  • The saying has the form of a question and an answer: one imperative, דַּע 'know', governs three questions, and each is then answered in turn.
  • אִי אַתָּה בָא is the Mishnaic negative with a participle (a future: 'you will not come'); אִי stands for אֵין.
  • עָתִיד is an adjective (the pattern qatil), 'ready, destined', and takes the infinitive לִתֵּן: 'you are destined to give'.
  • מֶלֶךְ מַלְכֵי הַמְּלָכִים is a double superlative ('King of the kings of kings'): the construct chain piles up the title.
  • מֵאַיִן, וּלְאָן and וְלִפְנֵי מִי are three interrogative phrases, each repeated in the answer; the third answer reuses the whole question.

דִּין וְחֶשְׁבּוֹן is rendered 'account and reckoning' (literally 'judgment and account'): the pair is a fixed idiom for a full accounting, and Bartenura reads the third item as the judgment before the King. Bartenura (3:1:1) reads the three items as three remedies: the putrid drop against pride, dust and worm against desire for wealth, the account against sin. The adjective סְרוּחָה is 'putrid'; Bartenura explains that the drop is called putrid because it is about to spoil once outside the womb.

3:2

Rabbi Hanina, the deputy of the priests, says: Pray for the welfare of the government, for were it not for the fear of it, each man would have swallowed his fellow alive. Rabbi Hanina son of Teradion says: Two who sit with no words of Torah between them, this is a seat of scoffers, as it is said (Psalm 1): "and in the seat of scoffers he has not sat." But two who sit with words of Torah between them, the Shekhinah dwells among them, as it is said (Malachi 3): "Then those who fear the LORD spoke, each man to his fellow, and the LORD listened and heard, and a book of remembrance was written before him for those who fear the LORD and for those who esteem his name." I know this only of two. From where do we learn that even for one who sits and occupies himself with Torah, the Holy One, blessed be He, sets a reward for him? As it is said (Lamentations 3): "He sits alone and murmurs, for he has taken it upon himself."

Grammar
  • The verse has three sayings: Rabbi Hanina the deputy on prayer for the government, Rabbi Hanina son of Teradion on Torah study in company, and its extension to one who studies alone.
  • שֶׁאִלְמָלֵא introduces a contrafactual clause; the apodosis is the perfect בְּלָעוֹ (בָּלַע with the suffix -וֹ, resuming רֵעֵהוּ): 'would have swallowed him'.
  • שְׁנַיִם שֶׁיּוֹשְׁבִין וְאֵין בֵּינֵיהֶן ... is a casus pendens: 'Two who sit and there is not between them ... this is ...'; הֲרֵי זֶה opens the main clause.
  • וְיֵשׁ and וְאֵין are the existential particles ('there is' and 'there is not') followed by a subject in the construct: 'words of Torah'.
  • מִנַּיִן is the standard Talmudic question, 'from where (do we learn)'; the answers are verses, quoted by their source.

סְגַן הַכֹּהֲנִים is 'the deputy of the priests'; the English keeps the title. מַלְכוּת is 'the government' (the sages read the ruling power, including foreign rule: Bartenura 3:2:1). The sentence 'each man would have swallowed his fellow alive' (Bartenura 3:2:2 cites Habakkuk 1:14, 'like the fish of the sea') is a contrafactual. The last verse, נָטַל עָלָיו, is read in the Mishnah as 'he has taken it upon himself': Bartenura 3:2:5 reads 'as though the whole giving of the Torah were for him alone'; וְיִדֹּם is rendered 'murmurs': Bartenura (3:2:4) and Rambam (3:2:2) read it as the soft voice of one who studies in a whisper; Rabbeinu Yonah reads silent study in thought. In Lamentations 3:28 the verse is more likely 'Let him sit alone and be silent, for he has laid it upon him' (God has laid it on him).

Rabbi Shimon says: Three who ate at one table and did not say words of Torah over it, it is as if they ate of sacrifices to the dead, as it is said (Isaiah 28): "For all the tables are full of vomit and filth, with no place." But three who ate at one table and said words of Torah over it, it is as if they ate from the table of the Omnipresent, blessed be He, as it is said (Ezekiel 41): "And he spoke to me: This is the table that is before the LORD."

Grammar
  • The saying sets two sentences in opposition: three who ate and did not speak words of Torah, and three who ate and did (אֲבָל, 'but'); both have the form 'three who ... as if they ate ...'.
  • שֶׁאָכְלוּ is a relative clause with a perfect: 'who ate'; the sentence is a casus pendens with כְּאִלּוּ opening the main clause.
  • מִשֻּׁלְחָנוֹ שֶׁל מָקוֹם uses the Mishnaic double genitive: 'from his table, of the Place'.
  • מָקוֹם is a name of God, 'the Place' (the Omnipresent), here without the article; בְּלִי מָקוֹם in Isaiah can be read 'with no place', and the sages read it as 'without the Place'.
  • The two scripture proofs are quoted with their sources (Isaiah 28:8, Ezekiel 41:22), the second ending with 'the table that is before the LORD'.

מָקוֹם בָּרוּךְ הוּא is translated 'the Omnipresent, blessed be He' by the project's decision; the literal sense of מָקוֹם is 'the Place'. מִזִּבְחֵי מֵתִים is 'sacrifices to the dead', the sacrifices of idols (Bartenura and Rambam; Psalm 106:28). In Isaiah 28:8 בְּלִי מָקוֹם means 'with no room'; Bartenura reads it as 'without the Place', for they did not mention the name of the Place at the table, so the English 'with no place' keeps both readings.

3:4

Rabbi Hanina son of Hakhinai says: One who is awake at night, or who walks on the road alone, and who turns his heart to idleness: this one is answerable with his life.

Rabbi Hanina son-of Hakhinai says the-one-awake at-night and-the-one-walking on-the-road alone and-the-one-turning his-heart to-idleness behold this incurring-liability with-his-life

Grammar
  • Three participles with the article (הַנֵּעוֹר, הַמְהַלֵּךְ, הַמְפַנֶּה) head the sentence; Bartenura reads the third as the condition of the first two: one who is awake at night or walks alone, and turns his heart to idle things.
  • הֲרֵי זֶה opens the main clause ('this one ...'), resuming the subject.
  • הַנֵּעוֹר is a Nifal participle of עור, 'is awake'; מְהַלֵּךְ and מְפַנֶּה are Piel participles. The Piel of פנה is 'to clear, make room': he frees his heart for idleness.
  • מִתְחַיֵּב is a Hitpael participle: 'makes himself liable'. The Vilna spelling is מתחייב.
  • בְּנַפְשׁוֹ 'with his life': he puts his own life at risk (Bartenura: demons by night, robbers on the road).
  • יְחִידִי, 'alone, by himself', is the Mishnaic adverbial form of יָחִיד.

מִתְחַיֵּב בְּנַפְשׁוֹ is rendered 'is answerable with his life'. The English follows Bartenura: the night (3:4:1-2) is the time of demons and the lone traveller is in danger from robbers and other harm, and the one in either case who turns his heart to idle thoughts is guilty, for words of Torah would have guarded him. Grammar
  • The verse is a descending ladder: ten, five, three, two, one, each given a scripture proof introduced by וּמִנַּיִן אֲפִלּוּ 'and from where even'.
  • The first sentence has the same casus pendens as 3:2: 'Ten who sit ... the Shekhinah dwells among them'.
  • שְׁכִינָה שְׁרוּיָה בֵּינֵיהֶם: a feminine noun with a passive participle of שרה, 'dwelling', with the suffix בֵּינֵיהֶם 'among them'.
  • The proof texts are cut off at the point of the proof: the quotation from Malachi ends with וְגוֹ' ('and so on'), and Exodus 20:21 is quoted from בְּכָל הַמָּקוֹם.
  • אֲגֻדָּה ('bundle') is read as a hand with five fingers (Rambam, Rabbeinu Yonah) or as a bundle of three elements or three stalks of hyssop (Bartenura); the verse is a wordplay on the number in the proof.

The proof of the five rests on אֲגֻדָּתוֹ, 'his bundle': Rambam and Rabbeinu Yonah read a bundle as what one binds in one hand, which has five fingers. Bartenura's own text reverses the two middle proofs: five from 'in the midst of judges he judges' (three judges and two litigants) and three from 'his bundle' (fire, air and water around the earth, or three stalks of hyssop); he gives the order of this text as the reading of 'some books', with the hand of five fingers. The English keeps 'his bundle'. In the first proof the congregation (עֵדָה) is not fewer than ten (Rambam; Bartenura from the spies, Numbers 14:27). In the third, אֱלֹהִים is 'judges' (a court of three: Rambam, Bartenura), and the English follows them. 'Kefar Hananiah' is a place, as in the pack. The pack's lemma link for אֵל (the particle אַל) does not fit; the word is אֵל 'God', in 'the congregation of God'.

And so David says: “For everything comes from You, and from Your hand we have given to You.” Rabbi Shimon says: One who walks on the road and reviews his learning, and interrupts his learning and says, “How fine is this tree, and how fine is this furrow!”, Scripture accounts it to him as if he were forfeiting his life.

Grammar
  • אוֹמֵר is a participle: in Avot the attribution formula is 'X says', not a past tense.
  • תֶּן is the imperative of נתן: the first nun of the root drops in the imperative, as in biblical תֵּן; the segol of תֶּן is the short form before a closely joined word (Bartenura's heading has תֵּן).
  • שֶׁ is the Mishnaic relative; before the pronoun in שֶׁאַתָּה וְשֶׁלְּךָ שֶׁלּוֹ it is causal ('for you and yours are His'). שֶׁלּוֹ is the possessive 'of-him'.
  • The saying has two parts: a positive injunction (Rabbi Elazar of Bartota) and a verse from Chronicles that supports it. Rabbi Shimon's saying is a separate sentence; the long participial subject is resumed by מַעֲלֶה עָלָיו.
  • הַמְהַלֵּךְ is a Piel participle; שׁוֹנֶה, מַפְסִיק and אוֹמֵר are participles that continue the subject. The subject hangs until the main verb מַעֲלֶה.
  • מִתְחַיֵּב בְּנַפְשׁוֹ: the Hitpael participle of חיב, 'be held liable'; the idiom means to be liable to forfeit his life.

Rabbi Elazar's 'Give Him what is His' is given as an imperative to the reader; the object of 'give' is God, as Bartenura reads it. The 'furrow' of the field is Bartenura's reading of ניר (the BDB lemma offered, a proper name, is not a fit and no sense is given). 'Forfeiting his life' renders מִתְחַיֵּב בְּנַפְשׁוֹ, literally 'liable with/in his soul', that is, liable to a penalty of life; the same phrase recurs in 3:8.

Grammar
  • בְּרַבִּי is a contraction of 'son of Rabbi' (bar-rabbi); בֶּן is not used. The chain is Dostai, son of Rabbi Yannai, in the name of Rabbi Meir.
  • The saying has a statement, a proof text, a challenge introduced by יָכוֹל ('one might think') and an answer introduced by תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ('the teaching says'); the final הָא ('so') draws the conclusion.
  • The first verse is Deuteronomy 4:9 and the second continues the same verse: שָׁם marks 'in the same place'. The subject of the plural verb רָאוּ is 'eyes', as in the verse.
  • שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר is the Nifal of אמר with שֶׁ: 'as it was said'. The citation in brackets (דברים ד) is a note in the printed text, not part of the saying.
  • הַשּׁוֹכֵחַ, מִתְחַיֵּב and the like are participles; כָּל הַשּׁוֹכֵחַ is 'every one who forgets', a topic that is resumed by מַעֲלֶה עָלָיו.
  • וִיסִירֵם is a Hifil imperfect with a 3rd masculine plural suffix, 'and he removes them'; the subject is the student who sits and does it on purpose.

'Learning' translates מִשְׁנָה in its general sense of what has been repeated and learned, not the book 'Mishnah'. 'Forfeiting his life' renders מִתְחַיֵּב בְּנַפְשׁוֹ, literally 'liable in his soul'. 'Too hard for him' is Bartenura's reading of תָּקְפָה עָלָיו: it was hard for him and he forgot it because of the difficulty. The two readings of why forgetting counts against him are recorded under the Bartenura anchor. The printed citations (דברים ד) and (שם) are not translated.

  • The saying is built on two matched pairs, each with an affirmative and a negative half. כָּל שֶׁ... 'anyone who/whose' opens each half and is resumed by the main clause (a casus pendens: 'anyone whose fear of sin precedes, his wisdom endures').
  • קוֹדֶמֶת and מִתְקַיֶּמֶת are feminine participles agreeing with יִרְאַת and חָכְמָה; מְרֻבִּין is masculine plural (the deeds), מְרֻבָּה feminine singular (the wisdom).
  • מִן in מֵחָכְמָתוֹ and מִמַּעֲשָׂיו is comparative: 'more than'. The contrast is between יִרְאַת חֵטְא (fear of sin) and wisdom, and then between deeds and wisdom.
  • הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר, 'He used to say': the same rabbi continues, with a second saying; the formula is the Avot formula for a second saying.
  • אֵין with a participle negates a predicate: אֵין חָכְמָתוֹ מִתְקַיֶּמֶת, 'his wisdom is not enduring'.

מִתְקַיֶּמֶת 'endures' is the Hitpael participle of קום 'stand', 'is established, lasts'. Bartenura explains it as the wisdom bringing him to what his heart desires, so that he takes pleasure in it. For 'deeds exceed wisdom' Bartenura says the case is one who is diligent in positive commandments, the earlier clause being about negative ones; the English keeps the plain 'deeds'.

Grammar
  • הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר 'He used to say' continues Rabbi Hanina ben Dosa's sayings from 3:9. The pair of sayings is built on כָּל שֶׁ... 'anyone whose/with whom'.
  • נוֹחָה הֵימֶנּוּ: נוֹחָה is a feminine participle (agreeing with רוּחַ, which is here feminine); הֵימֶנּוּ is the Mishnaic form of מִמֶּנּוּ, 'from him'. The idiom is 'the spirit is at ease with him'.
  • The two halves are parallel. Bartenura reads the first as a sign, not a cause: whoever is loved below is known (בְּיָדוּעַ) to be loved above. אֵין negates each half.
  • Rabbi Dosa's list has four items joined by וְ: two use the genitive particle שֶׁל (שֵׁנָה שֶׁל שַׁחֲרִית 'sleep of morning', יַיִן שֶׁל צָהֳרַיִם 'wine of noon') and two are construct chains (שִׂיחַת הַיְלָדִים, יְשִׁיבַת בָּתֵּי כְנֵסִיּוֹת); the plural participle מוֹצִיאִין has the whole list as subject.
  • עַם הָאָרֶץ is literally 'people of the land'; in the Mishnah it means an unlearned person, a person ignorant of Torah. הַמָּקוֹם is literally 'the Place', a name of God.

הַמָּקוֹם is rendered 'the Omnipresent' (literally 'the Place') as in the work's decisions. עַמֵּי הָאָרֶץ is 'the unlearned' (literally 'people of the land'). 'Synagogues' renders בָּתֵּי כְנֵסִיּוֹת, 'houses of assembly'. Bartenura says the last item is wrong because people gather there and talk of idle things. 'Spirit ... is pleased' renders רוּחַ ... נוֹחָה, 'the spirit is at ease'; Bartenura glosses it as love: whoever is loved below is loved above.

Rabbi Elazar of Modi'in says: One who profanes the sacred things, and one who despises the festivals, and one who shames his fellow in public, and one who breaks the covenant of Abraham our father, peace be upon him, and one who brazenly flouts the Torah, contrary to the halakha: even though he has Torah and good deeds, he has no share in the world to come.

Grammar
  • Each of the five offences is a participle with the article (הַמְחַלֵּל, הַמְבַזֶּה, הַמַּלְבִּין, הַמֵּפֵר, הַמְגַלֶּה) and all form the long subject; the main clause is only at the end: אֵין לוֹ חֵלֶק.
  • הַמַּלְבִּין פְּנֵי חֲבֵרוֹ is literally 'the one who whitens his fellow's face': a person shamed first reddens, then goes pale. The phrase is a fixed idiom for public shaming.
  • אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁ is a concessive conjunction: 'even though'. יֵשׁ בְּיָדוֹ is the possessive construction 'there is in his hand', that is, 'he has'.
  • אֵין לוֹ חֵלֶק is the negative possessive: 'there is not to him a share'. הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא (the world to come) is a participle used adjectivally.
  • The stem גלה in the Piel with פָּנִים means 'to uncover a face'; with בַּתּוֹרָה the idiom is 'to bare one's face (shamelessly) against the Torah'.
  • עָלָיו הַשָּׁלוֹם 'peace upon him' is a formula said after the name of a righteous person; it is not a statement of the mishnah.

'Brazenly flouts the Torah' renders הַמְגַלֶּה פָנִים בַּתּוֹרָה שֶׁלֹּא כַהֲלָכָה. Rambam, Rabbeinu Yonah and Bartenura's second explanation take the idiom as acting shamelessly, in the open, against the Torah. Bartenura's first explanation, one who 'shows faces' (explanations) in the Torah that are not according to halakha, is recorded under the Bartenura anchor. 'The sacred things' renders הַקָּדָשִׁים: Bartenura includes both the offerings of the altar and property consecrated to the Temple's upkeep. 'The covenant of Abraham' is circumcision; Bartenura takes the offence as one who was not circumcised, or who was circumcised and draws the foreskin forward to hide it; Rabbeinu Yonah names only the second. 'The festivals' is Bartenura's reading, the intermediate days (חוֹל הַמּוֹעֵד).

3:12

Rabbi Yishmael says: Be yielding to a superior, and composed with the young, and receive every person with joy.

my-master Yishmael saying: be light to-head, and-easy to-youth, and-be receiving the-all the-man with-joy.

Grammar
  • הֱוֵי is the Mishnaic imperative of הָיָה ('be'); וֶהֱוֵי is the same with the conjunction, the vowel pattern of the conjunction before a guttural.
  • הֱוֵי קַל לְרֹאשׁ: the predicate adjective with ל. קַל is 'light'; Rambam says its sense is known, 'lightness'; the figure is of a person who does not weigh on a superior, who is quick to serve.
  • נוֹחַ is used as an adjective, 'easy, at rest, composed' (Rambam: הַיִּשּׁוּב וְהַהֲנָחָה); תִּשְׁחֹרֶת is a rare noun from the root שחר 'black', for 'youth', the time of black hair (Bartenura, Rambam). Rabbeinu Yonah derives it differently: from שחוור, a lord or official, or from the Targum's שחרית 'forced service' (Num 16:15).
  • מְקַבֵּל is the Piel participle of קבל, 'to receive'. With הֱוֵי it forms a periphrastic imperative, 'be one who receives'.
  • The sentence is three clauses of the pattern 'be + adjective/participle'. The last sets the general rule for every person (אֶת כָּל הָאָדָם), after two specific cases of an older and a younger person.

'Yielding to a superior' renders קַל לְרֹאשׁ. Bartenura and Rambam both read it as being quick to serve a person of standing; the English keeps the idiom without expanding it. 'Composed with the young' renders נוֹחַ לְתִשְׁחֹרֶת; Bartenura and Rambam: do not make yourself light with the young but stand before them calmly and with dignity, and yet receive everyone cheerfully. 'Receive' is the plain sense of מְקַבֵּל; Bartenura also derives it from the Aramaic קֳבֵל 'facing', so 'meet'. Bartenura's alternative reading (youth and old age with the Creator) is recorded under his anchor.

Rabbi Akiva says laughter and-lightness-of head accustom to-lewdness. Tradition fence to-the-Torah. Tithes fence to-wealth. Vows fence to-abstinence. Fence to-wisdom silence.

Grammar
  • The saying is built of four short nominal clauses of the form 'X, a fence for Y', with no verb; the English supplies 'is/are'. The last clause reverses the order (the fence first, then its identity), which puts silence at the end.
  • מַרְגִּילִין is a Hifil participle, plural masculine, agreeing with the two subjects שְׂחוֹק and קַלּוּת רֹאשׁ.
  • The tradition (מָסֹרֶת) is listed with tithes, vows and silence: each is a protective fence (סְיָג) around something valuable.
  • The first sentence is a warning, the next four are the positive fences; the saying is one continuous teaching of Rabbi Akiva.

מָסֹרֶת is rendered 'Masoretic tradition' because Bartenura and Rabbeinu Yonah both say it means the received tradition of plene and defective spellings of the Torah text; the Hebrew word alone could also mean 'tradition' in general. The Jastrow lemma on לָעשֶׁר is a verb entry (עָשַׂר); I keep the data but read the noun 'wealth'. Rambam reads נְדָרִים as a means of acquiring the habit of restraint. For סְיָג לַחָכְמָה שְׁתִיקָה, Bartenura limits the silence to optional talk between people; the English keeps the Hebrew's unlimited wording.

He used to say: Beloved is a human being, for he was created in the image. Extra love was made known to him, that he was created in the image, as it is said (Genesis 9): 'For in the image of God He made the human being.' Beloved are Israel, for they were called children of the Omnipresent. Grammar
  • The saying has three parallel units, each of the same shape: (1) 'Beloved is X, for ...'; (2) 'Extra love was made known to X, that ...'; (3) a proof text introduced by שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר. The repetition is deliberate, and the English keeps it.
  • חִבָּה יְתֵרָה נוֹדַעַת לוֹ: the verb is a Nifal participle of יָדַע, 'is made known'. The point (Rambam, Bartenura) is that God's telling the human being of the gift is itself a further proof of love.
  • שֶׁנִּבְרָא, שֶׁנִּקְרְאוּ, שֶׁנִּתַּן are Nifal perfect forms with the relative particle שֶׁ ('that/for'). Three Nifal verbs (was created, were called, was given) carry the argument; the passive leaves God unnamed as the agent until the proof text.
  • The three proof texts come from Genesis 9:6, Deuteronomy 14:1 and Proverbs 4:2. In the third (Proverbs 4:2) the speaker is God (or wisdom's teacher) addressing Israel about the Torah.
  • אֱלֹהֵיכֶם is plural construct with a 2nd person plural suffix ('your God'); תּוֹרָתִי is construct with the 1st person singular suffix; the object marker אֶת has no English counterpart.

חִבָּה יְתֵרָה: 'extra love' (Jastrow: 'love'; יָתֵר 'surplus'). I keep 'extra' rather than 'special' to preserve the idea of a second, additional love beyond the first. נוֹדַעַת is read 'is made known' (Nifal of יָדַע) following Rambam as quoted by Bartenura: God informs the creature of the gift. The pack's BDB keys for טוֹב and אָדָם are proper names (Tob, Adam); the pack also lists jastrow:טוֹב II and jastrow:אָדָם, which give 'good' and 'man'. כְּלִי חֶמְדָּה is rendered 'a desirable vessel' to keep the biblical wording of חֶמְדָּה; the vessel is the Torah, by which the world was created. In Proverbs 4:2 תּוֹרָתִי can mean 'my teaching'; the English reads 'my Torah', as the mishnah applies the verse to the Torah.

Commentary (8)
Bartenuranormativefollows חִבָּה יְתֵרָה נוֹדַעַת לוֹ
Bartenura, citing Rambam: the Holy One showed the human being extra love by informing him that he was created in the image, for one who does a kindness and tells his friend of it shows more love than one who does it without telling. Another reading: נוֹדַעַת means a love that is revealed and public: not only a hidden love of the Omnipresent for the human being, but a love open and known to all.

The-all foreseen, and-the-permission given, and-in-goodness the-world is-judged, and-the-all according-to-abundance-of the-deed.

Grammar
  • Four short clauses, each a noun and a passive participle or verb: הַכֹּל צָפוּי, הָרְשׁוּת נְתוּנָה, הָעוֹלָם נִדּוֹן, הַכֹּל לְפִי רֹב הַמַּעֲשֶׂה. The first two clauses state an apparent tension (foreknowledge and choice) without resolving it.
  • צָפוּי and נְתוּנָה are passive participles (Qal) used predicatively; the passive leaves the agent, God, unstated.
  • בְּטוֹב is 'in goodness' (with the preposition בְּ): the world is judged 'with goodness' (by mercy), and the final clause qualifies this: each person receives it in proportion to deeds.
  • הַכֹּל occurs twice, in the first and the last clause, and carries a different sense each time: 'everything' that happens is foreseen; 'everyone' (or 'all') is judged according to deeds.

Rambam's reading of the last clause as 'but not according to the deed' (אֲבָל לֹא עַל פִּי הַמַּעֲשֶׂה) is a variant; I translate the text of the pack, which reads לְפִי רֹב הַמַּעֲשֶׂה. הָרְשׁוּת is translated 'freedom of choice' because both normative commentators say it means the power in the hand of a person to do good or evil; the plain sense of the word is 'permission, authority'. For רֹב I keep 'abundance' and give Bartenura's alternative 'majority' in the apparatus.

3:16

He used to say: Everything is given as a pledge, and a net is spread over all the living. The shop is open, the shopkeeper extends credit, the ledger is open, and the hand writes; and whoever wishes to borrow may come and borrow. The collectors make their rounds constantly, every day, and exact payment from a person with his knowledge and without his knowledge. They have what to rely on, and the judgment is a judgment of truth. And everything is prepared for the banquet.

Grammar
  • The saying is a single extended metaphor: a shop that gives credit. The clauses are strung with 'and' and almost all use participles (נָתוּן, פְּרוּסָה, פְּתוּחָה, מֵקִיף, כּוֹתֶבֶת, מַחֲזִירִים, נִפְרָעִין, מְתֻקָּן), giving the sense of an ongoing, present process.
  • הַכֹּל נָתוּן בְּעֵרָבוֹן, 'everything is given as a pledge', and the following clause וּמְצוּדָה פְרוּסָה עַל כָּל הַחַיִּים, 'a net is spread over all the living', open the images of security for a debt and of capture.
  • The pairs מִדַּעְתּוֹ / וְשֶׁלֹּא מִדַּעְתּוֹ ('with his knowledge' / 'without his knowledge') contrast the debtor who remembers his debt and the one who has forgotten it.
  • וְכָל הָרוֹצֶה לִלְווֹת יָבֹא וְיִלְוֶה uses a jussive-like imperfect ('let him come and borrow'): the borrower acts freely, linking to the freedom of choice in the previous mishnah.
  • The last clause וְהַכֹּל מְתֻקָּן לַסְּעוּדָה resolves the metaphor: the collection ends in a meal. Rambam and Bartenura read the meal as the world to come.

The shop metaphor is read as the commentators read it: the shopkeeper is God, the ledger is the record of deeds, the collectors are sufferings and death (Bartenura, Rambam), and the banquet is the world to come. The English keeps the images and puts the application in the commentary, as the saying itself does. מֵקִיף is rendered 'extends credit' (from הַקָּפָה); the pack's lemma is the root קוף. מַחֲזִירִים, 'make their rounds', follows Jastrow's 'to go around (searching)'. The Jastrow entries for בָּא I and לָוָה in the pack give only cross-references or a different meaning, so I give no sense for those verbs; the data are kept. Bartenura's explanations of לְדַעְתּוֹ and שֶׁלֹּא לְדַעְתּוֹ are attached to the whole verse in the pack.

3:17

Rabbi Elazar son of Azariah says: If there is no Torah, there is no proper conduct; if there is no proper conduct, there is no Torah. If there is no wisdom, there is no fear; if there is no fear, there is no wisdom. If there is no understanding, there is no knowledge; if there is no knowledge, there is no understanding. If there is no flour, there is no Torah; if there is no Torah, there is no flour. He used to say: Anyone whose wisdom exceeds his deeds, to what is he compared? To a tree whose branches are many and whose roots are few; and the wind comes and uproots it and overturns it on its face, as it is said (Jeremiah 17): 'He shall be like a bare shrub in the desert plain, and shall not see when good comes; he shall dwell in the parched places of the wilderness, a salt land, uninhabited.' But anyone whose deeds exceed his wisdom, to what is he compared? To a tree whose branches are few and whose roots are many, so that even if all the winds in the world come and blow against it, they do not move it from its place, as it is said (ibid.): 'He shall be like a tree planted by water, and by a stream it sends out its roots, and it shall not see when heat comes; its leaf shall be fresh, and in a year of drought it shall not worry, and it shall not cease from yielding fruit.'

Rabbi Elazar son-of Azariah says: if not Torah, not way-of land; if not way-of land, not Torah. If not wisdom, not fear; if not fear, not wisdom. If not understanding, not knowledge; if not knowledge, not understanding. If not flour, not Torah; if not Torah, not flour. Grammar
  • The first part is four mirror-image pairs of conditionals אִם אֵין X, אֵין Y / אִם אֵין Y, אֵין X: each of Torah and proper conduct, wisdom and fear, understanding and knowledge, flour and Torah depends on the other. The English keeps the repetition, because the symmetry is the argument.
  • אֵין is the negative existential particle, not a verb: 'there is not'. In a conditional with אִם: 'if there is no X, there is no Y'.
  • The second saying is introduced by הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר and has two parallel images (tree with many branches and few roots; tree with few branches and many roots), each with the same structure: כָּל שֶׁ... לְמַה הוּא דוֹמֶה, לְאִילָן שֶׁ..., then a proof text. The English keeps the repeated question.
  • מְרֻבָּה, מְרֻבִּין, מֻעָטִין are Pual participles ('are increased, are decreased'), used as predicate adjectives; the passive implies natural growth.
  • וְהָרוּחַ בָּאָה וְעוֹקַרְתּוֹ וְהוֹפַכְתּוֹ עַל פָּנָיו: three feminine participles (בָּאָה, then two Qal participles with object suffixes) in a series; the image is told in the present, 'the wind comes and uproots it and overturns it'. In the second image the verbs are participles too: בָּאוֹת וְנוֹשְׁבוֹת, 'coming and blowing'.
  • Both proof texts are from Jeremiah 17: the first (verse 6) is about 'the one who trusts in man'; the second (verse 8) about 'the one who trusts in the LORD'. The mishnah quotes only the images.

דֶּרֶךְ אֶרֶץ: rendered 'proper conduct' because Bartenura and Rabbeinu Yonah both read it as decent behaviour toward people and good character traits in the first pair; in the pair about flour (קֶמַח) the same phrase could be read as 'worldly occupation', and I note that rather than change the word. The mishnah's יִרְאָה is 'fear', without 'of God', as in the Hebrew. For the pair דַּעַת / בִּינָה I follow Bartenura's distinction (דַּעַת 'finds the reason of a thing'; בִּינָה 'derives one thing from another'); the English uses the ordinary words 'knowledge' and 'understanding'. Rabbeinu Yonah describes the two differently (knowledge as what one grasps of oneself, understanding as inferring one thing from another). The pack's default lexicon keys for טוֹב, בָּא, דָּמָה, יוּבַל and צֹרֶת point to different words (a place, 'father', 'to cease', a proper name, a proper name); where the pack also lists the right entry (jastrow:טוֹב II, jastrow:דָּמָה ²) it is used, otherwise no sense is given and the correct word is read. Rambam's comment on דעת and בינה is anchored in the pack to 3:16.21 and is ruled on under 3:16.

Rabbi Eliezer son of Hisma says: Bird offerings and the openings of the menstruant, these very things are the essentials of the laws. Astronomical cycles and gematria are the desserts of wisdom.

Rabbi Eliezer son-of Hisma says: nests and-openings-of menstruant, they they bodies-of laws. Cycles and-gematriot, desserts to-the-wisdom.

Grammar
  • Two sentences, each of two coordinated nouns followed by a predicate. The first has a doubled pronoun, הֵן הֵן, which marks emphasis: 'these, indeed these'. The second has no pronoun at all; the predicate is the single noun פַּרְפְּרָאוֹת.
  • קִנִּין is the plural of קֵן ('nest') used as a technical term for bird offerings. פִּתְחֵי נִדָּה is a construct chain, 'openings of the menstruant'.
  • גּוּפֵי הֲלָכוֹת is a construct chain: 'bodies of laws', meaning the core or essentials.
  • גִּימַטְרִיאוֹת and פַּרְפְּרָאוֹת are Greek loan words that the mishnah uses as technical terms; תְּקוּפוֹת is Hebrew, from the root קוף/נקף 'to go around' (Jastrow: 'turn, cycle'). The contrast of גּוּף ('body') with פַּרְפְּרָאוֹת ('after-dishes') is the point of the saying: the essential versus the ornamental.

Two choices to record. פִּתְחֵי נִדָּה: the English keeps the literal 'the openings of the menstruant' because the sense of 'openings' is not simple (Bartenura: her opening or onset; Rabbeinu Yonah: three places in the house, and many appearances of blood). קִנִּין is rendered 'bird offerings' following Bartenura, with the literal 'nests' in the gloss. פַּרְפְּרָאוֹת is 'desserts' following Bartenura's image of the sweets eaten at the end of a meal, which continues the meal metaphor of the previous mishnah. The default Jastrow entry גּוּף I is the verb 'to squeeze'; the noun is jastrow:גּוּף II, sense 2 'essence'.

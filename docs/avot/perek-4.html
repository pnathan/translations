4:1

Ben Zoma says: Who is wise? One who learns from every person, as it is said: "From all my teachers I have gained insight, for Your testimonies are my meditation." Who is mighty? One who subdues his impulse, as it is said: "Better is one slow to anger than a mighty man, and one who rules his spirit than one who captures a city." Who is rich? One who rejoices in his portion, as it is said: "When you eat the toil of your hands, happy are you, and it is well with you." "Happy are you": in this world. "And it is well with you": in the world to come. Who is honored? One who honors people, as it is said: "For those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me will be lightly esteemed."

Grammar
  • The saying has four parallel questions, each introduced by אֵיזֶהוּ ('who is it that...?') and answered by a participle with the article (הַלּוֹמֵד, הַכּוֹבֵשׁ, הַשָּׂמֵחַ, הַמְכַבֵּד): 'the one who learns', 'the one who subdues'. The participle with the article serves as a relative clause.
  • Each answer is closed by שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר ('as it is said') and a verse from the Bible; the verses are quoted in their biblical form, so Psalm 119 and 128 address God and the hearer directly, with biblical forms (עֵדְוֹתֶיךָ; לָךְ, the pausal form of the masculine לְךָ) that are not mishnaic Hebrew.
  • Proverbs 16:32 is built on מִ of comparison, doubled: טוֹב אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם מִגִּבּוֹר וּמֹשֵׁל בְּרוּחוֹ מִלֹּכֵד עִיר, 'better the slow-of-anger than the hero, and the ruler over his spirit than the taker of a city'.
  • Psalm 128:2: אַשְׁרֶיךָ is a plural construct noun used as an exclamation ('happy are you'). The mishnah splits the verse in two and gives a time to each half: 'in this world' for אַשְׁרֶיךָ and 'in the world to come' for טוֹב לָךְ.
  • 1 Samuel 2:30 pairs a Piel participle (מְכַבְּדַי) with the matching Piel imperfect (אֲכַבֵּד); the verb root כבד has the sense 'be heavy' and the Piel 'give weight to, honor'. The same root underlies מְכֻבָּד (Pual), 'honored'. יֵקָלּוּ 'will be light' is the opposite of 'heavy'.

The four questions are answered with the verb in a participle; I kept the form 'One who...' so that each answer stays as brief as the Hebrew. 'Impulse' for יֵצֶר: the inclination of a person's mind and desires, neither good nor evil by the word alone. 'Honored' (מְכֻבָּד) is passive: honored by others, not 'honorable'. Bartenura's reading of מִלֹּכֵד עִיר (see commentary entry 4:1:5) takes the min as 'coming from', and I record it. The Psalm's 'happy are you' and 'well with you' have no verb in Hebrew, and the English likewise has none for 'it is well'.

4:2

Ben Azzai says: Run to a light commandment as to a weighty one, and flee from transgression. For a commandment draws a commandment, and a transgression draws a transgression. For the reward of a commandment is a commandment, and the reward of a transgression is a transgression.

Grammar
  • הֱוֵי is an imperative of 'be' (an Aramaic-style form common in the sayings); with the participle רָץ it means 'be running', i.e. 'run'. The two commands are parallel: 'run to...' and 'flee from...' (בּוֹרֵחַ is also a participle).
  • קַלָּה and חֲמוּרָה are feminine adjectives agreeing with מִצְוָה; the second is used alone with כְּ + בְּ: 'as to a weighty one'.
  • The reasons come in two clauses with שֶׁ ('for'), each a pair of short sentences: 'a commandment draws a commandment, and a transgression draws a transgression'; 'the reward of a commandment is a commandment, and the reward of a transgression is a transgression'. The second pair has no verb 'is': the two nouns are placed side by side.
  • גּוֹרֶרֶת is a feminine participle of גרר, literally 'drag' (Jastrow 3), here 'draw along, bring after it'.

שְׂכַר is 'reward' (wages), and the saying is left as brief as the Hebrew: the reward of a commandment is a commandment. The ambiguity is kept: either the result is another commandment given to the doer (Bartenura's first reading) or the pleasure taken in the act is counted as a commandment (his second). I did not choose between them in the English.

Commentary (9)
Bartenuranormativefollows שֶׁמִּצְוָה גּוֹרֶרֶת מִצְוָה
Bartenura: it is the way of the world that one who has done one commandment finds it easy to do others, and one who starts on transgressions finds it hard to leave them; also, Heaven helps and arranges that he who has done one commandment does another, in order to give him reward for both. Another explanation: whatever pleasure a man takes in doing a commandment is counted as a commandment of its own, and he takes reward for the commandment and for the pleasure he enjoyed in doing it.

4:3

He used to say: Do not be contemptuous of any person, and do not dismiss any thing as remote; for there is no person who has no hour, and no thing that has no place.

he was saying not be despising to-every person and-do-not be keeping-at-a-distance to-every thing for-there-is-not to-you person that-there-is-not to-him hour and-there-is-not to-you thing that-there-is-not to-him place

Grammar
  • הוּא הָיָה אוֹמֵר: the personal pronoun with the perfect of 'be' and a participle gives the habitual past, 'he used to say'. The 'he' is the same sage as in 4:2 (Ben Azzai), since no new name is given.
  • Two parallel prohibitions with אַל and the jussive תְּהִי + a predicate (בָז; מַפְלִיג, a Hiphil participle). Both are followed by לְכָל, 'of any'.
  • The reason has the idiom שֶׁאֵין לְךָ X שֶׁאֵין לוֹ Y, 'there is no X that has no Y': the 'to you' (לְךָ) is not translated; literally 'for you have no person who has not his hour'.
  • The word מָקוֹם 'place' is a different word from the divine name הַמָּקוֹם in the decisions; here it means 'a place, a role'.

מַפְלִיג has several senses in Jastrow (put off, go too far, speak indefinitely, reject). Bartenura and Rabbeinu Yonah gloss it as 'regards as distant, far-fetched': 'it is far off that it will happen, no need to worry', so I chose 'dismiss as remote'. Another reading, 'speak too lightly of anything', would also fit the Hebrew, and I did not use it. שָׁעָה 'hour' is a short time, the moment when a person (or thing) counts, as the commentators say: a time when he can harm or help.

4:4

Rabbi Levitas of Yavneh says: Be very, very humble of spirit, for the hope of man is the worm. Rabbi Yohanan son of Beroka says: Whoever profanes the name of Heaven in secret, payment is exacted from him in the open. Whether unwitting or willful, in profaning the Name.

Grammar
  • רַבִּי is 'my master' with the pronominal suffix frozen into the title; אִישׁ יַבְנֶה is 'man of Yavneh', here 'of Yavneh'. The Latin name לְוִיטָס is Levitas.
  • מְאֹד מְאֹד הֱוֵי שְׁפַל רוּחַ: the repeated adverb stands before the imperative. שְׁפַל רוּחַ is a construct chain with an adjective, 'low of spirit'. The reason שֶׁתִּקְוַת אֱנוֹשׁ רִמָּה has no verb: 'the hope of man (is) the worm'.
  • כָּל הַמְחַלֵּל ... נִפְרָעִין מִמֶּנּוּ: a casus pendens. The subject is stated first ('whoever profanes...') and then picked up by the pronoun on מִמֶּנּוּ. נִפְרָעִין is an impersonal Nifal participle plural, 'they exact payment' in the passive.
  • אֶחָד שׁוֹגֵג וְאֶחָד מֵזִיד: the pattern 'one X and one Y' means 'X and Y alike', with the predicate unspoken. בְּחִלּוּל הַשֵּׁם gives the context, 'in the case of profaning the Name'.

תִּקְוַת אֱנוֹשׁ: 'the hope of man' can mean 'what man hopes for in the end' (the worm) or 'the expectation of man is for the worm'; Rabbeinu Yonah reads the end of man as the worm, which is the sense kept. For אֶחָד שׁוֹגֵג וְאֶחָד מֵזִיד I kept the ellipsis; Rambam (4:4:2) explains that in both cases payment is exacted openly, each for its own kind of offense, so the pair is 'alike' as to the open exaction, not as to the amount. 'The Name' is the divine name, as in 'profaning the Name'.

Rabbi Ishmael, his son, says: One who learns Torah in order to teach, they enable him to learn and to teach. And one who learns in order to do, they enable him to learn and to teach, to keep and to do. Rabbi Zadok says: Do not make them a crown to exalt yourself with, nor a spade to dig with. And thus Hillel used to say: "And he who makes use of the crown passes away." Hence you have learned: whoever derives benefit from the words of Torah takes his life from the world.

Grammar
  • The first two sayings are in parallel: הַלּוֹמֵד עַל מְנָת לְ... מַסְפִּיקִין בְּיָדוֹ ...; the subject is a participle with the article, the verb is an impersonal plural participle (מַסְפִּיקִין, 'they enable'), and the aims are infinitives with לְ. The second saying adds 'to keep and to do'.
  • אַל תַּעֲשֵׂם ... וְלֹא קַרְדֹּם: a prohibition with אַל and the imperfect; the second clause, with וְלֹא, takes its verb from the first ('nor (make them) a spade'). The suffix -ם 'them' stands for the words of the Torah, to which the prior saying referred.
  • Hillel's saying וּדְאִשְׁתַּמֵּשׁ בְּתָגָא חָלָף is in Aramaic: the relative דְּ, the Ithpaal perfect אִשְׁתַּמֵּשׁ, תָּגָא 'crown' with the Aramaic ending -ā, and the verb at the end. Rabbeinu Yonah notes 'tagā means crown, as in the Targum'.
  • הָא לָמַדְתָּ ('behold, you have learned') introduces the conclusion drawn by the editor from Hillel's saying; כָּל הַנֶּהֱנֶה is again 'whoever + participle'. נוֹטֵל חַיָּיו מִן הָעוֹלָם is a participle, the present of a general rule: 'takes his life out of the world'.
  • The word חַיָּיו is plural in form (חַיִּים 'life') and has a singular sense; מִן הָעוֹלָם 'from the world' needs the world to come in the Rambam's reading.

עַל מְנָת: 'in order to, with the intention of' (literally 'on the portion of'). The mishnah's first two sayings are about intent: in each case the aim of the learning decides what he is enabled to do. The reading 'they enable' is Bartenura's majority text; he records another text, 'one who learns in order to teach, they do not enable him to learn and to teach', for those who take 'to teach' as 'in order to be called Rabbi'; I follow the majority text. קַרְדֹּם is 'spade' (the tool named with לַחְפֹּר 'to dig'); Rabbeinu Yonah's variant 'to cut' (לַחְתֹּךְ) would make it an axe. נוֹטֵל חַיָּיו מִן הָעוֹלָם: Rambam says 'his life from the world', i.e. from the life of the world to come; I kept the literal words and give the gloss here.

Bartenura: 'we read': one who learns in order always to be occupied with Torah, with no intention to do acts of kindness, they enable his intention to be done, to learn and to teach; one who learns in order to do, wanting to be occupied with Torah and also to do kindness, they enable him to complete his intention and he merits to learn, teach, keep and do. Some explain 'to teach' as 'to be called Rabbi' and read 'they do not enable him'; but in most books he found the first reading, which is the main one.

4:6

Rabbi Yose says: Whoever honors the Torah, his body is honored by people. And whoever profanes the Torah, his body is profaned by people.

my-master Yose says all-of the-one-honoring [object-marker] the-Torah body-of-him honored upon the-creatures and-all-of the-one-profaning [object-marker] the-Torah body-of-him profaned upon the-creatures

Grammar
  • Two antithetical sayings, one with מְכַבֵּד / מְכֻבָּד (root כבד, Piel / Pual) and one with מְחַלֵּל / מְחֻלָּל (root חלל, Piel / Pual). In each, the Piel participle with the article after כָּל is the subject, and the Pual participle is the predicate of the main clause (a casus pendens: 'whoever honors..., his body is honored').
  • The two verbs are opposites: כבד 'give weight to', חלל 'treat as common, make profane'. The second saying keeps the same word order to make the contrast plain.
  • עַל הַבְּרִיּוֹת, literally 'upon the creatures', stands for the agent: 'honored by people', 'in the eyes of people'. The word גּוּף 'body' with the suffix is a way to say 'he himself, his person'.
  • The verb חִלֵּל is the same as in 4:4 (חִלּוּל הַשֵּׁם): to profane the Name and to profane the Torah are both 'make common'.

גּוּפוֹ is 'his body' in the Hebrew, and I kept it, though it can be read as 'he himself'; Rabbeinu Yonah explains that Heaven helps people to honor him (or to despise him), i.e. 'body' is the person in the eyes of others. 'By people' for עַל הַבְּרִיּוֹת: literally 'upon the creatures', the persons in whose eyes the honor is held.

Grammar
  • הַחוֹשֵׂךְ and הַגַּס are articular participle and adjective used as subjects: 'the one who holds back', 'the one who is coarse'.

Grammar
  • The two halves are in chiastic parallel: fulfilling from poverty leads to fulfilling from wealth; neglecting from wealth leads to neglecting from poverty.

Rabbi Meir says: Limit your business, and engage in the Torah. And be humble of spirit before every person. And if you neglect the Torah, you have many idle ones against you. And if you toil in the Torah, He has much reward to give you.

Rabbi Meir saying: be lessening in-business, and-occupy in-the-Torah. And-be low-of spirit before-every person. And-if you-neglected from-the-Torah, there-is to-you idle-ones many against-you. And-if you-toiled in-the-Torah, there-is to-him reward much to-give to-you.

Grammar
  • הֱוֵי and וֶהֱוֵי are the Mishnaic imperatives of הָיָה; וַעֲסֹק is an imperative of עסק.
  • The two conditions are parallel: if you neglect, if you toil; the first result is 'you have' (לְךָ), the second 'he has' (לוֹ), a shift to the third person.
  • בְטֵלִים הַרְבֵּה is 'many idle ones'; הַרְבֵּה is a Hifil infinitive absolute used as an adverb or adjective.
  • יֶשׁ לוֹ ... לִתֶּן is 'he has ... to give': a possessive idiom with an infinitive.

בְטֵלִים is kept as 'idle ones': Bartenura offers two readings, those who help make you idle, and the wicked and beasts who are idle, by whom the Holy One brings punishment. Rabbeinu Yonah has the second. The English keeps the plain word. 'He has' keeps the Hebrew shift to the third person; Bartenura reads God as the subject.

Grammar
  • הָעוֹשֶׂה and הָעוֹבֵר are participles with the article used as subjects; קוֹנֶה לוֹ is the predicate.

  • תְּשׁוּבָה וּמַעֲשִׂים טוֹבִים is a short sentence without a verb: a nominal predicate with כ-.
  • With Rabbi Johanan the Sandal-maker the saying changes: the two halves differ by לְשֵׁם שָׁמַיִם and its negation.

פְרַקְלִיט and קַטֵּגוֹר are rendered 'advocate' and 'accuser', following Bartenura's 'good intercessor' and 'evil intercessor' and Rambam's court setting before the ruler. פֻּרְעָנוּת is 'punishment'; Rambam says blows and diseases. טוֹב is Jastrow's טוֹב II and שֵׁם Jastrow's שֵׁם II; for קָנָה Jastrow only gives a cross-reference.

Grammar
  • יְהִי is the jussive of הָיָה: 'let ... be'.

  • שָׁמָיִם, with qamets, is the pausal form of שָׁמַיִם at the end of the saying; the noun, plural in form, stands for God.

The three comparisons increase in rank: student, colleague, teacher, Heaven. The decision on שָׁמַיִם, of God, is 'Heaven'. The commentators (Bartenura) bring a verse for each clause; see the apparatus.

4:13

Rabbi Yehuda says: Be careful in study, for an error in study counts as deliberate sin. Rabbi Shimon says: There are three crowns: the crown of Torah, the crown of priesthood, and the crown of kingship; but the crown of a good name rises above them.

Rabbi Yehuda says: be careful in-the-study, for-error-of study rises (to) deliberate-sin. Rabbi Shimon says: three crowns they: crown-of Torah, and-crown-of priesthood, and-crown-of kingship; and-crown-of name good rises over their-backs.

Grammar
  • Two sayings joined in one mishnah: Rabbi Yehuda on care in study, Rabbi Shimon on the three crowns.
  • שִׁגְגַת תַּלְמוּד עוֹלָה זָדוֹן: a wordplay of opposites, שְׁגָגָה (inadvertent error) and זָדוֹן (deliberate sin). עוֹלָה is 'rises to the level of, counts as'.
  • עוֹלָה / עוֹלֶה: the same verb used twice, first for an error that 'rises' to deliberate sin, then for the good name that 'rises' above the crowns.
  • כֶּתֶר is in the construct state throughout: 'crown of Torah', 'crown of priesthood', 'crown of kingship', 'crown of a good name'.
  • גַּבֵּיהֶן: the plural of גַּב with the suffix -הֶן, referring to the three crowns. כְּתָרִים is masculine (see שְׁלשָׁה); the Mishnah often writes -ן for the 3rd masculine plural ending.

תַּלְמוּד is rendered 'study' (Bartenura: one's learning done with precision and according to halakha), not the later Talmud. The pack lexicon gives the Jastrow entry for תַּלְמוּד and for זָהִיר. For וְכֶתֶר שֵׁם טוֹב the vav is rendered 'but' because the good name is set against the three crowns above it.

Grammar
  • The verse quoted at the end, Prov 3:5, is cited in the mishnah by name: משלי ג.
  • גּוֹלֶה is a participle after הֱוֵי: 'be one who goes into exile'.
  • יְקַיְּמוּהָ: Piel imperfect 3rd masculine plural of קום (Mishnaic קִיֵּם 'make endure, secure'), built with a consonantal yod on the Aramaic pattern, with the 3rd feminine singular suffix -הָ referring to the Torah.

Bartenura (first reading) and Rabbeinu Yonah take the clause שֶׁחֲבֵרֶיךָ יְקַיְּמוּהָ בְיָדֶךָ as what one must not rely on: colleagues who return from the teacher will not secure the Torah for you. Rabbi Yannai says: It is not in our hands: not the tranquility of the wicked, nor yet the suffering of the righteous. Rabbi Matya son of Harash says: Be first to greet every person, and be a tail to lions, and do not be a head to foxes.

Rabbi Yannai says: there-is-not in-our-hands, not from-tranquility-of the-wicked and-also not from-sufferings-of the-righteous. Rabbi Matya son-of Harash says: be preceding in-greeting-of every person, and-be tail to-the-lions, and-not be head to-the-foxes.

Grammar
  • Two sayings: Rabbi Yannai on the unanswerable question of the wicked and the righteous; Rabbi Matya on greeting and on where to stand.
  • אֵין בְּיָדֵינוּ לֹא מִ... וְאַף לֹא מִ...: 'it is not in our hands, neither from ... nor even from ...'. The מִ governs each object: neither the tranquility nor the suffering is within our grasp.
  • מַקְדִּים בִּשְׁלוֹם: a Hiphil participle after הֱוֵי: 'be one who is first in greeting'.
  • וֶהֱוֵי with the vav pointed with segol before the guttural-initial imperative; it is the same imperative as הֱוֵי.
  • זָנָב לָאֲרָיוֹת and רֹאשׁ לַשּׁוּעָלִים form a chiastic pair of opposites: tail/head, lions/foxes; לְ marks the group one is tail or head of.

אֵין בְּיָדֵינוּ is kept in its literal, ambiguous form. Bartenura and Rabbeinu Yonah take it as 'we cannot grasp (why the wicked prosper and the righteous suffer)'; Bartenura's second reading, 'in exile we have neither the ease of the wicked nor the sufferings of the righteous', is given in the commentary entry. In 4:15.23 וֶהֱוֵי has the same lemma as הֱוֵי. The BDB entries the corpus gives for אֵין, אַף, אָדָם, רֹאשׁ and שׁוּעָל are wrong homographs (where?, nose, Adam, Rosh, Shual); lex overrides point to the Jastrow entries.

Grammar
  • The saying is a simile (דּוֹמֶה לְ) followed by an imperative drawing the practical consequence.
  • בִּפְנֵי הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא: 'before the world to come', in front of it as an entrance hall is in front of the hall.
  • הַתְקֵן is a Hiphil imperative of תקן: 'make ready, put in order'; the object is the reflexive עַצְמְךָ.
  • פְּרוֹזְדוֹר and טְרַקְלִין are Greek loanwords; טְרַקְלִין is triklinion, a dining hall. The first is the entrance room, the second the inner hall.
  • הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא is the standard term for the world to come; הַבָּא is a participle used as an adjective.

פְּרוֹזְדוֹר has no lexicon key in the pack; it is 'vestibule', Bartenura's 'gatehouse' (the Targum's word for אוּלָם, porch). טְרַקְלִין is 'the great hall', Bartenura 'the king's seat', Rambam 'the palace hall'; the pack's Jastrow entry is a cross-reference only. כְּדֵי is the preposition כְּ with דַּי 'enough', 'so that' before שֶׁ.

Rabbi Shimon son of Elazar says: Do not appease your fellow at the time of his anger, and do not comfort him at the time when his dead lies before him, and do not question him at the time of his vow, and do not strive to see him at the time of his disgrace.

Rabbi Shimon son-of Elazar says: not you-appease [object] your-fellow in-hour-of his-anger, and-not you-comfort-him in-hour that-his-dead cast-down before-him, and-not you-ask to-him in-hour-of his-vow, and-not you-strive to-see-him in-hour-of his-disgrace.

Grammar
  • Four prohibitions, each with the same shape: אַל + verb + the person + בִשְׁעַת (at the time of) + his state.
  • אַל תְּרַצֶּה אֶת חֲבֵרְךָ: the Piel of רצה governs the direct object marked by אֶת.
  • תְּנַחֲמֶנּוּ: Piel imperfect 2nd masculine singular with the 3rd masculine singular suffix joined by an energic nun (-ֶנּוּ), as often on the imperfect.
  • מֵתוֹ מֻטָּל לְפָנָיו: מֻטָּל is the Hofal participle of נטל ('cast, lay'), the nun assimilated into the doubled ט: his dead 'lies before him', the time between death and burial.
  • תִּשְׁאַל לוֹ בִשְׁעַת נִדְרוֹ: 'question him'; the commentators explain it as probing for openings (פְּתָחִים) to release the vow while he is still angry.
  • The repeated בִשְׁעַת / בְּשָׁעָה gives the saying its rhythm: the right time matters as much as the act.

שָׁעָה is 'hour, moment', rendered 'at the time of'. תִּשְׁאַל לוֹ is to question the vower: Bartenura (seek openings to release his vow, for he will answer each that he vowed with it in mind) and Rabbeinu Yonah (do not ask of him the openings) agree. מֻטָּל is the Hofal participle of נטל; the corpus links it to טָלַל, the wrong root. תִּשְׁתַּדֵּל is the Hitpael of שׁדל, 'make an effort, strive'.

Grammar
  • The three sayings share the formula לְמַה הוּא דוֹמֶה ('to what is he like'), which opens a mashal (comparison).
  • The pairs run in parallel: child and old man (ink on new or erased paper); the young and the old (unripe and ripe grapes, wine fresh from the press and aged wine).
  • רַבִּי יוֹסֵי בַר יְהוּדָה: בַר is Aramaic for 'son of'; the Vilna text reads 'son of Rabbi'.
  • Rabbi answers with a different image: אַל תִּסְתַּכֵּל with בְּ takes the jar as the object looked at, and אֶלָּא בְמַה שֶּׁיֶּשׁ בּוֹ gives what to look at instead.
  • The last sentence is elliptical: 'a new jar full of old' means old wine, and 'an old one that has not even new in it' means an old jar with not even new wine.

The Hebrew of the first saying leaves the child as the learner ('learns a child'); the English reads 'learns as a child', as the commentators do. אִישׁ כְּפַר הַבַּבְלִי: 'a man of Kefar HaBavli', a place name; the literal 'village of the Babylonian' is given in the token glosses. דְּיוֹ, ink, is spelled לִדְיוֹ in the base text.

Rabbi Elazar the-Kappar saying the-envy and-the-desire and-the-honor they-take-out [object-marker] the-person from the-world

Grammar
  • The three nouns, each with the article, form the subject; the plural participle מוֹצִיאִין agrees with all three. The ending ־ין is the Mishnaic plural.
  • אֶת הָאָדָם is a definite object ('the person'): a person in general.
  • מִן הָעוֹלָם: 'out of the world'; the Hebrew does not say which world. Rambam explains that these traits destroy a man's faith in the Torah and keep from him the perfections of intellect and character; Rabbeinu Yonah's second explanation is early death (this world).
  • The saying is a short triad with no explanation, and the English leaves it so.

'Honor' stands for the pursuit of honor (Rambam: אהבת הכבוד; Bartenura: that people honor him). 'Desire' is Bartenura's appetite for food, drink and sex; 'envy' is קִנְאָה, as in the commentary. The ambiguity of 'out of the world' is kept.

Grammar
  • The first line is a threefold sequence with לְ + infinitive (or participle): born to die, dead to be revived, living to be judged.
  • לֵידַע לְהוֹדִיעַ וּלְהִוָּדַע is a series of three infinitives (Qal, Hiphil, Niphal) of ידע, and the three cases, Bartenura says, are from others, to others, and of oneself.
  • A string of הוּא clauses names God in seven roles; הוּא before each noun is a pronoun used as a copula ('He is ...').
  • Five times the same phrase עַל כָּרְחֲךָ ('against your will') opens a clause; each time אַתָּה with a passive or stative form follows.
  • מֶלֶךְ מַלְכֵי הַמְּלָכִים ('king of the kings of kings'): the construct chain heaps up the superlative; the Vilna text has the same.
  • וְדַע שֶׁהַכֹּל לְפִי הַחֶשְׁבּוֹן is a bare imperative and a short sentence: the Hebrew does not say who reckons.

The Hebrew leaves open who 'knows', 'makes known' and 'comes to know'; the English keeps the three infinitives bare, and Bartenura's glosses (from others, to others, from oneself) are given in the commentary entries. אֵל is read as 'God', following Bartenura's silence and Rabbeinu Yonah's gloss 'strong, mighty' recorded in the apparatus; the pack lexicon links token 14 to אַל ('not'), which is not the word, and its sense is removed. עָתִיד לָדוּן is 'is destined to judge' (Bartenura: in the world to come), the same word as עָתִיד לִתֵּן below. שֶׁהַכֹּל לְפִי הַחֶשְׁבּוֹן: 'according to the reckoning'; Bartenura gives the sum of small coins, which the English does not add. In the line on the impulse, שְׁאוֹל is Sheol; the pack lemma (Saul) is wrong, and its sense is removed.

Rambam: 'to know, to make known and to be known' refers to those to be born, those born now who will die, and those who will live after death; from these three classes one learns that He is the Former and the Creator. 'The Judge' is His judging now, in life and death; 'destined to judge' is the judgment, with reward and punishment, of those who live in the world to come. 'No taking of bribes' does not mean a bribe to pervert judgment, which is absurd of God, but that He does not let many good deeds buy off one transgression: He punishes the one and rewards the others; 'no partiality' means even the great are punished for a small sin (Moses). 'Against your will' lists only natural events without choice; it does not say 'against your will you sin'.

Apparatus
  • Variant: Vilna reads לידון (4:22.9), הדיין (4:22.22), שוחד (4:22.44), ליתן (4:22.78) for the base spellings.
  • Rambam (normative): מִקַּח שֹׁחַד does not mean a bribe to pervert judgment, but that a man's thousand good deeds do not buy off one transgression; מַשּׂוֹא פָנִים, that the great are punished for a small matter. He cites the Torah's לֹא יִשָּׂא פָנִים וְלֹא יִקַּח שֹׁחַד (Deut. 10:17).
  • Rabbeinu Yonah (not normative): אֵל from the sense 'mighty' (as in 2 Kings 24:15, 'the mighty of the land').
  • Rabbeinu Yonah: 'Former' is the start of the work and 'Creator' its completion.
  • Rabbeinu Yonah on 'let your impulse assure you': do not say that Sheol is a place of refuge, as the heretics say, 'in the world to come there is neither judgment nor judge'.
